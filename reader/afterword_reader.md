# Afterword

Qiji
Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung; Chung Ju-yung and Lee Byung-chull.

Both men have now left this world. Although they were born into different family circumstances, the two giants both became world-renowned entrepreneurs. This was what they shared.

First, both possessed a positive attitude and a passion for doing everything well. Even when everyone around them said something could not be done, they refused to give up.

Chung Ju-yung often asked, “Have you tried it?” Giving up before making an attempt, without ever taking on the challenge, was something he never did.

Chairman Lee Byung-chull was the same. Even when something appeared impossible to others, he approached it through meticulous investigation and research. If he found even the slightest possibility, he was willing to take the chance.

Korea has a saying: “From Gwacheon[^FB-F049] to Seoul, you have to crawl.” It conveys how forbidding Seoul can be. Chairman Lee came to Seoul alone and carved out a world of his own.

Second, they were skilled at employing people, bringing out their initiative, and managing personnel with exceptional ability.

Both Lee and Chung valued the recruitment of talented people and joined them in opening paths to world markets.

Third, even in situations that appeared impossible, they could devise good ideas and thereby achieve their goals.

Chung encountered difficulties while constructing the Jubail port project, just as Lee did while developing the semiconductor industry. Even in unfavorable circumstances, they found solutions and ultimately overcame the difficulties. They never believed in so-called luck. When the situation was adverse, they made every possible effort to solve the problem. Samsung's success in semiconductors is a fine example.

Fourth, both men experienced the setbacks of failure, but neither allowed himself to sink into despondency.

Chairman Chung accumulated enormous debts while building the Goryeong Bridge project and spent nearly twenty years merely repaying them. Yet the experience did not crush him. The same was true when the Narathiwat Highway in Thailand incurred a major deficit. After carefully accumulating experience, he ultimately reaped great rewards from it during the Vietnam War. Perhaps few entrepreneurs experienced as many failures as Chung did. No sooner had his automobile repair shop been built than a fire reduced it to ashes, together with the cars awaiting repair. Although his life was filled with hardship, he never bowed before any difficulty. At his most desperate financially, he even borrowed at usurious interest, yet he always believed tomorrow would be better.

Chairman Lee was ordered to surrender Korea Fertilizer to the state because of a smuggling incident. Later came the May 16 illicit-profiteering affair, during which the government for a time declared that it would confiscate all his property. In 1980, the government nationalized his Tongyang Broadcasting Company. During the Korean War, he lost nearly all his assets. Under Japanese colonial rule, failure in real estate returned him to his starting point. He experienced many failures, but he was also a genius at overcoming difficulty. If he was stronger than an ordinary person, it was because he never dwelled excessively on failure. Instead, he drew lessons from it and did better.

In the same circumstances, an ordinary person might drink himself into a stupor and become ruined for life. These men did not. They stood up again. They failed not merely once or twice, yet every time, they pulled themselves together and rose again like a weighted tumbler.

Fifth, both Chung and Lee were skilled at responding to changes in their era and keeping pace with the times.

Chung successively entered construction, shipbuilding, automobiles, and semiconductors, while Lee took on challenges in sugar, textiles, home appliances, semiconductors, and numerous other fields. Their paths demonstrate their exceptional ability to adapt to changing times. In this respect, both were highly flexible entrepreneurs, not stubborn men trapped in old ways.

Korea's economy is now in difficulty, but if we learn well from the enterprising spirit and courage of these two giants, anyone can rise again. This is what we should truly learn from them.

The persistent conviction that “I can do it” and the power to rally an immediate response from others—these are what we should learn from the lives of Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung.

Korea's national income now stands at only ten thousand dollars. In the future, we must raise that figure to about thirty thousand. Korea and its people cannot remain despondent amid today's temporary difficulties. We must tighten our belts and advance into international markets once more. Here, it is worth recalling Chung's words again.

“There will be trials, but I will never fail!”

Yes! We are being tested now; we have not failed. Before the test becomes failure, we must rise again and launch a new advance into world markets!

[^FB-F049]: A small city south of Seoul.
