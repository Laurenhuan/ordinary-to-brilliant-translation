# Fine Fabric and the Goryeong Bridge Project

To complete the project, Chung Ju-yung took on debts greater than the value of the contract. He did so because he honored his commitments. If he lost his credibility, then everything truly would be over.

“This is a trial, not a failure. If I do not regard it as failure, then it can never be failure.”

This was how Chung encouraged himself when the Goryeong Bridge project failed.

Those who readily admit defeat lose everything. But those who grasp the root of a setback, persevere to the end, and wait for an opportunity to rise again will ultimately have hope.

In 1954, with Cheil Sugar firmly established, Lee Byung-chull entered the wool-textile industry.

The wool-textile industry began in Britain and developed rapidly in the United States and Japan.

After Korea's liberation, many gentlemen in Seoul wore suits made from British fabric smuggled through Macao. It was the era of the “Macao gentleman.”

A suit made from British fabric, however, cost the equivalent of three months' salary for a company employee—about 60,000 won.

Lee also wore suits made from pure wool obtained through Macao, including on business trips to Japan. Because he was always dressed in a fine pure-wool suit, waitresses in coffee shops called him “Mr. Pure Wool.” Lee was thus among the first Koreans to wear pure-wool suits.

He observed that textiles sold extremely well in the United States and Japan and felt that the industry would have equally strong prospects if introduced in Korea. He considered entering the field, but textiles required enormous capital and advanced technology.

Britain's world-renowned textile industry had a history of more than four hundred years, during which it had undergone continuous technological innovation.

Korea at the time could do little more than make cotton undershirts. How could it possibly establish a wool-textile industry?

Yet the examples of the United States and Japan made one thing clear: in the near future, Korea would develop market demand like theirs.

Lee calculated that textiles manufactured domestically could be priced at only 20 percent of the cost of imported goods.

At 20 percent of the price of imported woolens and with comparable quality, he believed the products would have excellent prospects once they reached the market.

The problem was that producing goods of such quality required a large, world-class factory. Lee consulted widely to learn about textile mills.

A director of an American company mocked him: “If Korea can produce a decent product under its own power within three years, I will fly through the sky for everyone to see.”

Lee did not retreat. He investigated textile-production technology and the requirements for building a factory in depth.

He ultimately decided to import only manufacturing technology from foreign companies and rely on Korean capabilities for all installation and operations.

The problem was money. Building the factory, purchasing equipment, and recruiting technicians all required astronomical sums.

Lee knew that the government was also deeply interested in establishing a domestic textile mill. With the help of Kang Seong-tae, then minister of commerce and industry, he obtained a one-million-dollar loan from the U.S. Foreign Operations Administration (FOA). Adding 100 million won of his own and 58.3 million won in financing from Korea Development Bank, he finally established Cheil Wool Textile.

The date was September 15, 1954.

A year and a half later, a complete production line was imported from the West German company SPINBAO. The factory was built with only Korean technicians and six technical workers from West Germany.

An incident during the construction of the Cheil Wool Textile plant is worth noting. Even while activity at the construction site was at its height, Lee devoted considerable effort to landscaping the courtyard in front of the female employees' dormitory.

One day, general manager Lee Chang-eop said to him, “Before the factory itself is finished, you are building employee dormitories and devoting so much effort to beautifying the grounds in front of them. What will people say? Installing heating, laying floors in the corridors, and carefully landscaping the courtyard all seem excessively expensive.”

Lee Chang-eop meant that money was being wasted on something of no practical use.

“It is far from enough merely to provide female employees with a place to work. They are all cherished daughters of their parents, and we must consider their health. Dormitory life should improve their cultivation and give them enjoyment. Factory life is monotonous; without care, they may grow weary of their work and even suffer harm to their health. We must therefore make every effort to create a good dormitory environment.”

“Even so, some of the expense is still wasteful,” Lee Chang-eop replied.

“In a poor working environment, people easily lose interest in their jobs and become weary. Efficiency falls, and many leave their positions. Ultimately, the company suffers. Anyone will feel happier and become dedicated to their work only in a pleasant environment.”

“But aren't we still spending a great deal of money needlessly?” Lee Chang-eop persisted.

“We are spending money, but in the long run it will benefit the company. If we can improve the female employees' efficiency, production costs will ultimately fall.”

Lee Byung-chull held firmly to his views. Regardless of the expense, he insisted on building the most modern employee dormitories and making their courtyards pleasant. The first buildings completed in the factory project were three elegant dormitories named “Sincere Heart,” “Kind Heart,” and “Graceful Heart.”

At the time, Cheil Wool Textile offered not only excellent dormitory conditions but also first-rate supporting facilities.

The factory provided employees with a laundry, beauty salon, bathhouse, ironing room, and reading room. When employees' families visited, a dedicated photographer even took commemorative photographs for them. Lee paid such close attention to these details because he believed that employees who produced high-value wool textiles deserved the finest treatment.

Because the accommodations were good and the benefits were considered the best in Korea, crowds of applicants appeared as soon as recruitment notices were placed in newspapers.

Having heard about all this, President Syngman Rhee attended the factory's completion ceremony and personally inscribed the words “Clothe the People.”[^TN-CH08-001]

With every preparation complete, full production began in 1956.

In its first year, Cheil Wool Textile produced enough suit fabric for roughly 500,000 suits. A suit made with Golden Wool fabric cost about 10,000 won, compared with around 60,000 won for fabric from Macao. It was extremely inexpensive, yet its quality was every bit as good.

Korean gentlemen at the time nevertheless continued to regard clothing from Macao as supreme. They remained con-

vinced that domestically produced suit fabric could never compare with imports.

Sales were poor, and Lee began looking for a solution.

To show that domestically produced fine suit fabric was superior to foreign goods, he ran an advertisement declaring, “Golden Wool suit fabric that surpasses imports.”

Lee also deliberately wore suits made from fabric produced at his own woolen mill.

Many government officials and businesspeople he met nevertheless assumed that he was wearing a British pure-wool suit.

“President Lee certainly knows how to choose a suit. That is no ordinary fabric.”

He often heard comments of this kind.

“Thank you. I take that to mean you are praising Golden Wool for matching British quality.”

Smiling, Lee would turn back his cuff to reveal the conspicuous words “Made in Korea,” proving that the fabric came from Cheil Wool Textile.

Yet in a society that favored foreign goods above all else, Golden Wool recorded a loss of 500 million won in its first year.

Korea at the time produced almost no domestic goods, and those that did exist were generally of very poor quality. Until the mid-1960s, people therefore relied mainly on American or Japanese products. President Park Chung Hee ordered schoolchildren to begin chanting “Use Korean goods” from elementary school onward, and the same slogan was often posted on roadside walls. Against this social background, domestically produced fabric inevitably faced difficult sales conditions.

Lee worked vigorously both to improve product quality and to promote it. In January 1958, the government prohibited imports of fine wool yarn.

When determining which items to restrict, officials found that six million pounds of fine wool yarn were being imported each year, causing a major outflow of foreign currency. Moreover, the yarn produced by Cheil Wool Textile was fully comparable in quality to imports.

This policy was, from another angle, an acknowledgment that Cheil Wool Textile's quality matched imported goods. It was also a protective measure intended to help the company's products meet future domestic demand. Such support had been won through persistent effort.

Although sales were disappointing at first, word gradually spread that Cheil Wool Textile's products were as good as imports, and the number of buyers steadily grew.

Sales became so strong that counterfeit products appeared on the market.

As Cheil Wool Textile prospered, new wool-textile companies such as Kyungnam Wool Textile, Korea Wool Textile, and Daehan Wool Textile sprang up one after another.

By then, however, Cheil Wool Textile already held 70 percent of the market.

To achieve complete self-sufficiency, the government banned imports of foreign wool textiles.

Korea's textile industry, which had begun with Cheil Wool Textile, developed rapidly. Its output rose thirteenfold, from 14.9 billion won in 1960 to 197.5 billion won in 1970. The industry's 247,191 workers accounted for 24 percent of all manufacturing employment.

Thirty years later, in 1991, total production reached 14.5355 trillion won—one thousand times the initial level.

Through the success of Cheil Sugar and Cheil Wool Textile, Lee Byung-chull came to account for 4 percent of the nation's total tax revenue and became Korea's richest man.

Cheil Wool Textile ultimately drove foreign fabrics from the Korean market and brought new vitality to people's lives. It saved Korea 2.5 million dollars in foreign currency each year.

While Lee Byung-chull's business was advancing from success to success, Chung Ju-yung was engaged in a bitter struggle.

After suffering enormous losses on the Goryeong Bridge project, a government minting bureau contract begun in April 1954, Hyundai Group faced a crisis of survival.

Goryeong Bridge connected Daegu and Geochang.[^TN-CH08-002] After the war, only its piers remained; everything else had sunk beneath the water. The government urgently needed the bridge rebuilt for military purposes.

It was the largest government project of the time. The total contract was worth 545.7 billion won[^TN-CH08-003] and had to be completed within twenty-four months. Chung lost more than 65 million won on the project.

Cheil Sugar's operating capital the previous year had been 20 million won, which meant

that Chung's loss amounted to more than three times that figure.

Because it was Korea's largest civil-engineering project at the time, Chung had approached it with great expectations. It was nominally a restoration project, but in practice it was more difficult than building a new bridge. In winter, the water receded and exposed white sand; in summer, the Nakdong River suddenly became deep.

Chung's equipment consisted of only one crane, one mixer, and one compressor. The machinery was primitive, and most of the work had to be done by hand.

The workers built one reinforced-concrete pier and then another in the same fashion. Unexpectedly, torrential rain arrived, the river rose sharply, and the piers were swept away.

In the end, Chung and Hyundai Construction failed to erect even a single pier, while prices rose 120-fold amid staggering inflation. Funds became desperately scarce.

A contract had already been signed, and because the work was not finished on time, no payment could be collected. Meanwhile, the workers demanded their wages.

Chung sold his younger brother Chung Soon-yung's twenty-square-meter property in Samseon-dong and his brother-in-law's twenty-square-meter property in Donam-dong to finance the project. Even this was insufficient, so he sold his own automobile repair shop and borrowed money at monthly interest of 18 percent to pay the workers. In all, he raised 100 million won.

Goryeong Bridge was finally completed two months later than the original agreement required.

Chung received the full contract payment, yet was left with even greater debts. The project was finished, but he was so exhausted that he no longer had the strength even to clear the site.

Creditors pursued him relentlessly, while others in the industry looked upon him with contempt.

People said that Chung Ju-yung, who had not even graduated from elementary school, knew nothing about inflation and was now finished.

To complete the project, Chung took on debts greater than the value of the contract. He did so because he honored his commitments. If he lost his credibility, then everything truly would be over.

“This is a trial, not a failure. If I do not regard it as failure, then it can never be failure.”

This was how Chung encouraged himself after the failure of the Goryeong Bridge project.

Those who readily admit defeat lose everything. But those who grasp the root of a setback, persevere to the end, and wait for an opportunity to rise again will ultimately have hope.

Chung was fond of a line from the *Caigentan* (*Vegetable Roots Discourse*): “At the moment of success, the sorrow of failure is already being born.” It means that the seeds of failure are planted even while a person is enjoying success. The force that encouraged him never to lose heart, even in failure, was “credibility.”

The following maxim hung prominently inside Hyundai Construction:

“Remain calm and composed; this will make you strong, rational, and wise.”

This was the lesson Chung wrote down after the failure of the Goryeong Bridge project.

Chung began a long struggle of patient endurance. It took him twenty years to repay the 65 million won in debt incurred by the bridge project.

Although Hyundai Construction suffered enormous losses on Goryeong Bridge, the reputation for keeping its word that it established there gave Chung the opportunity two years later, in 1957, to restore the Han River approach bridge—and achieve a brilliant success.
