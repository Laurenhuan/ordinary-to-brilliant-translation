# After the Age of the Giants

Chung Ju-yung's “I can do it!” spirit of challenge and Lee Byung-chull's meticulous, detailed analysis and judgment are precious assets bequeathed to our economic history.

Although the age of the two giants has ended, further study and development of the spiritual legacy they left behind will provide a cornerstone for future progress.

The two giants, Chung Ju-yung and Lee Byung-chull, were leading forces in the development of Korea's national economy in their time. Like many figures of our parents' generation, they possessed immense personal charisma and the ability to rally others. No one can deny that they played foundational roles in Korea's economic takeoff after the 1960s.

Compared with Japan and the United States, Korea was once among the countries that placed relatively heavy pressure on businesspeople. As Korea gradually joined the ranks of developed countries, those engaged in economic activity received increasing respect. During the difficult 1950s and 1960s, business experts such as Lee and Chung rushed tirelessly among construction sites, while the Korean people displayed craftsmanship, humility, and honesty equal to those of any other nation. Through their efforts, Korea leaped from a backward, impoverished country to become the world's eleventh-largest economic power. Korea's per capita national income now approaches ten thousand dollars, although it remains below its level before the Southeast Asian financial crisis.

For Korea to maintain strong development momentum in the twenty-first century, today's businesspeople must demonstrate their abilities on the world stage, while the country's policies and institutions align as closely as possible with international standards. Yet Korea is still judged an unsuitable place for enterprise development. The government should therefore act decisively to improve the investment environment and abolish restrictive provisions.

The overall caliber of the Korean people is outstanding by global standards. They have erected high-rise buildings in deserts hotter than sixty degrees Celsius and laid natural-gas pipelines in Siberia at temperatures below minus forty. From sixty above zero to forty below, our people endured a full one-hundred-degree temperature range. It may be difficult to find another people anywhere in the world who have done the same. There are, of course, the Japanese, whose appearance and physique resemble ours. Yet even among the peoples of Asia, it would be hard to find people as adaptable to their environment as Koreans, or as capable of working through sheer force of will.

Chung Ju-yung's “I can do it!” spirit of challenge and Lee Byung-chull's meticulous, detailed analysis and judgment are precious assets bequeathed to our economic history.

Although the age of the two giants has ended, further study and development of the spiritual legacy they left behind will provide a cornerstone for future progress.
