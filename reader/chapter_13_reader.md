# Vietnam War Projects and Korea Fertilizer Company

If you are always chasing after others and merely playing the role of a troubleshooter, you will forever lose in competition. Whatever the undertaking,

I hope you will not always think of doing it alone. Cultivate unity among the people in the company, and bring everyone's intelligence, wisdom, and moral standing into play.

—Lee Byung-chull

“What a beautiful morning—let us move forward.” The song came over the radio.

In the mid-1960s, the sky over Seoul was a clear blue. Environmental pollution did not yet exist.

The fountain in front of Seoul City Hall sent water spraying into the air, and a banner reading “Beautiful Seoul, Population 3.5 Million” hung before the main hall.

Across the intersection, in Deoksugung Palace, stood a “Domestic Products Exhibition Hall.” It displayed and sold sneakers, clothing, and even salt made by Korea's emerging enterprises. On occasion, it exhibited whales caught off Pohang in North Gyeongsang Province.

In March, as construction began on the Export Industrial Complex[^FB-F022] in Guro-dong,[^FB-F021] Koreans began to feel confident that they could succeed if only they set to work.

In 1965, Korea dispatched two thousand noncombat troops of the Dove Engineering Corps to Vietnam, followed later by the Tiger Division.

Students flocked to Busan Harbor holding the Taegeukgi and sang to the soldiers departing for Vietnam: “Onward to Vietnam, unafraid of mountains high or waters far...”

In January 1966, Chung Ju-yung also became involved in the Vietnam War. While Korea provided military assistance by dispatching troops, it also gained the right to participate in construction work in Vietnam.

Korean construction companies mainly took part in Vietnam's reconstruction, particularly civil engineering and building projects. Hyundai Construction chiefly undertook dredging, while Daelim Industrial handled harbor work. Many Korean laborers were also hired by American companies and sent to work in Vietnam. In addition to construction and civil engineering, Koreans participated in loading, unloading, and overland transport.

Although Chung had suffered a huge loss on Thailand's Narathiwat Highway, the experience he gained there gave him complete confidence that he could succeed in Vietnam.

Hyundai's first contract was to dredge the US military base at Cam Ranh Bay. Facing the South China Sea, Cam Ranh Bay is an excellent natural harbor and was a strategic supply point for the US military.

The United States needed to unload enormous quantities of supplies at Cam Ranh Bay to win the Vietnam War, but the harbor's unloading facilities were severely inadequate. The US military needed to dredge the bay on a large scale and build it into a larger military base so that freighters of more than ten thousand tons could enter and leave freely.

At the time, twenty-one dredgers from the United States, Australia, Singapore, Japan, France, and other countries were working on the development of Cam Ranh Bay.

Hyundai's vessel was assigned to work in a group with ships from Australia and two other countries, because a project of such magnitude was beyond the capacity of any one country's company.

Working around the clock with a 2,550-horsepower dredger, Hyundai brought the already delayed project to completion ahead of schedule. Its capabilities immediately drew the US military's attention. Hyundai next received a housing-construction contract in one city. While that project was underway, the US military also entrusted Hyundai with dredging in the Mekong Delta.

The Mekong Delta was Vietnam's great granary. One-third of Vietnam's territory was mountainous, and the delta was its broadest agricultural region. It was also an area the US military had to secure for strategic reasons. Because of the war, however, the delta had become overgrown with weeds.

At the time, the US military's 8,000-horsepower dredger *Jamaica* churned up sand and soil in the Mekong Delta and spread it over the weeds. In other words, it first cleared the land and then constructed a navigable channel through the Mekong.

The area was also a granary for the Viet Cong, who had long sought to retake the Mekong Delta from the US military. One day, they shelled the *Jamaica*, sinking it and dealing the Americans a severe blow.

General Westmoreland, commander of US forces in Vietnam, ordered a rear admiral to resume work immediately. Because Hyundai had demonstrated exceptional ability on the Cam Ranh Bay project, the US military strongly recommended that it continue the work.

Even Chung Ju-yung, who never backed down, hesitated this time. Viet Cong firepower had been able to sink the *Jamaica*, and Hyundai's workers wondered whether they would return alive

from such a mission. Under threat from armed US troops, Hyundai had no choice but to accept the Mekong project, for this was a granary that could not be allowed to fall into Viet Cong hands under any circumstances. In the end, the *Hyundai No. 1* dredger headed for the Mekong Delta.

As expected, US and Viet Cong forces were engaged in fierce fighting in the Mekong Delta. Viet Cong troops hid in the weeds and attacked around the clock; at night, flares rose without pause as the battle continued day and night. Amid this maelstrom of danger, Hyundai's workers turned the soil by day to cover the weeds. Equipment was severely lacking, so Chung added the 4,000-horsepower dredger *Hyundai No. 2*.

Work continued. By the time the Lunar New Year arrived, Hyundai employees who had originally been working in Vinh Long,[^FB-F023] three hundred kilometers from the Mekong, found themselves caught in the Viet Cong's Tet Offensive.

Viet Cong and South Vietnamese forces fired at one another from opposite banks of the river, filling the air with bullets. Hyundai's workers on the delta between them were a step away from death. The US military dropped food by helicopter. Still, the work continued.

After fifteen long days, the Tet Offensive ended, and Hyundai's workers were able to withdraw from the Mekong Delta.

Hyundai earned enormous profits from the Vietnam War and once again accumulated experience working overseas. That experience was applied to the Kyungbu Expressway project the following year.

Let us return to the bitter, bone-chilling cold of the late 1950s. After the great success of Cheil Sugar and Cheil Wool Textile, Lee Byung-chull conceived a fertilizer plant that would be the largest in the world, or at least in East Asia. It was a dream he had long cherished. He had begun considering it late in President Syngman Rhee's administration, but because it was an enormous project, it required government support and approval.

As noted earlier, it was in Tokyo in 1959 that Lee formally resolved to begin planning the fertilizer plant—what became known as the Tokyo Initiative.

Watching a New Year's interview program on television at Tokyo's Imperial Hotel, Lee learned that ten developed countries—including the United States, Britain, West Germany, France, Italy, Canada, and Australia—intended to provide long-term, low-interest loans to developing countries with growth potential. It was exhilarating news.

Lee had long wanted to establish a world-class fertilizer plant but lacked the funds. After making detailed inquiries about the program, he learned that it offered not aid money but private commercial loans: a sound plan could qualify for long-term, low-interest financing. For Lee, who had been struggling with the funding question, the information was a timely lifeline.

At the time, Korea received 250 million dollars in aid each year, of which approximately 100 million dollars was used to import fertilizer. In other words, fertilizer accounted for the largest share of the goods imported with aid funds.

The government knew this well. It began building the Chungju Fertilizer Plant in 1955 and the Naju Fertilizer Plant in 1958. Yet even with both factories operating at full capacity, their combined annual output was only sixty thousand tons.

Korea's fertilizer demand was three hundred thousand tons in 1960 and four hundred thousand tons in 1966.

In short, Korea would need six more fertilizer plants like those in Chungju and Naju to meet domestic demand.

Lee was determined to establish a fertilizer plant capable of competing in export markets. To achieve this, he believed he would have to import large quantities of the most advanced equipment. He planned a plant with an annual capacity of 350,000 tons, producing urea and compound fertilizers suited to Korea rather than ammonia fertilizer.

As soon as he returned to Korea, Lee visited President Syngman Rhee, who knew him personally. Lee's father, Lee Chan-woo, and Rhee had participated in a Christian youth movement together when they were young.

Lee had come to present his plan to build a fertilizer plant and request government approval.

President Rhee asked how much money would be needed.

Lee replied, “Approximately forty to fifty million dollars.”

“What? Forty to fifty million?” President Rhee exclaimed, his face

showing his astonishment.

At the time, Rhee was so frugal that he personally scrutinized expenditures of even a single dollar. It was no wonder that hearing a figure of forty to fifty million dollars came as a shock.

Lee explained that he planned to raise the money through loans from Europe. Only then did President Rhee look satisfied, and he immediately promised vigorous support. With the president's approval secured, Lee moved actively to attract financing.

On February 1, 1960, President Syngman Rhee embarked on a visit to Europe.

Through Hertz, the West German ambassador to Korea, Lee arranged to meet West German economics minister Ludwig Erhard. At the time, West Germany was an economic power with 8 billion dollars in foreign-exchange reserves.

Introduced by West German officials, Lee met a vice president of Krupp. Founded in 1801, Krupp was a steel company and one of the great industrial conglomerates of its day. During the First Sino-Japanese War in 1894, the Qing dynasty's main warships—the 7,335-ton *Dingyuan*, then the largest warship in East Asia, and the *Zhiyuan*—had both been manufactured by Krupp.

Lee came straight to the point: he wanted to build a fertilizer plant with an annual output of three hundred thousand tons and sought a loan. To his surprise, the Krupp vice president agreed on the spot, having already received the details from Minister Erhard. West German policy at the time was to devote approximately 300 million dollars of its 8 billion dollars in foreign-exchange reserves to aid for developing countries, and loans had already been extended to India. The vice president promised that the loan would be provided as soon as a bank guarantee was issued.

Lee sent out the project proposal and proceeded to his next destination, Italy, where he intended to visit a fertilizer company in the Montecatini group. Montecatini was a major corporation with more than one hundred chemical plants, fertilizer works, power stations, mines, and other enterprises across Europe. Its manager, too, was very friendly.

In 1930, Montecatini's fertilizer company was the first in the world to develop a process for producing nitrogen and ammonia, and the first plant built using that process was the Hungnam[^FB-F024] Nitrogen Fertilizer Plant. The company did not share its proprietary technology with anyone, so the fact that the Hungnam plant in Korea obtained it demonstrated the sincerity of the cooperation.

Cooperation with West Germany and Italy proceeded unexpectedly smoothly, and the loan formalities were simple and trouble-free. Lee spent several days sightseeing in Italy. It was then that he received shocking news: the April 19 Revolution had broken out in Korea, and President Syngman Rhee had stepped down. When Lee returned home, it felt as though the world had changed.

The fertilizer-plant loan, which had been proceeding so smoothly, ran aground over the issue of so-called illicit profiteering after the April 19 Revolution. Public opinion strongly demanded that illicit profiteers be punished. Samsung employees and Lee himself were investigated for illicit profiteering, and Lee was summoned by a minister for questioning. Tax evasion was the focus of the investigation. Lee said that he had evaded taxes, while his employees each claimed responsibility themselves, both sides trying to take the blame for the other.

The investigation continued for several months. In the end, Samsung's six companies were ordered to pay a total of 5 billion won in back taxes.

Lee handed the fertilizer-plant plans he had labored over to Kim Young-sun, the company's finance director. A few days later, he flew to Japan with a heavy heart, beginning more than a year of life abroad.

The fertilizer-plant issue resurfaced after the May 16 military coup. Seeking to revitalize the national economy and raise incomes by building factories, the military government encouraged the attraction of foreign capital. As noted earlier, Lee played a crucial role in that effort.

Once foreign capital began to arrive, each entrepreneur chose the field he wanted to pursue. Lee Byung-chull, Chung Jae-ha of Samhwa Textile, and Kim Ji-tae of Chosun Light Textiles chose fertilizer; Namgung Ryeon chose light oil; Lee Yang-gu of the Tongyang Group and Seol Gyeong-dong of Daehan Industrial chose ironmaking; Koo In-hoe chose electric cables; and Kim Sung-kon of GoldStar Textile and Lee Han-won of Daehan Flour Mills chose electric power.

In May 1962, when General James Van Fleet led a large delegation of twenty-eight American businesspeople, including representatives of Westinghouse Electric, to Korea, a preliminary agreement on investment in steelmaking, fertilizer, and other industries was signed at Prime Minister Song Yo-chan's office. It was an agreement between the government and the business community.

Under this agreement, Lee Byung-chull, Chung Jae-ha, and Kim Ji-tae formed the Ulsan Fertilizer

Investment Consortium and returned to Japan and Europe to seek investment.

As a result, Kobe Steel, Fuji Electric, and Mitsubishi Shipbuilding of Japan, Weecho of the United States, Krupp of West Germany, and other companies submitted investment proposals. Kobe Steel's terms were the most favorable.

Lee's enthusiasm soared. He planned to attract 55 million dollars in foreign capital, add 5 billion won in domestic financing, and build a large fertilizer plant with an annual capacity of three hundred thousand tons.

Yet the Korean government, whose annual exports amounted to only 100 million dollars, ultimately abandoned the astronomically expensive fertilizer plant. Everything returned to where it had begun, and Lee suffered another emotional blow.

Another opportunity arose later. In October 1963, Park Chung Hee was elected president and pressed for construction of the fertilizer plant to resume. But Lee did not accept rashly; he had already learned from two failures.

President Park sent Deputy Prime Minister Chang Key-young to persuade Lee. Chang approached him with great enthusiasm, but Lee did not commit himself immediately, having grown disillusioned with the government's constant policy reversals.

Chang began telephoning Lee and later visited his home several times.

“I will back you on the administrative side. There will be no problem, so please begin building the plant as soon as possible.”

In the end, the government actively supported administrative approvals, foreign financing, and every other aspect of the project, and construction of the fertilizer plant was placed on the agenda. Lee had decided to build it.

Lee traveled to Japan to raise funds, carrying an official letter stating that “the Korean government has entrusted Samsung with the construction of a large-scale fertilizer plant.” He met with the president of Mitsui & Co., with whom he had long maintained close relations, President Inayama of Yawata Steel, and President Kaijima of Kobe Steel to discuss the project.

President Inayama of Yawata Steel was especially enthusiastic.

“Let us give it everything we have and work together.”

Word of these contacts spread rapidly, causing considerable alarm in Japan's fertilizer industry. Korea had always been an important market for Japanese fertilizer; no one had imagined that it might one day become a competitor.

At the time, Japan's largest fertilizer plant produced 180,000 tons a year; most others produced fifty to sixty thousand tons, or at most one hundred thousand. News that Lee intended to build a plant with an annual capacity of three hundred thousand tons placed immense pressure on them.

A representative of Japan's fertilizer industry came to see Lee.

He proposed, “Do not build the fertilizer plant. From now on, we will supply fertilizer to Korea for the long term at the lowest possible price.” Lee, of course, refused.

People in Japan's fertilizer industry believed that if Lee completed a plant capable of producing three hundred thousand tons a year, they would be unable to compete with it. Fortunately, the Japanese business community remained very friendly.

With the help of President Inayama of Yawata Steel, an agreement was finally signed under which Mitsui & Co.'s fertilizer division would provide the plant equipment and financing. Annual fertilizer output was set at thirty-three tons, with 42 million dollars in foreign capital to be repaid over eight years at annual interest of 5.5 percent. An average annual output of 330,000 tons would make it the largest facility of its kind in the world.[^TN-CH13-001]

At the time, fertilizer in Korea cost approximately 86 to 90 dollars per ton, while Lee's Korea Fertilizer could sell it for 56 dollars per ton. Once completed, the plant would therefore both create jobs and provide inexpensive fertilizer. After many twists and turns, construction of the Korea Fertilizer plant in Ulsan finally began. Yet the difficulties were not over.

On September 16, 1966, when the Korea Fertilizer plant was 80 percent complete, the so-called Korea Fertilizer smuggling incident erupted. It began when a chemical called OTSA, stored in a bonded warehouse, was sold on the market without government permission.

OTSA was a special chemical developed by the Italian company Montecatini and was required for a carbon-dioxide absorption and regeneration unit, one stage in an ammonia-fertilizer plant. Put simply, it was a raw material for saccharin. At the time, saccharin was used in the food industry to add sweetness.

Sugar was expensive then, so saccharin was widely used as a sweetener. It was added to bread and fruit juice alike, and was regarded as a daily necessity.

Someone sold six tons of the saccharin stored in the bonded warehouse for fifty thousand dollars, and it entered the market. Newspapers traced its origin, and rumors spread that Samsung's eldest daughter had smuggled it from abroad. The papers ran prominent

stories accusing Korea's largest conglomerate of smuggling saccharin. An investigation began, and the origin of the saccharin eventually came to light. It was connected to the 42-million-dollar loan provided by Japan's Mitsui & Co. Not all of that loan had been supplied in cash; part had taken the form of machinery needed for the plant. In the process, Mitsui offered a one-million-dollar rebate as thanks for the purchase of its machinery. It was an unexpected windfall, but the problem was how to bring the money into Korea through normal channels. Lee wrestled with the issue.

According to the autobiography of Lee's eldest son, Lee Maeng-hee, Lee informed President Park Chung Hee about the money. President Park allegedly instructed that, because the money could not enter Korea in cash, it should be used to purchase one million dollars' worth of goods. After those goods were sold, one-third of the proceeds would go to political funds, one-third to construction funds, and one-third to the operating funds of Korea Fertilizer.

Following Park's suggestion, the one million dollars was used to import OTSA, refrigerators, air conditioners, telephones, stainless-steel sheets, and other goods in secret. If Lee Maeng-hee's account is factual, then President Park himself was involved in the smuggling. The incident remains unresolved to this day, and Lee ultimately returned Korea Fertilizer Company to the state because of it.

The completion ceremony was held, and the Japanese friends who had helped the project hosted a celebratory banquet at Tokyo's Imperial Hotel. Yet Korea Fertilizer Company, into which Lee had poured ten years of effort, vanished from his hands. It was his greatest misfortune. After the affair ended, Lee once again withdrew into travel and seclusion.

Korea Fertilizer Company rejoined Samsung in 1994. Under the Korean government's privatization policy for the company, an open tender was held with an opening price of 130 billion won. Samsung won with a bid 100 billion won above that price—a total of 230 billion won—and thus regained control of Korea Fertilizer after twenty-seven years.
