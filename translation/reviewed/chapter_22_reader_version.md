# Making Something of Play

Once, when Chung Ju-yung attended a Children's Day event, a child asked him, “Grandpa, how did you make so much money?”

Chung answered with the example of climbing a mountain: if you stare at the summit as you climb, reaching it will certainly seem extremely difficult. But if you follow the footprints of those who came before you and climb one step at a time, you can make it to the top.

In the summer of 1991, Chairman Chung Ju-yung attended the Summer Seaside School for Poets organized by the poetry magazine *Simsang*. The seaside school brought writers and readers together in summer to enjoy the beach during the day and hold poetry readings and discussions in the evening. Chung always took an active part in events of this kind, as well as in various Children's Day[^FB-F040] activities.

Although Chung may have seemed to us like a man of physical action, he was in fact a meticulous thinker with the romantic temperament of an artist. One important trigger for his departure from home as a boy was *The Dong-A Ilbo*, which he delivered every day to the district head's house. The newspaper brought him information about the outside world, and it was also serializing Yi Kwang-su's novel *The Heartless*, which Chung loved.

As a passionate teenager, Chung yearned to enter the wider world and pursue his dreams. For this reason, he ran away from home four times.

In his youth, Chung read many biographies of great figures, including a life of Napoleon, and drew considerable inspiration from them. In his later years, he interacted extensively with Korean artists.

He met television writer Kim Soo-hyun, novelists Park Kyongni and Lee Mun-yol, comedian Lee Ju-il, and others to discuss literature and art. His reasoning was that “anyone recognized by the public in a particular field must have something distinctive.” He associated with these prominent people in order to learn from them.

In 1993, Chung went to visit the novelist Park Kyongni at her home. That day, Park was holding a commemorative event to celebrate the completion of her epic novel *Land*. Chung had been traveling abroad in connection with the World Cup, and as soon as he returned to Korea, he went straight from the airport

to Park's home in Wonju.[^FB-F041]

It was Park and Chung's second meeting. At their first, Park had been struck by how Chung's face seemed at once childlike and elderly. At their second meeting, he was extremely humble and sensitive, yet also gave an impression of keen intelligence.

Why would Chung, despite being so busy, make time to meet a novelist who appeared unrelated to his business and attend an event celebrating the completion of her book? The explanation is that he went there to acquire knowledge and improve himself.

When Daewoo Group's Kim Woo-choong traveled abroad on business, he too often invited Korean intellectuals and men of great learning to accompany him, engaging them in marathon conversations on the plane. A representative example was his travel with the philosopher Kim Yong-ok[^FB-F042] and the novelist Choi In-hun. Kim spoke with them at length throughout flights lasting more than ten hours. An in-depth conversation with an expert in a particular field could provide not only knowledge but also insight into changes and trends in the world.

Chung shared this view. No one pursued learning more actively than he did. On one occasion, he and Kwon Ki-tae, a vice president of Hyundai Engineering & Construction, visited a Norwegian port city on a construction matter. When they went to a bar for a drink that evening, Norwegians were dancing the waltz. Chung did not know how to dance it, so he watched from the side all evening.

Not long afterward, Chung and Kwon traveled to Guam on business. When Kwon awoke in the morning and drew back the curtains, he saw someone dancing on the beach outside. It was Chung, practicing alone the waltz steps he had learned in Norway. At that moment, he looked like a thirteen-year-old boy. The elderly chairman, as curious as a child, practiced his dance steps on the sand. This was another side of Chung Ju-yung.

Why did Chung associate so closely with writers and artists? Through his contact with poets, novelists, and other literary figures and artists, he sought to expand the horizons of his imagination. Poets and artists possess freer imaginations than anyone else. Chung hoped to learn that imaginative power from them and put it into practice. The extraordinary act of driving five hundred cattle on a visit

to North Korea revealed the richness of his sensitivity and imagination. A French cultural critic praised the event extravagantly, calling it the greatest feat of the twenty-first century.

During the Seosan seawall project, Chung adopted an extraordinary method that no one else could have imagined. Because the tide was so powerful, it swept away every foundation stone. He therefore sank a disused oil tanker weighing more than 200,000 tons on the seabed to block the waves, filled in above it, and completed the seawall. His imagination had been put to work.

By contrast, rather than seeking the company of artists, Lee Byung-chull immersed himself in his own artistic interests. Two or three times a week, for example, he went with close friends to listen to traditional Korean music.

According to the recollections of Kyobo[^FB-F043] founder Chairman Shin Yong-ho, Lee once even organized a traditional Korean music concert simply so that he could listen to the music.

Because Shin did not understand music theory, he would sometimes grow bored and want to leave. Lee would gently persuade him to stay: “Oh, listen a little longer.”

Lee particularly loved performances of the *gayageum*.[^FB-F044] He would close his eyes softly and immerse himself in the music. After a while, tapping his knee lightly, he would exclaim, “Ah, how exquisite,” and savor the joy the music brought him. During sorrowful passages, his eyes often filled with tears.

Lee's artistic interests did not end there. The Painting of the Tathagata in his collection was another example. In 1979, Chairman Lee acquired this Buddhist painting from the Goryeo period from a cultural institution in Japan.

It was a cultural artifact that had found its way to Japan during the period of Japanese rule over Korea. Large numbers of Goryeo Buddhist paintings left the country during that period, leaving only a little more than one hundred in Korea. Even today, it is extremely difficult to view a fine Buddhist painting in Korea.

“The triangular composition formed by Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva, Avalokitesvara Bodhisattva, and the Tathagata both relieves tension in the viewer's mind and gives the whole work a harmonious and tranquil quality.”

This was the assessment made by Cho Yu-jeon, director of the National Research Institute of Cultural Heritage, when he saw the painting.

After the painting returned to Korea, it caused a tremendous sensation. Regarded as the most valuable Buddhist painting in the country, it was designated National Treasure No. 218.

Lee's aesthetic interests continued to develop. In April 1982, he used the artworks he had collected over many years to establish the Ho-Am[^FB-F045] Art Museum. Today, the museum in Yongin displays 1,336 works of art. Its collection of historic works includes twelve National Treasures and eight designated Treasures.

The National Treasures include a celadon gourd-shaped ewer with a lotus design in underglaze copper (National Treasure No. 133); a gilt-bronze triad of bodhisattvas (No. 134); a dragon-headed banner finial (No. 136); bronze artifacts excavated from Bisan-dong, Daegu (No. 137); a gold crown and its ornaments (No. 138); a screen depicting a gathering of immortals (No. 139); a mirror inlaid with mother-of-pearl designs of flowers, birds, and animals (No. 140); a celadon vase with an incised bamboo-joint design (No. 169); a bronze box with silver-inlaid designs of floral scrolls and phoenixes (No. 171); artifacts excavated from the tomb of Lady Jeong in Jinyang County (No. 172); and an ink-on-white-paper manuscript of the *Avatamsaka Sutra* from Silla (No. 196).

The designated Treasures include a flat-bottomed, boat-shaped earthenware vessel (No. 555); an earthenware shoe (No. 556); large-hoop earrings (No. 557); a celadon *maebyeong* with inlaid designs of clouds, cranes, peonies, and chrysanthemums (No. 558); an inscribed lacquer bowl (No. 559); and a gilt-bronze pensive Maitreya Bodhisattva (No. 643).

Today, artifacts from the Ho-Am Art Museum are on display in the British Museum. The treasures in the British Museum's Korean Gallery all come from the Ho-Am Art Museum. The British Museum was completed in 1997;[^TN-CH22-001] before that, it had no Korean Gallery. When the author first visited the museum, he was deeply disappointed to find no Korean Gallery. Thanks to the efforts of Samsung Group, the British Museum now has one. This deserves considerable praise.

The British Museum's Japanese Gallery, however, is larger than its Korean Gallery, and its artifacts are somewhat higher in artistic quality.

From a national perspective, the quality of the artifacts in the Korean Gallery also needs to be raised. The discussion has strayed somewhat from the subject, but in any event, Lee Byung-chull was a farsighted connoisseur of cultural artifacts.
