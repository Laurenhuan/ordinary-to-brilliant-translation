# Trust and Scrutiny

“Whatever it is, if you think it is difficult, it will become infinitely difficult. If you think it is simple, it truly will become simple.”

Chung Ju-yung and Lee Byung-chull approached the assignment of work to their subordinates differently. Chung trusted his subordinates and entrusted tasks to them, whereas Lee trusted them while continuing to scrutinize their performance.

One incident occurred when Hyundai Engineering & Construction was selecting a site for a shipyard in Ulsan. Chung first bought land at Yeompo-ri in Ulsan's inner harbor and designated it for the shipyard, then sent a team to assess the soil.

The Hyundai Engineering & Construction executive in charge was Jeon Gap-won. Tests concluded that the soil there was unsuitable for a shipyard. When Jeon returned to Seoul, Chung gave him a severe scolding.

Jeon returned to Ulsan to withdraw the British technical team responsible for the soil tests. Physically and mentally exhausted, he ordered Bangeojin's[^FB-F038] famous whale meat with soju. After becoming slightly drunk, he walked to Ulsan's outer harbor.

Ulsan's three outer harbors—Jeonha Bay, Mipo Bay, and Ilsan Bay—lay side by side, amid picturesque beaches and fishing villages. As Jeon took in the scene, a thought suddenly came to him: this area was better suited to a shipyard than the inner harbor.

Jeon took the British technical team, which he had originally intended to send home, to Bangeojin. Beginning the next day, they assessed the soil and geological strata around Mipo Bay. He had not reported this to the chairman and was acting on his own initiative.

The investigation showed that the underlying strata were extremely solid. Geographically, the harbor was surrounded by mountains, so winds and waves were relatively mild. As an outer harbor, it also offered far better conditions for a shipyard than the inner harbor. It was closer than Yeompo-ri to Pohang Iron and Steel, then under construction, and its low shoreline gave it virtually limitless development potential.

After four days of surveying, the results showed that the strata were firm and the soil excellent. But Jeon began to worry. He had ordered the survey without seeking prior approval, and he had no idea how furious the chairman would be.

Back in Seoul, Jeon went to the chairman's office and reported what had happened. Chung flew into a rage when he heard the report.

“Who told you to come back like this?”

“What?”

Jeon did not understand what the chairman meant and stood there dumbfounded.

“Once you had the survey results, you should have stayed in Ulsan, negotiated the price, and bought the land. Why did you come all the way back to Seoul?”

Chung smiled as he finished speaking. Only then did Jeon realize that the chairman was praising rather than criticizing him, and a smile spread across his own face.

When Samsung built a paper mill in Jeonju, Lee Byung-chull considered the site's geographical conditions, but gave even greater priority to benefiting the local community.

When word spread that Samsung was going to build a factory in Jeonju, local residents competed to sell their land. Lee purchased 67,000 *pyeong*. A survey, however, found that the ground was clay and unsuitable for construction. A paper mill was a heavy-industrial facility and could not be built on clay, yet bringing in soil from elsewhere would entail enormous construction costs.

Jeong Su-chang, who was responsible for the project, reported to Lee: “Chairman, perhaps this is for the best. Why not take this opportunity to move the site to Yeri in Gunsan,[^FB-F039] where access to sea and rail transport and to water is much more convenient?”

Lee replied, “We knew from the beginning that Jeonju was at a disadvantage compared with other places. As I often say, a business must not pursue immediate profits. When running a business, the first consideration should be contributing to the nation and society. In particular, wasn't the main purpose of building this paper mill to fulfill the people of Jeonju's desire for economic development?”

“Yes, yes, it was.” Only then did Jeong understand Lee's thoughtful intentions, and he nodded repeatedly.

“To disregard the enthusiasm of the people of Jeonju and build the factory somewhere else would betray those who sold us their land. So put that idea completely out of your mind. Think instead about how to build the factory at the foot of Jeonju's renowned Hwangbang

Mountain.”

In the end, Lee's proposal was followed, and the paper mill was built on more than 100,000 *pyeong* of land in Palbok-dong at the foot of Hwangbang Mountain.

Chung Ju-yung often assigned work to his subordinates on the basis of seemingly sudden judgments. Yet those sudden judgments contained both singular insight and precise calculation. From the moment an employee handed him a report, his mind would race, producing what appeared to be an impromptu decision. Rather than agonizing over a problem, he treated it simply and then set about accomplishing the task.

“Whatever it is, if you think it is difficult, it will become infinitely difficult. If you think it is simple, it truly will become simple.”

Just as Chung himself said, whenever he began something, he simplified the problem and then worked to carry it out. Precise calculations naturally lay behind this approach, but there was another reason: he knew that simplifying a matter before tackling it made the goal easier to achieve.

“The success or failure of everything depends on the mindset and attitude of the person doing it. Taking on something new certainly involves great risk. Without risk, one can only stand still, and then retreat, until finally giving up.” This was Chung's view of the spirit of risk-taking.

Lee Byung-chull believed that large enterprises must sometimes rise above profit. He once put it this way:

“Every enterprise has social responsibilities of its own, and those responsibilities differ according to the size of the enterprise. Compared with large enterprises, small businesses operate on a smaller scale and with a narrower field of vision, so their first priority is to manage their own affairs well. That is not enough for a large enterprise. Especially given its close ties with numerous related businesses, a large enterprise cannot concern itself solely with its own development. It must broaden its perspective; in many cases, business activity must transcend the temptation of profit.”

Lee's insistence on the Jeonju site for the paper mill arose from his belief that the development of the regional economy was essential, even if the company had to incur some losses.

[^FB-F038]: A place name in Ulsan, Korea.

[^FB-F039]: Both are place names in North Jeolla Province, Korea.
