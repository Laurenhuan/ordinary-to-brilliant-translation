# The Thrift of Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung

When Chung Ju-yung went to meet National Defense Commission Chairman Kim Jong Il, he wore only a fedora, a worn jacket, and

a pair of leather shoes he had owned for more than ten years as he broke through the barrier between North and South. In his entire life, Chung never heard anyone praise his clothes. To Koreans, however, his figure stood taller than it would have in any expensive attire: he was a beloved entrepreneur with the presence of a father.

Wealthy people have all passed through periods of diligence and thrift. Contrary to rumor, Lee Byung-chull also had a frugal side.

Once on a golf course, Lee picked up a tee from the spot where the player ahead of him had just taken a shot. The caddie beside him saw that one side of the tee was broken and that it was very worn. Lee picked it up, flicked off the mud, wiped it clean, and carefully placed it in his trouser pocket, looking thoroughly satisfied. The caddie observed the entire scene. Lee gave a slightly embarrassed smile. “One must be economical in life,” he said, then walked toward the next position.

A new tee cost only one or two thousand won, yet finding such an old one brought a look of deep satisfaction to his face, and he put it in his pocket. The caddie was profoundly moved by what he had seen.

As Lee walked to the next point on the course, the caddie pulled the cart beside him. Whenever an incline made the cart difficult to move, Lee always helped pull it. This revealed something of his character.

Chung Ju-yung was also extremely sparing in daily life.

At the golf course, he would take out a tee so worn that the caddie could not imagine how long it had been used. Yet Chung still used it for his shot and never forgot to take it home afterward.

After Chung died, his home in Cheongun-dong was opened to the public, revealing white cloth spread across the rooms. He had often told employees that his home had never contained luxurious carpeting. After his death, people saw that it was exactly as he had said.

Instead of carpet, the floor was covered with a white cloth, washed perfectly clean and densely covered in patches. The television in his room was equally astonishing: an old, battered Goldstar set of indeterminate vintage, the kind that even an ordinary salaried household would no longer watch.

Another striking object in his room was a pair of leather shoes he had worn for twenty-two years. They were black shoes used for so long that their surfaces were covered in dents. Such was the life of Chung Ju-yung, the richest man in the Republic of Korea.

Anyone who hears the story of Chung's shoes can understand how frugally he lived even after becoming a *chaebol* leader.

Chung had large feet and could not wear ready-made shoes. They had to be custom-made, so he cared for them attentively and wore them until they fell apart.

One day, Adviser K of the Korean Olympic Committee attended an international conference with Chung and traveled on the same plane. By chance, they were seated next to each other.

Chung was often short of sleep, so he slept while riding in cars and airplanes. That day, too, he fell sound asleep as soon as he sat down.

Adviser K caught sight of Chung's shoes beneath the seat. “What? How could they be like that?”

Adviser K was so astonished that his mouth fell open. He rubbed his eyes and looked again. Each side of Chung's shoes had a hole where his big toe pressed against it. K could not bring himself to ask Chung directly, but he judged that the shoes had been worn for at least fifteen years.

Chung wore every pair of shoes for more than ten years.

His choice of cars offered another glimpse of his thrift. At all times, he preferred to ride in vehicles made by Hyundai.

On one occasion, Dr. K, honorary director of Hospital S, went to the nineteenth floor of the Publishing Center to attend a celebration for the publication of Dr. M's book. When the event ended and he entered the elevator, Chung walked in alone. Despite being chairman of a major corporate group, he had not brought a single attendant.

When they reached the ground-floor lobby, Dr. K asked Chung, “What is your license-plate number? I'll have someone bring your car around.”

Dr. K was calling for his own car and thought he would have Chung's brought around at the same time. But Chung firmly declined. “There's no need. I'll do it myself.”

About seven or eight minutes later, Dr. K's car arrived first. Thinking that he should not leave before Chairman Chung, he waited a little longer. But after a long while, Chung's car still had not appeared.

“What is your license-plate number? Let me have someone call again,” Dr. K asked once more.

“I'll do it myself.” Chung strode over to the building's security guard and asked him to call for the car over the public-address system.

After another three or four minutes, a car stopped in front of the lobby. To everyone's surprise, it was a

Stellar,[^FB-F046] and Chung immediately got inside.

At that moment, the security guard ran over and shouted, “Hey! Who told you that you could park here?”

The guard had not seen Chung get into the car. Fearing that the foreign cars belonging to other chairmen would soon arrive, he protested at the audacity of this Stellar in stopping directly in front of the lobby.

Realizing what had happened, Dr. K rushed over and shouted at the guard, “Hey, do you know who's in that car? That's Hyundai Group Chairman Chung Ju-yung. Look carefully at the guest before you speak. How can you shout merely on the basis of the car?”

Only then did the guard see Chung sitting inside, and he was terrified.

Though only a small episode from daily life, it showed how Chung, by riding in a car made by his own Hyundai Group, used his own face as a mobile billboard for the company.

Once, Chung went for a drink at a nightclub owned by comedian Lee Ju-il. Like anyone else, he ordered the minimum purchase—five bottles of beer and a plate of snacks—and watched the singers and dancers for a while. Then he rose to leave. Two bottles of beer remained unopened. At the counter, he said they should not be included in his bill.

The nightclub explained that the amount was the “minimum purchase” and had to be paid whether the beer was consumed or not. Chung insisted that he would not pay for what he had not drunk. Although this put the nightclub in a very difficult position, it ultimately followed his wishes and removed the price of the two bottles from the bill.

Chung would not waste a single coin. The incident illustrates his simplicity and thrift.

Lee Gwang-ha, age fifty-two, at Cheil Industries' Gumi plant received an order. It came from Chairman Lee Byung-chull: develop “the finest fabric in the world.” By then, Cheil Industries had developed for more than thirty years and had gained wide recognition in Korea. Lee's sights, however, were set on the highest standard in the world.

During his lifetime, Chairman Lee was the most fashionable figure in the Korean business community. Even as a young man, he wore the finest pure-wool clothing made in Britain, and he was exceedingly particular about his attire.

Cheil Industries assigned the task to Lee Gwang-ha, who immediately began preparing. Since joining the company in 1968, Lee had spent twenty years identifying and selecting wool and was considered the foremost figure in the field.

To produce the finest fabric in the world, he began by selecting the wool itself. First he took the best-quality fibers, from the sheep's shoulders, then selected the finest strands one by one by hand. From wool that ordinarily measured 18.5 microns, he chose only the finest fibers, measuring 16.5 microns. From three tons of wool, Lee ultimately selected two hundred pounds, a process that took ten days. Choosing fine wool by the feel of one's fingers was arduous work.

He spun those two hundred pounds into yarn, producing 100-count fabric. A 100-count yarn, the source explains, means that one gram of wool can be drawn out to a length of one hundred meters. The resulting thread was finer than the silk spun by a young spider.

Korea became the third country in the world to develop 100-count yarn, after the fashion leaders Italy and Japan. It was a remarkable achievement for Cheil Industries.

The next challenge was 130-count yarn: developing the world's finest 14.5-micron wool. When the 130-count fabric appeared, it was highly praised in Australia, a country renowned for the quality of its wool.

Australia's wool market is the most demanding in the world, dividing wool into as many as 917 types. Even wool of identical thickness is classified into seven grades. It was in this market that the 130-count fabric won recognition. Light, soft, warm, and highly breathable, it ranked among the finest fabrics in the world. Following Lee Byung-chull's instruction, Lee Gwang-ha had developed 130-count fabric of world-leading quality.

In 1987, despite being seventy-seven years old, Lee personally oversaw affairs under the conviction that every company he had founded should become a world-class enterprise.

Lee ordinarily favored American-style casual wear, with attractive-colored cashmere trousers. Possessing an exceptional sense of color, he wore pink T-shirts that were extremely rare at the time. His aesthetic sensibility directly influenced Cheil Industries' technological development.

Chairman Chung Ju-yung, by contrast, paid no attention to clothing. This may have been related to the industries he ran: in shipbuilding and heavy industry, excessive concern with attire could interfere with work. He was also personally unpretentious and did not wish to devote much thought to his clothes.

He wore the same jacket for more than ten years and did not care even when its lapels became damaged. The only fashionable item he owned was his hat. When he crossed Panmunjom to visit North Korea, television coverage invariably showed him wearing a fedora. He owned more than ten hats, but used his gloves and leather jackets until they wore out; only his fedoras suggested the slightest extravagance.

When Chung went to meet National Defense Commission Chairman Kim Jong Il, he wore only a fedora, a worn jacket, and a pair of leather shoes he had owned for more than ten years as he broke through the barrier between North and South. In his entire life, Chung never heard anyone praise his clothes. To Koreans, however, his figure stood taller than it would have in any expensive attire: he was a beloved entrepreneur with the presence of a father.

[^FB-F046]: The Stellar was an inexpensive car made by Hyundai Group.
