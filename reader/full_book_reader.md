# The Son of a Great Landowner and the Son of a Poor Farmer.

The two men came from starkly different backgrounds. A similar contrast can be found in Japan:

Sony founder Akio Morita, one of the figures associated with “the three great myths of Korean business,” held a doctorate in business administration. By contrast, Hitachi founder Namihei Odaira[^TN-CH01-001] never even finished primary school, dropping out after the fourth grade.

From birth, Lee Byung-chull never had to worry about food or clothing. Chung Ju-yung, by contrast, had something to eat in the morning but went hungry at noon; at night, he had only a bowl of wild-greens porridge to fill his stomach before going to sleep.

Lee Byung-chull (1910–1987) was born into a wealthy family.

The landowning family could harvest 2,000 seok (a traditional Korean grain measure) in a good year and 1,500 seok even in a poor one.

Lee Byung-chull was born in Uiryeong, Gyeongsangnam-do[^FB-F002], into the family of Lee Chan-woo, a landowner, and was the youngest of four sons. His grandfather, Lee Hong-seok, was an eminent Confucian scholar of his time and a wealthy landowner whose family had held considerable property for generations. Born into a landowning family, Lee Byung-chull studied Chinese classics for a time at a traditional Korean village school known as a seodang before entering primary school and continuing his formal education through middle school. He later studied at Waseda University in Japan.

Chung Ju-yung (1915–2001) was born into a poor farming family in Tongcheon County, Gangwon Province. He was the eldest of six sons and had two sisters. His entire education consisted of the schooling he received from ages six to nine at a seodang and at Songjeon Elementary School.

Born into poverty, Chung Ju-yung ran away from home four times in search of a livelihood.

The two men came from starkly different backgrounds.

In Japan, a similar contrast can be found. Sony founder Akio Morita, one of the figures associated with “the three great myths of Korean business,” held a doctorate in business administration, while Hitachi founder Namihei Odaira never even finished primary school, dropping out after the fourth grade.

From birth, Lee Byung-chull never had to worry about food or clothing. Chung Ju-yung, by contrast, had something to eat in the morning but went hungry at noon; at night, he had only a bowl of wild-greens porridge to fill his stomach before going to sleep.

While Lee Byung-chull was studying at Waseda University in Japan, the monthly allowance from his family equaled five months' earnings for a salaried worker. Chung Ju-yung, meanwhile, was running away from home time and again. He hoped to earn money elsewhere and escape a life in which even ceaseless farm labor in the sweltering summer heat could not provide enough to eat.

If the two men had anything in common, it was that both had studied at a seodang.

Beginning at age five, Lee Byung-chull studied *The Thousand-Character Classic* at Munsanjeong, a seodang founded by his grandfather. Over the next five years, he studied works including *Zizhi Tongjian* (*Comprehensive Mirror in Aid of Governance*) and *The Analects*.

Chung Ju-yung's grandfather also taught at a seodang. Beginning at age six, Chung studied at his grandfather's seodang for three years, reading *Elementary Learning*, *The Great Learning*, *Zizhi Tongjian*, and five-character and seven-character verse, among other works. There was little left for him to learn at the primary-school level.

It is worth emphasizing that although Chung Ju-yung spent only three short years at a seodang, the classical Chinese works he studied—including *The Great Learning*, *Zizhi Tongjian*, and five-character and seven-character verse—are texts that even today's graduates of four-year universities would be unable to read and translate.

By that measure, Chung Ju-yung's learning could hardly be called limited.

Leaving aside the question of how extensive that learning was, *Elementary Learning* and *The Great Learning* alone cover much of what one needs to know about human relations and the ways of the world. *Zizhi Tongjian* chronicles the history of China's successive imperial houses over a span of 1,300 years. Scholars of the Joseon period regarded it, together with *Tongjian Jieyao* and *Zizhi Tongjian Gangmu*, as essential reading.

Before Kim Ok-gyun—a renowned calligrapher of the late Joseon period[^FB-F003] and a gifted figure who launched the Gapsin Coup—negotiated with Li Hongzhang at Donghwa Inn in Shanghai in 1894, he kept *Zizhi Tongjian* beside his bed.

*Zizhi Tongjian* is more than a history book. It also records the conduct of China's emperors across successive dynasties and offers a wealth of historical perspectives.

In other words, all the management principles used to build a modern “kingdom” can be found within it. This shows the depth of Chung Ju-yung's learning.

On the first floor of his Cheongun-dong home, Chung Ju-yung displayed his favorite maxim: “With diligence, no task is impossible.” Whenever he faced a grave crisis, he would recall and quote sayings such as “Where there is a will, there is a way” and “the investigation of things and the extension of knowledge.” Such allusions and quotations could hardly have come from someone without learning or cultivation.

In fact, *The Thousand-Character Classic* alone is enough to grasp the principles of how to conduct oneself and deal with others. A single careful reading provides an understanding of nearly everything about human relations and the ways of the world. Together with *Elementary Learning*, *The Great Learning*, and *Zizhi Tongjian*, this learning would already meet the requirements of a modern university education.

Lee Byung-chull also frequently quoted the classics. While still at a seodang, he had already reached the point where he could read *The Thousand-Character Classic* in full and even *The Analects*.

*The Analects* presents a spiritual ideal centered on humaneness (ren), harmony (he), and joy (le).

To lead a large workforce and build a major enterprise, the principles of humaneness and harmony are indispensable. Equally indispensable is the idea of joy: working with pleasure, taking on challenges in one's own endeavors, and recognizing one's achievements.

Yet aside from their shared cultural foundation in the ancient classics of the East, the two men could not have been more different.

# “The Returnee” and the Runaway Boy

In the early 1930s, Lee Byung-chull cut short his studies abroad and returned home. Chung Ju-yung, after running away from home four times, finally found his first job with a steady income.

While Lee Byung-chull experienced firsthand the sweeping tide of history on the streets of Tokyo, Japan, then under the shadow of the Great Depression, Chung Ju-yung was embarking on bold ventures far from home in search of a livelihood.

In October 1929, Lee Byung-chull, then twenty years old, left to study in Japan.

He left Busan aboard the 3,000-ton “Busan–Shimonoseki” ferry, which operated between Busan, Korea, and Shimonoseki in Japan, and sailed for the Japanese port.

As the ship neared Genkai Sea, the waves grew fierce, and the second-class cabin at the bottom of the ship pitched violently. Lee Byung-chull became increasingly seasick and tried to move to first class, where the motion was less severe. But a Japanese police officer stopped him at the cabin entrance and said, “How could a Korean have any money? And you think you can enter first class?” The officer then began questioning Lee closely about his identity. It was then that Lee felt deeply what it meant to endure “the misery of a people deprived of their country.”

Korea at the time was what was described as a country of “four lacks”—no country, no sovereignty, no enterprises, and no wealthy people.

Humiliated by the Japanese police officer, Lee Byung-chull resolved that, whatever it took, he would make his country strong and its people prosperous.

From Shimonoseki, he traveled by train for more than twenty hours to Tokyo, Japan's capital. He rented a room on the outskirts of the city, paying by the month. The following year, in the spring of 1930, Lee Byung-chull entered the School of Political Science and Economics, Waseda University.

At that very time, a financial panic was unfolding on Wall Street in the United States.

October 24, 1929.

The stock market plunged, and an American bank in New York found itself unable to honor withdrawals. Bank failures followed. As many as 1,300 banks went bankrupt, unleashing panic on a global scale.

The number of unemployed people in the United States suddenly rose to 12 million. At the same time, unemployment in Germany reached 6 million, and its economy began to slide into depression. Australia's central bank failed. Economic panic then spread to Britain, where the Bank of England also found itself unable to meet demands for cash.

In an instant, the financial crisis swept across the globe.

Japan's economy, already depressed after the Great Kanto Earthquake of 1923, was suddenly struck by the global financial crisis. Industrial output fell by 70 percentage points, exports declined by 37%, and imports plunged by 40%. Prices then began to fall, and the price of rice dropped by half within a matter of months.

Japanese industry was also hit by the Great Depression and began laying off workers. Factory closures followed. In an instant, hundreds of thousands of unemployed people poured into the streets; together with those who had lost their jobs and returned home, their number exceeded 3 million. Strikes broke out in factories day after day, and even university graduates could not find work. A film satirizing this reality, *I Graduated, But...*, became a subject of widespread discussion.

The atmosphere in Japanese society became deeply unsettled.

Young people influenced by leftist thought took to the streets in demonstrations day after day, while books by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels became enormously popular.

Lee Byung-chull also joined the demonstrators and spent two days in detention, though he himself was not a follower of proletarian ideology. Around that time, he developed a severe case of beriberi. Despite careful recuperation, his condition showed little improvement, and he decided to return home. This was in the autumn of his second year at Waseda University. Japan's launching of the Mukden Incident may also have contributed to his decision to return.

Lee Byung-chull's studies abroad ended with his withdrawal from university. During the little more than two years he spent overseas, he witnessed the devastation of the economic crisis and experienced the rapid social decline it brought in its wake.

Lee Byung-chull experienced firsthand how shifts in the world economy could reach ordinary people and profoundly affect their lives.

In 1930, while Lee Byung-chull was studying in Japan, Chung Ju-yung graduated from Songjeon Elementary School.

It was the first—and last—formal education he would receive in his life.

Having graduated from primary school only at the age of fifteen, the only work before him was farming.

He took up grueling farm work at a young age, and rural life continued day after day. He ate millet rice in the morning, went hungry at noon, and in the evening had only wild-greens porridge to fill his stomach.

When spring came, farmers who had been living on grass and tree roots had to leave their homes in search of a livelihood in northern Gando, within “Manchukuo”[^FB-F004].

Chung Ju-yung hated farm work. No matter how hard he labored in the fields,

there was still no guarantee of three meals a day.

At the time, the greatest source of hope in life for Chung Ju-yung, a primary-school graduate, was the novel *The Soil* by Yi Kwang-su. The novel was serialized in *The Dong-A Ilbo*. The district chief's household subscribed to the newspaper, and every evening Chung walked two kilometers to their home so that he could read the novel.

Chung Ju-yung wanted to become a lawyer like the protagonist of *The Soil*. Determined to make something of himself in Seoul, he decided to run away from home.

His first attempt took him toward Chongjin[^FB-F005].

He had read in a newspaper that a large ironworks and port were being built in Chongjin. He held a firm belief: as long as he had physical strength and confidence, there was nothing he could not accomplish.

On the way to Chongjin, he passed a railroad construction site. He decided to earn some money there first and began hauling earth in a wheelbarrow. He lived and ate at the site. His daily wage was 45 sen,[^TN-CH02-001] but meals cost 32 sen, leaving only 13 sen after a full day of backbreaking labor. Even that 13 sen was not guaranteed: when rain halted work, he earned nothing at all. At times, he even fell behind on his meal charges. The punishing work was also more than a boy who had not yet reached adulthood could bear. Even so, he felt the fulfillment of earning his own living through his own labor.

After working at the site for about two months, he was found by his father, who had tracked him down. He had no choice but to return home with him.

That brought his first attempt to run away to an end.

On his second attempt to run away, he headed for Mount Kumgang. A swindler cheated him out of his travel money along the way, and his father found him once again and took him home.

His third attempt was bolder still: he stole the money from the sale of the family cow and ran away.

With that money, he reached Seoul and enrolled in a local bookkeeping school to study accounting, only to be found by his father in Seoul and taken home again.

From today's perspective, after running away from home three times in succession, Chung Ju-yung might look like a troubled youth. But he was not. Why? Because he did not squander any of the money he had stolen from the sale of the cow.

In 1933, at age nineteen, Chung Ju-yung ran away for the fourth time. At first he worked as a stevedore and performed other heavy labor on the Incheon docks; later he went to Seoul.

By his own account, it was there that he found his first steady job, working as a deliveryman at Bokheung Rice Store (Bokheung Sanghoe).

In the early 1930s, Lee Byung-chull cut short his studies abroad and returned home. Chung Ju-yung, after running away from home four times, finally found his first job with a steady income.

While Lee Byung-chull experienced firsthand the sweeping tide of history on the streets of Tokyo, Japan, then under the shadow of the Great Depression, Chung Ju-yung was embarking on bold ventures far from home in search of a livelihood.

The courage never to give up ran throughout Chung Ju-yung's life.

Where did that courage come from?

Chung Ju-yung once reflected on this question.

After his father found him at the railroad worksite, Chung Ju-yung walked 300 ri home.[^TN-CH02-002] Along the way, they passed an orchard near Anbyon[^FB-F006]. There his father bought several apples to take home to Chung's grandmother. But the apples were neither fresh nor attractive; they were the kind of cheap, inferior apples no one wanted.

A few rotten apples.

Although Chung Ju-yung wrote in his autobiography that the incident had “left a deep impression on his memory,” those words did not fully convey the pain he felt.

A farmer's life in which one could afford only cheap, rotten apples. An ordinary person might at the time have felt sorrow, dejection, and despair. Chung Ju-yung, however, turned that despair into a driving force that propelled him forward.

More than seventy years after running away with the money from the sale of his father's cow, Chung Ju-yung drove a herd of 500 cattle to North Korea.

Anyone familiar with the story of his childhood attempts to run away can understand that his act of turning his father's one cow into 500 and driving them back to his hometown was

an expression of how deeply he regretted that episode.

He had stolen the money his father had earned through the hard work of raising and selling the cow, then used it to pay tuition and lodging at a local bookkeeping school. Now he wanted to repay his father five hundredfold—no, to make amends for his unfilial act with whatever he could offer.

A few rotten apples. The money from a single cow. A farmer's life in which a full day of labor could not fill one's stomach. Such was the portrait of Chung Ju-yung's life in the 1930s.

There is one more story worth telling: the lesson Chung Ju-yung learned from bedbugs.

It happened during his fourth attempt to run away, when he was doing hard labor at the Incheon docks. The workers' bunkhouse was a paradise for bedbugs. Although everyone was exhausted after a full day's work, the bites made it impossible to sleep at night. The insects were unremarkable to look at, but they caused unbearable misery. One day Chung Ju-yung devised a solution. Since sleeping in bed under a blanket left him vulnerable to bites, he slept on the dining table instead. Just as he expected, the bedbugs could not reach him there.

But the relief was only temporary. The bedbugs began climbing the table legs and biting him there.

Chung Ju-yung came up with another good idea. He stood each of the table's four legs in an enamel basin filled with water. Any bedbug trying to climb a leg would fall into the water and drown. Sure enough, bedbugs trying to climb the legs fell into the basins and drowned. But only a few died. The bedbugs, too, found a new way: to reach human blood, they climbed the walls to the ceiling and then dropped onto the sleepers below.

At that moment, Chung Ju-yung grasped a truth:

If insignificant bedbugs could persevere until they found a way past the water-filled basins and achieved their goal, what could human beings, the highest of all creatures, possibly fail to accomplish?

“Where there is a will, there is a way.”

That was the lesson Chung Ju-yung drew from the bedbugs.

In later years, this lesson proved immensely powerful whenever his enterprises faced difficulties.

Whether he secured orders worth tens of millions of dollars to establish a shipyard while holding a banknote bearing an image of a turtle ship, played golf with red balls on a snow-covered course in the depths of winter, or transplanted barley to complete a project when no turf could be found in winter, all these feats sprang from that lesson.

On his fourth attempt to run away, he gained tremendous insight from the bedbugs.

That insight later gave him the certainty that whenever he encountered a difficulty—whatever it might be—he would find a solution.

Lee Byung-chull was one of the elite, having studied at Waseda University in Japan. It may not seem surprising that he founded Samsung Group, but it is far more astonishing that Chung Ju-yung, with only a primary-school education, built a great enterprise like Hyundai.

Similar examples can be found in other countries.

Consider Japan first: Namihei Odaira,[^TN-CH02-003] founder of Hitachi Group; Akio Morita, founder of Sony; and Soichiro Honda, founder of Honda.

Of the three, Namihei Odaira left school in the fourth grade, Soichiro Honda never attended school at all, and only Akio Morita held a doctorate in business administration.

Interestingly, Namihei Odaira, Soichiro Honda, and Chung Ju-yung shared many similarities.

First, consider Namihei Odaira (1894–1989). A fourth-grade education was all the schooling he received, and his family was extremely poor.

At age ten, he went to another household to look after children, then learned to repair bicycles at a bicycle shop. Later he moved into the electrical industry and laid the foundation for Hitachi Group, now a world-class manufacturer of household appliances.

Soichiro Honda (1906–1991), who built Honda, was the son of a blacksmith. Because his family was poor, he could not attend school and began working at a bicycle shop when he was very young. From age sixteen, he apprenticed in automobile repair in Tokyo. That experience had profound significance for his life.

Soichiro Honda devoted his life to the automobile business and resembled Chung Ju-yung in many respects.

Strangely enough, people with little formal education seem to start businesses more often than people with advanced degrees.

Henry Ford (1863–1947), founder of Ford Motor Company, had also gone no further than primary school. Ford's father hoped that his son would become a farmer and help him, but Ford ran away from home and became an apprentice at a machine shop.

Ford's father, too, tracked down his son and asked him to return home with him, but Ford insisted on following his own path and took a job at a shipbuilding company in Detroit. In the end, his father gave way. The situation closely resembled Chung Ju-yung's.

Kiichiro Toyoda[^TN-CH02-004] (1867–1930), who could be called Japan’s automotive king, followed a similar path. Kiichiro Toyoda created Toyota, a brand that came to represent Japan.

Kiichiro Toyoda's father was a carpenter. As soon as Kiichiro Toyoda graduated from middle school, his father began teaching him carpentry.

At first, Kiichiro Toyoda settled down to learn the trade. But at nineteen he left without a word and walked to Tokyo, without first telling his parents. He could not suppress his curiosity about and longing for the outside world. Eventually, he invented a cotton loom and achieved enormous success in the automobile industry. In this respect, too, he closely resembled Chung Ju-yung.

Viewed in this light, founding a business and developing oneself have little to do with academic credentials. Indeed, knowledge gained through practical work is more useful than knowledge learned in school.

Professional Go player Cho Chikun once said, “School makes people stupid.”

He, too, left school in his first year of high school.

By contrast, Lee Byung-chull was born the son of a wealthy landowner, grew up in favorable circumstances, received a good education, and founded a major enterprise. Cases like his are comparatively rare.

From this perspective, Lee Byung-chull's entrepreneurial journey is all the more worthy of study. Why? Because people raised in affluent circumstances often find it easy to settle for the status quo and lose the drive to advance.

# A Young Rice-Store Owner and a Major Landowner in His Early Twenties

After Kyungil Rice Store (Kyungil Sanghoe) closed, Chung Ju-yung arrived at the simple conclusion that “Nothing in the world is difficult for a determined person”;

Lee Byung-chull, by contrast, drew together and analyzed the many lessons he had learned from his experiences during that period.

Lee Byung-chull summarized those lessons as follows:

1. In business, one must always take stock of conditions across the country.

2. Do not pursue a goal blindly; always assess your own abilities and level of competence with a cool head.

3. Do not engage in speculative business in the hope of getting lucky.

4. While valuing your intuition, do not forget the second and third lessons.

5. If the prevailing trend has shown your earlier judgment to be wrong, do not cling to it; find another way forward.

At twenty-two, Chung Ju-yung opened a rice store called Kyungil Rice Store. It was 1936, the year Korean athlete Sohn Kee-chung became an Olympic champion at the Berlin Games.

After running away from home four times, Chung Ju-yung went to work at Bokheung Rice Store (Bokheung Sanghoe). He later took over the store and renamed it Kyungil Rice Store. He was able to take over Bokheung Rice Store solely on the strength of the trust he had earned through his honesty.

Although he was working for someone else, he rose earlier than anyone each day, swept the area in front of the store until it was spotless, and sprinkled it with water. When delivering rice to customers, he never spared himself, diligently weighing and carrying the rice.

The store owner's son was devoted to drinking and women, and the owner was deeply disappointed in him. After Chung Ju-yung had worked at the store for six months, he won the owner's complete trust and was placed in charge of all the accounts at Bokheung Rice Store. Even while managing the books, he remained conscientious and meticulous. Each day, he kept the warehouse in perfect order, with the rice and other grains arranged so that everything was easy to see. Eventually, the owner gave up all hope for his idle son and entrusted Chung Ju-yung with the store, along with its business relationships with suppliers and customers.

The store owner's daughter, Lee Mi-sun—eighty-five years old at the time of the source account—had graduated from Gyeonggi Girls' High School and attended Gyeongseong Normal University.[^TN-CH03-001] She was known as a remarkably talented young woman. In her eyes, Chung Ju-yung was strong and sturdy, always hoisting sacks of rice with ease. At night, while some of the other workers played Chinese chess and others smoked, he invariably sat with a book in his hands. He was quite unlike the rest. Among the books he read then were *The Good Earth* by Pearl S. Buck (1892–1973), *Evergreen Tree* by Sim Hun, and *The Soil* by Yi Kwang-su. Even when he was still a deliveryman at the rice store, he never abandoned his ambition of becoming a lawyer and kept up the habit of reading late into the night by candlelight.

He ran Kyungil Rice Store in Sindang-dong and was constantly busy. Even so, he reached many new customers beyond his existing clientele, including Paiwha Girls' High School and Seoul Girls' Commercial School[^TN-CH03-002] in Hongje-dong, and became a successful rice merchant.

Misfortune, however, soon struck. In 1937, Japan instigated the Marco Polo Bridge Incident in China, and full-scale war broke out between the two countries. The Government-General of Korea[^FB-F007] issued the “wartime system” decree. Its “rice control” measures had a profound impact on Chung Ju-yung. Because a food-rationing system was to be introduced, every rice store in Korea was closed. At that point, Chung Ju-yung had been successfully operating Kyungil Rice Store for only two years. The blow was severe. The rice store he had labored over for two years was forced to close, but for the first time he had demonstrated through his own actions that honest work could lead to success.

Both Chung Ju-yung and Lee Byung-chull came close to achieving business success in their twenties, only to see their ventures fail for external reasons. In this respect, their experiences were remarkably similar.

When Chung Ju-yung's rice store closed because of the Second Sino-Japanese War, Lee Byung-chull suffered a similar bankruptcy.

At twenty-six, Lee Byung-chull resolved to invest in a business venture. He obtained capital from land in Bucheon that produced 300 seok (a traditional Korean grain measure) of rice a year.

Land that produced 300 seok of rice.

In his autobiography, Lee Byung-chull wrote that such land hardly amounted to anything as start-up capital. In reality, that was not the case.

At the time, even an entire myeon[^FB-F008] might not have had more than one or two landowners whose holdings produced 300 seok of grain.

The year Lee Byung-chull acquired this property, Chung Ju-yung was working as a deliveryman at Bokheung Rice Store and, for the first time in his life, had a steady job.

After acquiring the assets, Lee Byung-chull considered what to do with them. At first, he thought of starting a business in Seoul, where there were many possibilities and friends who could help him. But his capital seemed insufficient for a venture there.

He also considered Daegu, Busan, and Pyongyang, but commerce in all three cities was then controlled by the Japanese, and with the financial resources at his disposal it seemed difficult to gain a foothold.

In the end, he chose Masan, close to his hometown, as the place to launch his business.

At the time, Masan was a warm port city blessed with clear water, and agricultural produce from

across Gyeongsangnam-do converged there. Rice alone amounted to several million seok each year, almost all of it shipped to Japan.

Lee Byung-chull concluded that Masan's capacity was far too limited to process a million seok of rice.

The processing plants run by the Japanese were enormous, while those run by Koreans were negligible by comparison. Merchants at the time paid the processing fee in advance and then waited a long time for their grain to be processed, a clear sign of the shortage of capacity. At every plant, the unhusked rice awaiting processing was piled as high as a mountain.

Lee Byung-chull and his friends Jeong Hyeon-yong and Park Jeong-won each invested 10,000 yen[^TN-CH03-003] to establish Hyeopdong Rice Mill in northern Masan. The mill primarily processed rice: it bought unhusked rice, removed the husks, and turned it into polished rice.

The mill's work was simple: it kept milling rice. Even so, when the books were closed after a year, two-thirds of the original capital had been lost. The result was startling.

An analysis revealed the problem. The mill had bought unhusked rice while prices were rising, but by the time the processed rice was sold, prices had fallen. Although the price of rice was set by the Incheon Rice Exchange, business owners in Seoul and other cities commonly engaged in rice futures trading. Lee Byung-chull, however, was buying and processing unhusked rice locally. He believed that movements in rice prices were unimportant and that the heart of the business was simply to keep the machinery running. As a result, he suffered heavy losses from the change in market prices between the purchase of the unhusked rice and the sale of the processed product.

At the time, he made his buying and selling decisions largely on the basis of rumors about changes in rice prices. As with stock-market investing today, constantly acting on rumors leads to losses.

After analyzing the situation, he began moving against the rumors. When rice prices rose and many people bought in expectation of still higher prices, he sold. When prices fell and many people sold for fear that they would fall further, he bought instead. His strategy proved highly effective. By the next accounting, he had recovered the full 30,000 yen in capital and earned an additional 20,000 yen in net profit. His first business venture was a great success.

Lee Byung-chull learned that Masan had too few trucks. Freight charges were high, and he saw good prospects in buying several trucks and establishing a company to transport grain.

Lee Byung-chull bought the Japanese-owned Masan Ilchul Automobile Company, which already had ten trucks, and purchased another ten to launch his next venture. The result was just as he had expected: profits poured in day after day. The rice mill and the transport company flourished in tandem.

Lee Byung-chull began spending his spare time eating and drinking. Masan then had eight or nine restaurants, and he was a regular at every one of them. He visited so often that all eighty or ninety of the gisaeng (professional female entertainers) who worked there knew him.

Lee Byung-chull then began planning a third line of business: buying land. Large amounts of farmland near Gimhae were for sale because the predatory policies of Japanese imperialism were growing steadily harsher, forcing farmers to sell their land. Lee Byung-chull surveyed the arable paddies across the Gimhae Plain, including those already on the market. At the time, 400,000 pyeong[^FB-F009] of paddy land belonging to a Japanese man named Amano was for sale.

Compared with the income the paddies generated at the time, interest on bank loans was low. If most of the purchase price for the land could be borrowed from a bank, it would be like making money while sitting still.

He took his projected income-and-expense statement to the Masan branch of the Chosen Industrial Bank and applied for a loan. Hirata, the bank's branch manager, already regarded him as trustworthy, so the loan was readily approved.

He then used the 400,000 pyeong of land as collateral for further loans and bought more land. He purchased all the farmland near Gimhae. Only a year later, he had become a major landowner with a total of two million pyeong producing 10,000 seok a year.

By the end of the autumn harvest, the proceeds from selling the grain were enough to pay all the interest owed to the bank and still leave a surplus.

While still in his twenties, Lee Byung-chull became the largest landowner in the Gyeongsangnam-do region.

Just as he was dreaming still bigger dreams and preparing for greater endeavors, the Second Sino-Japanese War broke out.

Because of the Second Sino-Japanese War, which began in 1937, the Japanese government took the emergency step of halting bank lending. Lee Byung-chull described the way he felt at the time as “a bolt from the blue.”

The Japanese government ordered all Korean-run rice stores to close. Like Chung Ju-yung, Lee Byung-chull now faced the threat of bankruptcy.

In this respect, the two men had much in common. Both saw their personal business ventures collapse because of changes in the international political situation.

Many years later, a reporter asked Chung Ju-yung, then in his eighties, “What do you consider the most frightening thing in the world?”

Chung Ju-yung replied, “Political change.”

Political change had twice had a tremendous impact on Chung Ju-yung's business.

The first time was when the Second Sino-Japanese War forced his rice store to close. The second came in 1980, with the corporate restructuring demanded by the Special Committee for National Security Measures[^FB-F010] under Chun Doo-hwan's new military group—namely, the consolidation of heavy and chemical industries. In fact, the 1980 restructuring was no more than an exchange between Daewoo Motor and Hyundai Heavy Industries and could hardly be considered a political change affecting Hyundai as a whole.[^TN-CH03-004] The principal occasion on which political change forced Chung Ju-yung to give up his entire business operation was still the closure of Kyungil Rice Store because of the Second Sino-Japanese War.

Once the emergency measures of Japanese imperialism took effect, the market for paddy land collapsed. Lee Byung-chull, who had financed his land investments entirely with bank loans, realized that he could not sustain the situation by his own efforts. He sold all the paddies he had previously bought, accepting prices below market value, and transferred the rice mill and transport company to others as well. With the proceeds, he repaid the loan he had taken from the Chosen Industrial Bank to purchase the two million pyeong of paddy land. Afterward, he was left with only 20,000 yen in cash and 100,000 pyeong of paddy land.

Overnight, Lee Byung-chull was back where he had started.

Recalling that time, Lee Byung-chull said, “Three gains bring three harms.”

By this he meant that when three good things happen, three bad things follow.

As a young man, Lee Byung-chull readily obtained loans from the Chosen Industrial Bank, bought two million pyeong of farmland, and became a major landowner. The land produced 10,000 seok of rice a year. With that money, he even bought residential building sites in Busan and Daegu. Yet the outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War reduced everything to nothing.

After Kyungil Rice Store closed, Chung Ju-yung arrived at the simple conclusion that “Nothing in the world is difficult for a determined person”; Lee Byung-chull, by contrast, drew together and analyzed the many lessons he had learned from his experiences during that period.

Lee Byung-chull summarized those lessons as follows:

1. In business, one must always take stock of conditions across the country.

2. Do not pursue a goal blindly; always assess your own abilities and level of competence with a cool head.

3. Do not engage in speculative business in the hope of getting lucky.

4. While valuing your intuition, do not forget the second and third lessons.

5. If the prevailing trend has shown your earlier judgment to be wrong, do not cling to it; find another way forward.

Although Lee Byung-chull suffered a major failure in his first business venture, he was not discouraged in the least.

He consoled himself with a saying attributed to Helmuth von Moltke the Elder, a Prussian field marshal of the Bismarck era: “I often watch with keen interest as young people make mistakes. Their failures are the measure by which to judge whether they will achieve success later. How they regard failure, how they confront it—whether they become discouraged and shrink back, or draw courage from it and keep moving forward—has a decisive bearing on their lives.”

The lessons he learned at that time became the foundation for his greater successes later in life. When preparing to invest billions of dollars in the semiconductor industry, he conducted an exceptionally detailed study of markets at home and abroad before formally committing the investment.

There were also times when, once circumstances had shown his judgment to be wrong, he pulled back and went no further.

After the May 16 Military Revolution[^TN-CH03-005] in 1961, he held a press conference at Hotel Okura Tokyo and announced that he would return all his assets to society. After the Korea Fertilizer incident,[^TN-CH03-006] he donated the fertilizer plant to the state.

In the years that followed, a survey of the new businesses Lee Byung-chull established shows that almost none were founded out of a blind pursuit of profit. In the 1970s, he was eager to enter the telephone-switching industry, but several specialists on his staff set out reasons for opposing the plan, and he abandoned his original idea entirely.

Even today, Samsung Group does not operate speculative businesses. In other words, the lessons Lee Byung-chull drew at that time became the coordinates that guided his conduct throughout his life.

# Star Noodles and Ado Service Garage

Looking back over Samsung Group from its beginnings to the present, one can identify the following management principles:

Enter a market only after meticulous preliminary research, compete on quality, and give employees ample decision-making authority.

Over all these years, whenever Samsung has entered a new field, it has never departed from these principles.

Chung Ju-yung's boldness in asking for another loan is astonishing, and it is just as surprising that the elderly Oh Yun-geun was willing to lend him money again. That was because he trusted Chung Ju-yung. Chung Ju-yung was a man of honesty and trustworthiness. Even after becoming a business titan, he continued to emphasize the importance of trustworthiness.

After his investment in two million pyeong of land in Gimhae failed, Lee Byung-chull gathered himself and set out once again as an entrepreneur.

On March 1, 1938, at the age of 29, he founded Samsung Company (Samsung Sanghoe), the predecessor of today's Samsung Group, with capital of 30,000 yen.[^TN-CH04-001]

Lee Byung-chull founded Samsung Company in the same year that Chung Ju-yung launched his first business venture.

Samsung Company was established at 61-1 Ingyo-dong in present-day Daegu, near what is now Seomun Market.

The building survived into the present day, but its foundations had sunk with age, and a decision was made to demolish it. When Samsung Company was founded, however, it was a fairly impressive wooden building for its time, with four stories above ground and one below. Lee Byung-chull did not then have the financial means to purchase it outright. The building cost a total of 20,000 yen. He bought it by making a down payment of 10,000 yen and agreeing to pay the remaining 10,000 yen within two years.

Lee Byung-chull chose to start his business in Daegu because of its convenient transportation links. At the time, Daegu was not only a major station on the Gyeongbu Line (Seoul–Busan), but also the center of Yeongnam[^FB-F011] and a distribution hub for goods of every kind.

Even today, Samsung Group places great importance on location when building factories overseas. It calculates the travel time from the port to the factory and from the airport to the factory by car, subway, train, and other forms of transportation, compares the results, and only then selects the factory site.

Samsung Company's business at the time consisted of transporting newly harvested apples and other fruits and vegetables from around Daegu, along with dried fish from Pohang, for sale in Northeast China and Beijing. It also produced and sold noodles, purchasing a flour mill and a noodle-making machine so that it could manufacture the noodles itself.

That Lee Byung-chull built Samsung Group from the trade in noodles and fruit now stands as a model of success in business history.

Although it was only a small company that sold fruit in China and made and sold noodles, Lee Byung-chull conducted extraordinarily thorough research beforehand. Starting in Busan, he traveled through Pyongyang, Sinuiju, Wonsan, and Hungnam, then on to Changchun, Shenyang, and Qingdao in Northeast China, and finally to Shanghai, conducting market research throughout the journey.

Today, Beijing and Shanghai are only two or three hours away by air. At the time, however, traveling to those cities through Northeast China was an unimaginably arduous journey.

Even today, the train journey from Beijing to Shanghai generally takes about 14 hours, and travel through Northeast China took even longer than the journey between Beijing and Shanghai.

Under the conditions of the time, the hardships of the journey were beyond description. Why did he undertake a trip of such unimaginable difficulty?

It was a journey intended to clear his mind and reconsider how to make a fresh start. After his failure in real estate, he experienced bitter regret that came too late. He resolved never again to pursue an undertaking that benefited him at the expense of others. From one perspective, real estate could also be regarded as an industry in which one grew rich through the losses of others.

After failing in real estate, Lee Byung-chull headed north to Seoul, then traveled through Pyongyang, Sinuiju, Wonsan, Hungnam, Changchun, and Shenyang, passed through Shanhaiguan in North China, and reached Beijing. Throughout the journey, he kept thinking about “a business that benefits others while also benefiting oneself.”

Along the way, Lee Byung-chull paid close attention to businesspeople. Apples and dried fish were in very short supply across Northeast China. He thought that selling apples from Daegu and dried fish from Korea's east coast there could bring substantial profits.

Such a business would benefit the fruit growers of Daegu and the fishermen on Korea's east coast, while also serving the residents of Northeast China.

After two grueling months of market research, Lee Byung-chull concluded that there were good prospects for exporting fruit, vegetables, and dried fish to China. He also observed that almost no companies at the time specialized in transporting goods from Korea to China.

This was precisely what set Lee Byung-chull apart from most people.

It took courage to make a journey through China that most people could not imagine, and it took keen powers of observation to conduct meticulous market research in a completely unfamiliar country. After returning home, Lee Byung-chull carefully investigated the apple harvest and the supply of

fish products.

Such exceptional courage and meticulous research remain important intangible assets of Samsung Group even today. When Lee Byung-chull later entered the semiconductor industry, he likewise made his final decision only after detailed research. In this respect, Lee Byung-chull's management philosophy was highly scientific: it rested neither on rough estimates nor on luck.

Having investigated the Chinese market in detail and concluded that the venture was worth pursuing, he founded Samsung Company and entered the world of business. He also established a noodle factory and created the Star Noodles brand. As Japanese imperialism intensified its plunder of food supplies, food became extremely scarce. Witnessing these conditions, Lee Byung-chull opened a noodle factory. He purchased apples from around Daegu and dried squid and other dried fish from around Pohang, then sold them in Northeast China and the Beijing area.

As he had predicted, fruit sold well not only in China but also in Japan. The noodles likewise sold briskly. Each bundle cost 10 sen,[^TN-CH04-002] and each carton contained 60 bundles; more than 100 cartons could be sold in a single day. The customers were mainly wholesalers from Dandong, Shenyang, and other places.

There were five noodle factories in Daegu at the time. Although the Star Noodles made by Samsung Company were more expensive, they sold very well because they tasted good. Lee Byung-chull was already pursuing a strategy of competing on quality.

In an era of food scarcity, when even getting enough to eat was a struggle, pursuing a high-price, high-quality strategy was a remarkably unexpected way of doing business.

The Daegu branch of Seoul Bank[^TN-CH04-003] was Lee Byung-chull's principal business partner. Because of his success in trade, he received a loan of 100,000 yen from the Daegu branch soon after opening his business; within a year, the amount of credit extended to him had soared to 200,000 yen.

Lee Byung-chull was a methodical thinker whose judgments were repeatedly borne out.

Another point worth noting is that he hired a manager named Lee Sun-Geun.

One defining feature of Samsung Group today is that the company is run by professional managers. In other words, it places its trust in management specialists and entrusts them with major responsibilities. The management principle “Do not employ those you doubt; do not doubt those you employ” had already been established at that time.

Looking back over Samsung Group from its beginnings to the present, one can identify the following

management principles:

Enter a market only after meticulous preliminary research, compete on quality, and give employees ample decision-making authority.

Over all these years, whenever Samsung has entered a new field, it has never departed from these principles.

This was true when Cheil Industries was established, when Samsung Electronics was established, and when Samsung entered the semiconductor industry that would become its core. Lee Byung-chull entered each market only after rigorous research, pursued a high-quality strategy, brought in the managers and technical specialists the business required, and empowered them to take the lead within the company.

Retired Samsung Electronics chairman Kang Jin-ku and Samsung Semiconductor chairman Kim Kwang-ho were both examples of this approach. Samsung Group has now established a professional management system, and its documents contain no line for the group's owner, chairman Lee Kun-hee, to sign off on decisions. Even in the days of Samsung Company, Lee Byung-chull entrusted every matter other than a handful of extremely important decisions to manager Lee Sun-Geun. Yet trusting others and entrusting them with responsibility did not mean that he took no interest in the company himself.

When it came to concentrating on their work, both Chung Ju-yung and Lee Byung-chull were entrepreneurs without equal.

According to the recollections of Lee Byung-chull's eldest son, Lee Maeng-hee, the machinery at the Star Noodles factory ran 24 hours a day, and business was thriving. Yet Lee Byung-chull's entire family lived in a small room at the factory, while he poured all of the income back into its operations. Lee Byung-chull even slept in cardboard boxes inside the factory. Given what Samsung has become today, the hardships of that period are almost impossible to imagine.

As a reward for such hard work, Lee Byung-chull repaid the outstanding 10,000 yen on the Samsung Company building in less than two years.

Samsung Company was growing rapidly, and Lee Byung-chull began searching for a new investment. With his keen insight, he studied which industries had the brightest prospects.

At the time, Daegu had eight breweries: four operated by Japanese owners and four by Korean owners. The Japanese breweries produced sake, while the Korean breweries produced rice wine or medicinal wine. Lee Byung-chull considered producing sake.

At the time, a Japanese man named Munesue was looking to sell the Chosun

Brewery, which he operated. Chosun Brewery was then the leading brewery in Daegu, with annual production of 7,000 seok (a traditional Korean grain measure),[^FB-F012] but it was being put up for sale because of problems within its leadership. Private brewing was prohibited at the time, and the Japanese authorities determined how many breweries could operate. Lee Byung-chull believed that acquiring Chosun Brewery would be profitable.

Because of the Second Sino-Japanese War, every industry was stagnating. Brewing was perhaps the only industry in which anything produced was certain to find a market. Essential commodities such as petroleum, calcium carbide, and rice were all centrally controlled by the Japanese. If one could obtain authorization to operate a brewery, however, alcohol production would remain free from those controls.

Lee Byung-chull acquired Chosun Brewery for 120,000 won.[^TN-CH04-004] He appointed Lee Jae-so as president of the brewery, Lee Chang-eop as general manager, and Kim Jae-myeong as plant manager, entrusting them with full authority. A professional management system had already been established by then.

Lee Byung-chull's investment in brewing proved highly successful. After he acquired Chosun Brewery, its annual production rose sharply from 7,000 seok to 10,000 seok. On June 3, 1941, Samsung Company was registered as “Samsung Sanghoe Trading Company.” This marked its progression from a sole proprietorship toward the form of a modern enterprise.

While Lee Byung-chull was running Samsung Company, keeping its noodle-making machines turning, and achieving great success through the brewery, Chung Ju-yung was roaming the streets of Seoul amid the whirl of traffic. He was searching for a new business opportunity.

It was then that he happened to meet Lee Eul-hak, a former customer of the rice store. Lee Eul-hak worked at Gyeongseong Auto Repair Shop. He told Chung Ju-yung that Ado Service Garage in Ahyeon-dong[^FB-F013] was for sale and suggested that he give it a try. At the time, wealthy people drove American-made sedans, while charcoal-powered cars had only just begun to appear on the streets of Seoul. After several days of careful deliberation, Chung Ju-yung purchased Ado Service Garage. It cost 5,000 yen, of which he borrowed 4,500 yen: 3,000 yen from Oh Yun-geun, a former customer of the rice store, and 1,500 yen from a friend. He could contribute only 500 yen of his own—one-tenth of the total.

Chung Ju-yung wrote in his autobiography that he knew nothing about automobiles at the time, but this was not entirely true. What he did not know was merely how automobiles were constructed; he understood very well that humanity had entered the automobile age.

Chung Ju-yung began operating Ado Service Garage in 1941. Because Korea was then under Japanese colonial rule, the number of automobiles cannot be determined, but partial figures indicate that Korea had 4,331 vehicles in 1931. By the time of Korea's liberation in 1945, private cars, trains, and buses[^TN-CH04-005] together numbered 7,386, of which 70%–80% were in Seoul.

Taxis appeared in Korea in 1912, but the name “taxi” did not become widely familiar to the public until the mid-1920s. By the time of Korea's liberation in 1945, Seoul had 54 taxi companies operating a total of 949 taxis.

Buses also became a major form of transportation in the 1930s. When Chung Ju-yung opened Ado Service Garage, Seoul had at least 4,000 automobiles. Seeing those 4,000 automobiles crowding Seoul's streets, he sensed that the automobile age had arrived. When he worked as a deliveryman at Bokheung Rice Store, he made deliveries by bicycle. Having grown up in the countryside, he had transported rice by handcart. After arriving in Seoul, he stayed up late learning to ride a bicycle. Most people could sense the world's progression from handcarts to bicycles, from bicycles to motorcycles, and from motorcycles to automobiles. Otherwise, Chung Ju-yung would not have borrowed 4,500 yen—90% of the purchase price—to acquire an auto repair shop.

Chung Ju-yung progressed from making deliveries by bicycle to running an auto repair shop and finally founding Hyundai Motor Group. In this respect, his path closely resembled that of Soichiro Honda, founder of Honda Motor Co., Ltd. and a figure hailed as one of the three great legends of Japanese business. Honda, too, worked as a mechanic in a bicycle repair shop when he was young. In 1922, at the age of 16, he went to Tokyo to learn automobile repair. At 22, he opened his own auto repair shop, which later developed into a major enterprise producing Honda motorcycles and automobiles. The paths taken by these two men were remarkably similar, reflecting the trend of the times. The automobile age followed the bicycle age. Japan had already demonstrated this transition with the emergence of figures such as Soichiro Honda.

Chung Ju-yung's auto repair shop prospered at first. A major fire, however, destroyed two trucks being repaired for the Nippon Nitrogen Fertilizer plant, a privately owned truck that had fallen

into the Imjin River, an Oldsmobile belonging to the family of Yun Deok-yeong, a member of the social elite, and two other trucks.

The fire began when an employee who wanted to heat water to wash his hands poured thinner onto the flames. The vehicles awaiting repair and the entire shop were reduced to ashes. It was truly a stroke of terrible luck.

Most people, after burning up a customer's rare foreign automobile, would have lost all hope and never recovered.

Chung Ju-yung became penniless overnight. He returned to the elderly Oh Yun-geun, who had already lent him 3,000 yen, and borrowed another 3,500 yen. Chung Ju-yung's boldness in asking for another loan is astonishing, and it is just as surprising that the elderly Oh Yun-geun was willing to lend him money again. That was because he trusted Chung Ju-yung. Chung Ju-yung was a man of honesty and trustworthiness. Even after becoming a business titan, he continued to emphasize the importance of trustworthiness.

Chung Ju-yung moved the shop to Sinseol-dong and resumed auto-repair work with 50 employees. Business at the Sinseol-dong shop prospered, and within three years he had not only repaid all his debts but also accumulated considerable wealth. His misfortunes, however, were not yet over.

After Japan launched the Pacific War, Chung Ju-yung's auto repair shop was forcibly absorbed by another company. Having ceased operating the repair shop, he purchased three trucks and used them to haul ore to a gold mine. He eventually sold the trucks to the mine for more than 50,000 yen and left. Three months later, Japan was defeated and Korea was liberated.

# Samsung Moolsan Trading Company and Hyundai Auto Service

Before embarking on any venture, Lee Byung-chull always conducted an exceptionally meticulous and detailed market analysis before taking

action. Chung Ju-yung, by contrast, would act first and then fight to make the venture succeed.

If Lee Byung-chull planned first and then acted, Chung Ju-yung acted first and then thought.

With Korea's liberation in 1945, Korean-owned enterprises also gained an opportunity to develop.

Under Japanese colonial rule, Korea had only a handful of Korean-owned enterprises, among them Kim Yeon-su's Kyungsung Spinning Company, Park Heung-sik's Hwashin Department Store, and Lee Dong-man's Daedong Mining.

Many entrepreneurs began launching businesses. Park Yong-hak, a fellow native of Chung Ju-yung's hometown and later associated with today's Daenong Group,[^FB-F014] began by operating a soy-sauce factory in his hometown of Tongcheon, Gangwon Province, before moving to Seoul after liberation. Lee Yang-gu came to Seoul from Hamhung,[^FB-F015] where he began selling snacks by bicycle; in 1948, he established Tongyang Confectionery Corporation in Seosomun-dong, the predecessor of today's Orion-Tongyang Group. Choi Seong-mo, founder of Shindonga Group, began with a rice-processing plant in Sariwon[^FB-F016] and later opened a rubber factory in Seoul. Lee Won-man, founder of Kolon Group, operated a hat factory in Japan and returned home with the enormous sum of 1.8 million won to establish a synthetic-fiber company called Kyungbuk Enterprise.

At about the same time, SsangYong Group founder Kim Seong-gon moved to Seoul. He sold his Chosun Textile Company in Anyang and established Kumsung Textile in Seoul, laying the foundations of today's SsangYong Group.

Koo In-hwoi, founder of LG Group, likewise established Chosun Heungeopsa in Busan in 1945. Its main business was exporting charcoal to Tsushima Island in Japan, but after storms brought the venture to ruin, he turned to the chemical industry.

In other words, Korea's liberation brought a proliferation of homegrown Korean enterprises, which laid the foundations for the country's major corporations today.

As soon as Korea was liberated in 1945, Lee Byung-chull came to Seoul. Samsung Company and Chosun Brewery in Daegu were flourishing, but he came to Seoul with ambitions to build a larger business there. He moved his family to Seoul that May. Leaving behind the brewery and Samsung Company he had operated in Daegu, he settled with his family in a traditional Korean house at 125 Hyehwa-dong. The property now houses the offices of Myung Films, which made *Joint Security Area*.

There, Lee Byung-chull spent a year thinking deeply about his future business and ultimately decided to enter trade.

Korea was suffering from an acute shortage of goods, and he judged that trade offered tremendous opportunity.

In November 1948, he rented a 200-square-meter building near the Yeongbo Building in Jongno 2-ga—where the YMCA now stands—and put up a sign reading “Samsung Moolsan Trading Company.”[^TN-CH05-001]

The Samsung brand is now known throughout the world, yet when the business first opened, even its premises were rented.

The company used *gongsa* in its name because Korean merchants at the time dealt mainly with Chinese merchants from Macao and Hong Kong, and the term was readily understandable to them.

Samsung Moolsan Trading Company began with a joint-stock structure. Lee Byung-chull supplied 75 percent of the capital, while Cho Hong-je, who later founded Hyosung Group; Kim Saeng-gi, who later founded Yungjin Pharmaceutical; Lee O-seok; Moon Cheol-ho; Kim Il-ok; and others jointly supplied the remaining 25 percent. Lee served as president, Cho as senior managing director, and Kim Saeng-gi as managing director. Lee did not finance the company alone; joint investment was part of his management strategy.

Lee Byung-chull's principles for running the company at the time were broadly as follows. They were the product of the year he had spent reflecting after moving to Seoul.

First, every employee, regardless of the amount, could invest for an equity stake, and profits would ultimately be distributed fairly in proportion to each person's investment.

Second, everyone, from the president to ordinary employees, should perform their duties in a spirit of mutual respect, and the company should establish a clear system of rewards and penalties based on ability.

Third, the company should safeguard its employees' livelihoods and, without impeding operations, do everything possible to foster a family-like company atmosphere.

Lee Byung-chull gave employees a 25 percent stake so that every investor would feel that the company's interests were closely bound to their own and would strive to advance the business.

Joint-stock companies of this kind are common today, and some companies even give every employee

shares, but at the time this management approach was exceptionally bold and novel.

Every company employee could invest for an equity stake, which then served as the basis for profit distribution. Because they owned shares, employees would regard the company as their own and work hard to contribute to its growth.

In return, Lee Byung-chull did everything he could to provide employees with the best possible living conditions.

Even today, Samsung Group is known for providing its employees with excellent benefits.

That tradition may be said to have begun at this time.

Lee Byung-chull's conviction that employees must be treated well also arose from witnessing the 1946 Daegu uprising after liberation. He attributed the uprising to the extreme hardship of people's lives.

He concluded that a person who made money solely for his own enjoyment was merely a merchant, whereas an entrepreneur made money and shared it with others. In Lee's view, in other words, those who made money should treat the people who worked with and under them well. Beyond that, they should benefit the nation.

This later became one of Samsung Group's corporate mottos: “Contribute to the National Prosperity.”

Samsung Moolsan Trading Company began as a trading business. Trade at the time worked as follows: cargo ships from Hong Kong and Macao would dock in Busan or Incheon; Korean merchants bought the goods they carried and sold them on the domestic market, or sold domestic products to the ships' operators for a profit. It was called trade, but in substance it was little more than buying and reselling at the level of a small shop.

Lee Byung-chull did not operate that way. Cho Hong-je, senior managing director of Samsung Moolsan Trading Company, shipped 15,000 kilograms of dried squid directly to Hong Kong. There he entrusted the squid to Korean expatriates to sell and bought 50 bales of cotton yarn on credit. In other words, he used the squid as collateral to purchase the cotton yarn on credit. He then used the same method to buy another 50 bales of cotton yarn on credit from the trading partner Channel Sanghoe.[^TN-CH05-002] Lee had thus begun engaging in trade in the true sense. The cotton yarn Cho brought back sold in Korea for twice its purchase price and could not keep up with demand.

Lee Byung-chull's Samsung Moolsan Trading Company then made a formal entry into international trade. It imported daily necessities such as sugar, cotton yarn, sewing machines, medical supplies, sheet metal, and fertilizer from Hong Kong, Macao, Singapore, and other parts of Southeast Asia, while exporting dried squid and other seafood. Because goods were scarce at the time, trade was extremely brisk.

Lee Byung-chull's business grew rapidly on the strength of his long-term market research and analysis of trading activity. As the company began handling raw materials such as steel, its range expanded to several hundred products and its trading partners came to include developed countries such as the United States.

The imported products were merely small items for everyday life, yet demand for them in Korea was extraordinary. This was the result of a careful analysis of market needs at the time.

In 1949, only the second year after Lee Byung-chull established Samsung Moolsan Trading Company, it ranked seventh nationwide by trading volume, alongside such major trading companies as Cheonwoo-sa, Dong-A Trading Company, and Daehan Mulsan. After only a year and a half, it had taken first place domestically and laid a solid foundation. By March 1950, one year after the company was established, its income had reached 120 million won. But its good fortune was short-lived: the Korean War broke out. With the onset of war, Samsung Moolsan Trading Company's stocks of sugar, cotton yarn, traditional medicine, fuel, and other goods went up in flames. Lee lost everything and was left penniless.

In April 1946, Chung Ju-yung, his brother-in-law Kim Yeong-ju, and his friends Choi Gi-ho and Oh In-bo established Hyundai Auto Service at the site of today's Myeongbo Theater on Chungmuro in Seoul.[^TN-CH05-003]

In 1939, Kyungil Rice Store closed because of Japan's wartime food-rationing system. Chung Ju-yung then began operating a vehicle repair shop, Ado Service Garage, and gained some experience in manufacturing operations. Japanese imperial controls forced it to close in 1943, after which he made a second start in manufacturing. Calling it manufacturing put the matter grandly; in reality, it was an automobile repair shop. But Korea at the time did not yet have the conditions needed to manufacture cars, so the business could still be classified as manufacturing.

What deserves mention here is the birth of the Hyundai brand. The name Hyundai conveyed the idea of “facing the modern age and striving to create the future.”

Hyundai Auto Service initially rebuilt engines for a U.S. military plant and later began modifying old Japanese vehicles. Its work included cutting a 1.5-metric-ton truck through the middle, lengthening it, and converting it into a 2.5-metric-ton truck.

When Chung Ju-yung went to city hall to collect payment for vehicle repairs, his eyes were opened: he saw that while repairing cars brought him an income of only a few hundred won, people in construction were receiving tens of thousands of won.[^TN-CH05-004]

That very day, Chung Ju-yung put up a Hyundai Civil Works Company sign inside Hyundai Auto Service and entered the construction business. It was May 25, 1947.

His partner and friend Oh In-bo and his brother-in-law Kim Yeong-ju strongly tried to dissuade him. They said that he knew nothing about construction and had little chance of success. Chung, however, did not regard construction as an entirely unfamiliar field. He had worked on the construction site when Korea University's main building was erected, and he believed construction did not require especially advanced technical knowledge: once a budget was prepared and a contract signed, making money from it was no different from repairing automobiles.

At the time, U.S. companies held most of Korea's civil-engineering and construction market. Apart from a few large firms, more than three thousand small companies competed fiercely for business.

Chung hired a technician who had taught at an industrial school, assembled a team of more than ten skilled workers, and began taking work from the U.S. military authorities. In its first year, the business signed contracts worth 15.3 million won, giving it a successful start.

Taking advantage of this opportunity, Chung moved Hyundai Civil Works Company, which had shared offices with Hyundai Auto Service, into the Pyonghwa Newspaper building in Gwanghwamun and formally began competing with other firms.

This reveals the difference between the ways Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung approached business.

Before embarking on any venture, Lee Byung-chull always conducted an exceptionally meticulous and detailed market analysis before taking action. Chung Ju-yung, by contrast, would act first and then fight to make the venture succeed.

If Lee Byung-chull planned first and then acted, Chung Ju-yung acted first and then thought.

Today, the two titans, Chung Ju-yung and Lee Byung-chull, have respectively built Hyundai and Samsung into global enterprises, and it is impossible to say which approach is superior.

Chung Ju-yung's entry into construction may seem almost comical: after seeing at a government office that people in construction were collecting far more money than he was, he returned that very day and put up a Hyundai Civil Works Company sign. Yet his intuition told him that the industry had excellent prospects.

When Samsung began a new business, it first drew up a plan, then spent time and effort producing an extremely detailed proposal before taking action. Hyundai, by contrast, first set up the operation and then went out to make money.

In what is now called speed-oriented management—the ability to decide swiftly and move quickly to the next step—Hyundai was ahead of Samsung and larger in scale. In substantive terms, however, including operating results and profit structure, Samsung was stronger, precisely because of the lengthy research and analysis it undertook before making decisions.

This is not a comparison of which company's management philosophy was better. Rather, the difference may be said to reflect what enterprise management required in different periods.

If Hyundai's driving force is what a company needs when fighting its competitors on a smoke-filled battlefield, then building a brand in the consumer market requires Samsung's model: precise analysis of market conditions and strategies derived from that analysis.[^TN-CH05-005]

If workers laboring at a construction site in temperatures of 60 degrees Celsius collapse from heatstroke under the sun, what is needed is a commander's rapid judgment. But when selling televisions, refrigerators, computers, semiconductors, and similar goods, even setting up a factory production line takes considerable time, after which market changes must still be forecast—and circumstances may change at any moment.

Today's Hyundai Group is engaged in a difficult struggle, and it would be dangerous for it to neglect its own corporate spirit.

If Hyundai can once again execute projects as successfully as it did in Vietnam and the Middle East, Hyundai's corporate spirit may once more become a subject of discussion.

# Lessons from a Barber

What Lee Byung-chull admired was the barber's dedication to his craft. What moved him was that same dedication. Although

barbering was a humble occupation, the barber hoped his son would carry on the family trade. It was this devotion that moved Lee Byung-chull.

In it, Lee saw the spirit of Japan. Although Japan had been reduced to ruins in the Second World War, he already sensed that the country would rise again.

Samsung's corporate spirit today closely resembles Japan's professional ethos—what is often called the “craftsman spirit.” This spirit means devoting all one's energy to a single pursuit and striving to reach the highest level in one's field.

In February 1950, Lee Byung-chull visited Japan as part of a group of fifteen people, including Jeon Taek and Seol Gyeong-dong. Japan's economy was just beginning to recover.

At the time, Japan hoped to revive its economy through renewed trade with Korea. Korean businesspeople visited Japan at the invitation of their Japanese counterparts.

Private travel was impossible in those days, and Lee Byung-chull was no exception. President Syngman Rhee bitterly hated Japan and opposed all commercial dealings with it.

Yet the times were quietly changing. After leaving his student life at Waseda University and returning to Korea, Lee set foot on Japanese soil again for the first time. The defeated country's economy was in dire straits. From Haneda Airport to central Tokyo, the route was lined with dilapidated wooden shacks and devoid of large buildings. Even Kawasaki Heavy Industries, which had manufactured various weapons during the war, had been bombed into an empty shell of ruins. By launching a misguided war, the country had reduced itself to rubble.

For three months, the Korean economic delegation traveled tirelessly throughout Japan. In that short time, Lee came to feel that trade with Japan should be reestablished as a practical necessity. Near the end of the visit, he took a walk along a street behind Akasaka in Tokyo. Today, Akasaka is one of Tokyo's best-known commercial districts, with clubs, bars, and restaurants glowing in neon at night, a city that never sleeps. At the time, however, it was little more than a desolate street.

“One evening, while walking along a road without even a streetlight, I entered a barbershop. A sign reading ‘Morita’ hung outside the shabby premises. The owner looked to be around forty, and I struck up a conversation with him.

“‘How long have you been a barber?’

“‘I am the third generation. Our family has run this shop for about sixty years. I hope my child will carry on the work...’

“It was only casual conversation, yet what he said was far from ordinary. As a citizen of a defeated nation, the barber might naturally have suffered a profound sense of frustration. Instead, he was striving ceaselessly to improve himself. I was deeply struck by his dedication to his work.”

This passage comes from Lee Byung-chull's *Ho-Am Autobiography*.

As a successful entrepreneur, Lee had countless stories he could have told. What did he mean by including his encounter with this barber in his autobiography?

If Lee visited the barbershop in 1950, he was forty-one years old. This was before the establishment of Cheil Sugar and his wool-textile, trading, electronics, and semiconductor businesses—in other words, while he was still preparing for his entrepreneurial career.

What Lee Byung-chull admired was the barber's dedication to his craft. What moved him was that same dedication. Although barbering was a humble occupation, the barber hoped his son would carry on the family trade. It was this devotion that moved Lee Byung-chull.

In it, Lee saw the spirit of Japan. Although Japan had been reduced to ruins in the Second World War, he already sensed that the country would rise again.

Samsung's corporate spirit today closely resembles Japan's professional ethos—what is often called the “craftsman spirit.” This spirit means devoting all one's energy to a single pursuit and striving to reach the highest level in one's field.

Whether the story is true or not, the barber is said to have received a gift while Lee was still alive: a complete set of Japanese-made barbering tools. It was a gift very much in Lee Byung-chull's style.

The lesson Lee Byung-chull drew from the barber named Morita in Akasaka was precisely Japan's “craftsman spirit”—the force that would enable Japan to rise again.

This is indeed so. The Japanese are a people who take their work extremely seriously. During the Second World War, for example, the Japanese Army needed to supply rice cakes to the one million troops of the Kwantung Army stationed in Manchuria, China.

While searching within Japan for a suitable confectioner, the army learned of a rice-cake shop beside Ise Jingu that made excellent cakes. It placed an order for one million rice cakes a week, but the shop refused.

Supplying one million cakes every week would have made the shop rich almost overnight, provided it could deliver them on time, yet it declined the order. Its reason was that in producing a million cakes, it could not guarantee that every one would meet its quality standards. The shop lacked confidence that every cake in such a vast production run would pass inspection. This demonstrates the Japanese people's

precision and sense of responsibility in their work.

Nor is that all. Century-old businesses can be found everywhere in Japan. Bunmeido in Nagasaki has produced the same kind of cake for a hundred years. The Shindoya inn in Nara opened in the sixteenth century and has operated for more than four hundred years. Kongō Gumi, an Osaka company specializing in temple construction, was founded in 587 and, after forty generations and some fourteen hundred years, continues to build temples. To my knowledge, it is the world's oldest company. In Tokyo's Ginza, meanwhile, one finds Ginza department stores—century-old establishments gathering merchandise from throughout the country.

Mitsui and Mitsubishi, the groups that represent Japan today, likewise have long histories. Mitsui Group was founded in 1876 and often takes pride in its century-long history. Mitsubishi Group was founded by Yataro Iwasaki in 1870 and has a history of more than 130 years.

What has enabled these groups to survive for centuries? In a single phrase: quality first. Generation after generation has devoted painstaking study to producing goods of the highest quality. They have worked ceaselessly to improve it. If even barbershops are passed down through generations, the same is all the more true of other trades. Here lies the driving force behind Japan's conquest of world markets: dedication to one's work.

Travel around Japan and its bakeries, rice-cake shops, boxed-lunch shops, sashimi restaurants, soy-sauce makers, and inns all give the impression of being run by specialists. Every employee of a company is likewise an expert in his or her own field.

Lee Byung-chull saw this, and his intuition was accurate. Japan in the 1950s lay in ruins after its defeat, yet thirty years later, through that dedication to work, it had become the world's second-largest economy. Japanese steel, home appliances, semiconductors, shipbuilding, and automobiles conquered global markets.

For Samsung to travel farther and endure longer, Lee believed it had to uphold the spirit of quality first. From then on, Samsung continued moving forward with quality as its highest goal.

Quality first. To sustain that principle, a company must select talented people and make talent first the foundation of its enterprise. Today, with only a few exceptions, Samsung holds the leading domestic position across Korea.

# A Barley Lawn and Sugar

If Lee Byung-chull won the public's trust through business ethics, Chung Ju-yung built a positive public image through entrepreneurial spirit.

One day in the winter of 1953, the United Nations forces urgently contacted Chung Ju-yung. They wanted him to lay turf at the UN cemetery in Busan. In five days, envoys from the countries that had sent troops to fight in the Korean War were due to visit and pay their respects, but the cemetery was desolate. It was the depths of winter, when a lawn was impossible. What could be done? The UN military turned to Chung precisely because the greater the difficulty, the more fully he displayed his ingenuity.

Chung asked whether any green ground cover would be acceptable. With time running out, the U.S. Eighth Army said yes. Including a fee for the idea, Chung asked for three times the original construction budget. Given the emergency, the Eighth Army readily agreed.

Chung went to barley fields near the Nakdong River, bought the entire crop, and transported it in thirty trucks to the UN cemetery in Busan, where it was planted. The lush green winter barley transformed the cemetery like a new lawn. The foreign envoys who came to pay their respects in midwinter were full of praise.

In 1952, Chung Ju-yung was thirty-eight. General Dwight D. Eisenhower, the president of the United States, was to visit war-torn Korea. A suitable welcome and preparations had to be arranged for the American president's visit in the bitter cold of winter. But Korea was reeling amid the turmoil of war, and U.S. Eighth Army officials were deeply concerned. Even Seoul had been reduced to ruins, with nowhere for Eisenhower to stay.

It was first decided that the president would stay at Unhyeongung Palace in Jongno 3-ga.

But Unhyeongung was a traditional Korean residence, creating problems with its bathrooms and heating. Only fifteen days remained before the president's arrival. The U.S. Eighth Army decided to entrust the work to Chung Ju-yung.

If he finished by the deadline, he would receive double the usual payment; if he failed, he would owe twice that amount as a penalty. Although Chung had never even seen a Western-style toilet, he accepted the job.

The war had left almost nothing fit for use, but Chung led several workers on a “treasure hunt” through secondhand shops in Yongsan.

The shops stood empty, their owners having fled the fighting. In them Chung found a water heater, water pipes, sinks, bathtubs, and Western-style toilets.

With no shopkeepers present, they wrote on the doors what they had taken and where the owners could collect payment when they returned.

Using these salvaged items, they worked day and night and finished on the twelfth day, installing flush toilets and heating equipment.

Three days ahead of schedule, Chung went to collect his payment.

After inspecting the work, the U.S. Eighth Army official showered Chung with praise, repeatedly declaring, “Hyundai is number one!” From then on, all U.S. military construction work was entrusted to him.

It was these small projects that gave Chung the imagination and practical drive to undertake major ones.

The Korean War turned Lee Byung-chull into a beggar.

On the morning the Korean War broke out, Lee rose early as usual at his home in Hyehwa-dong.

When he casually turned on the radio, it repeatedly broadcast shocking news: “Communist forces have crossed the 38th parallel and invaded the South. Our forces are fighting them fiercely.”

It came like a bolt from the blue. He had moved his entire family to Seoul to expand his business only three years earlier. Just as the enterprise was beginning to flourish, this enormous upheaval occurred.

During the day, trucks packed with South Korean soldiers streamed north through the city. Lee and ordinary citizens alike applauded loudly and cheered them on.

At this time, the Ministry of National Defense broadcast assurances that South Korean forces were vigorously holding off the People's Army and that citizens need not worry.

Lee, too, believed that the People's Army would soon be driven back.

But two days later, the government announced that the capital was being moved to Suwon. From that evening, artillery fire echoed through Seoul's streets and alleys, and columns of refugees began moving south.

Lee went to work at his company in Jongno 2-ga and discussed the situa-

tion with his subordinates, but none of them knew what to do.

They agreed to contact one another if difficulties arose and then dispersed.

On the night of June 27, earth-shaking artillery fire continued without pause. Lee, his wife, and their children stayed awake all night, hoping it would stop. Air-raid sirens mingled with the shelling, and for one moment he imagined the guns had fallen silent. He stepped outside anxiously, only to find the power out and the entire world plunged into darkness.

At first light on June 28 came the rumble of wheels. Streetcars carrying People's Army troops sped through Hyehwa-dong with flags flying. The People's Army had captured Seoul.

Only then did Lee realize how naive he had been to trust the government's assurances that the People's Army would soon be repelled. It felt as though the sky had fallen. Dazed, he shut himself inside his home.

On the twenty-ninth, Communist Party members from the People's Committee came to his home in succession to investigate his property and political views. They were followed by repeated inquiries from the internal-security office and other investigative bodies.

The days passed in anxiety. All of Lee's property was confiscated and made public property. The goods in his warehouses and even his automobile were seized.

On September 28, U.S. forces under Douglas MacArthur landed at Incheon and eventually retook Seoul. But China's entry into the war darkened the outlook once again.

It was extremely dangerous to remain in Seoul. Having lost almost everything, Lee took his wife and children south with the mass of refugees in the January 4 Retreat. He reached Daegu, where he had begun his career by founding Samsung Company. Now he returned penniless. Yet someone was waiting for him there: Lee Chang-eop, to whom Lee had entrusted the Daegu brewery and orchard when he moved Samsung to Seoul. Lee Chang-eop had continued managing both properties after his departure. When Lee returned to Daegu, Lee Chang-eop handed over all 300 million won in earnings from them.

After moving to Seoul, Lee had been so occupied with larger business dealings that he had no time to attend to the brewery and orchard in Daegu. For several years, Lee Chang-eop had operated and managed both properties, safeguarding a combined profit of 300 million won for him.

Today, income and expenses might be reported by telephone or email and profits transferred through a bank, but none of that was possible then.

Lee Chang-eop had managed the businesses conscientiously. For Lee Byung-chull, who had just lost all his property in Seoul, the 300 million won he handed over was aid in the hour of greatest need.

Lee, who had never imagined such a sum existed, was deeply moved:

“When you appoint someone, if he has made no mistakes, you should continue to trust him and entrust him with important responsibilities.”

No one benefited more from Lee Byung-chull's management philosophy than Lee himself.

With the 300 million won, Lee prepared to make a comeback. In Donggwang-dong, Busan, where he had taken refuge, he used the money to reopen Samsung Moolsan.

At first, Lee traded in scrap iron. It was wartime, and scrap was everywhere: shell casings, abandoned military vehicles and parts, and machinery from bombed factories. The metal was collected and sold to Japan. The dollars earned were then used to import sugar and fertilizer from Hong Kong. This, too, was a form of trade.

There was no difficulty at all selling sugar and fertilizer, because wartime shortages were acute. Whatever was imported sold out immediately. It was an age when, if one had dollars, any quantity of imports could be sold; without dollars, all one could do was sigh in frustration.

Then Lee received more good news. Payment of 30,000 dollars arrived for cotton yarn that had been sold to Hong Kong before the war. This gave him another excellent opportunity. He used the money to import still more sugar and fertilizer. In only six months, net income exceeded one billion won, and a year later his assets had grown to six billion won. He had accumulated the capital needed to undertake a major enterprise.

He later recalled:

“Although it was the path I had chosen, I kept wondering whether I was truly satisfied with it and whether there was not something more important I should be doing.”

For some reason, in other words, he always felt an emptiness inside.

That emptiness probably came from a question he kept asking himself: although importing sugar and fertilizer produced substantial income, could he truly call himself an entrepre-

neur?

“There is no future in merely importing consumer goods that people need in daily life. Korea's only way forward is to import raw materials the country lacks, process them into a variety of products, and export those products.”

This was the judgment Lee made at the time.

Until then, he had never truly engaged in manufacturing.

He had processed grain and operated a brewery and, in today's terms, had also conducted a real-estate business on the Gimhae Plain. He had become rich overnight and had also suffered failure.

But none of these was the kind of enterprise he envisioned.

Grain processing merely created jobs; it could not be called an enterprise that repaid the nation. It was simply a business that bought grain, processed it, and sold it again. Brewing, too, was no more than a profit-making activity, not a constructive enterprise. He had to turn his attention to manufacturing. We can understand how he felt.

I once interviewed an entrepreneur whose business had been lending money at high interest. By his own account, he earned a great deal and could have lived without financial worries had he continued. Yet for some reason, he never felt entirely honorable. Living by collecting interest from others was not, after all, a noble occupation.

One day, he settled all his moneylending accounts and opened a very large wholesale store for suit fabrics at Cheonggyecheon. Business was reportedly good. He made substantial money, yet still felt that it was not a noble occupation.

Selling suit fabric made by others to consumers for a profit still made him no more than a merchant.

He ultimately concluded that he should provide jobs for the unemployed who wandered the streets, manufacture products for export to earn foreign currency, and benefit the nation and its people. He therefore used all the money he had earned to establish Korea's largest audio-equipment company. As he saw it, he could employ thousands of workers, give them jobs, and manufacture products for sale in the United States. Only then did the emptiness in his heart subside. This is what an enterprise means. At first, a person seeks the greatest possible profit; after earning a great deal, he hopes to make money in a way that benefits society. Lee Byung-chull may have been in just such a frame of mind.

He began preparing to enter manufacturing.

What should he manufacture?

He first began researching and preparing to decide which field of manufacturing to enter. The inquiry narrowed the options to sugar, penicillin, and paper.

At the time, Korea depended entirely on imports for sugar, flour, fiber, woolen textiles, fertilizer, paper, and medicine.

All were urgently needed industries, but Korea possessed neither the technology nor the trained personnel. It was an era when anything that could be produced would sell briskly.

Lee asked Japan's experienced Mitsui Group for assistance in calculating the construction and equipment costs of sugar, penicillin, and paper plants. It was characteristic of Lee never to approach a matter carelessly.

Three months later, the overall blueprint for constructing a sugar refinery arrived. It was followed by production plans for penicillin and construction plans for a paper mill.

Comparison showed that penicillin offered the broadest prospects, but its production technology was also the hardest to master. The same was true of papermaking.

Lee ultimately chose sugar. Demand was rising steadily, yet there was not a single domestic sugar producer.

Lee examined and considered the production plan in every detail, but he also placed great value on his intuition.

This is clear from the following statement:

“A company often has data in hand yet finds it difficult to reach a conclusion. At such times, what is needed is the chief executive's intuition. But intuition grows out of rigorous planning, abundant experience, and comprehensive research. What an executive needs is not merely intuition, but the ability to put it into practice.”

Lee judged that sugar offered the best prospects and decided to enter the industry.

Under the circumstances, sugar promised faster results than either penicillin or paper. Since the country relied entirely on imports, domestic retail prices were three times the import price. Building a factory, however, required enormous capital. Mitsui Group's estimate put the cost at more than 180,000 dollars: 150,000 dollars for purchasing and installing equipment and 30,000 dollars for raw sugar used in trials. Another 20 million won in operating capital was also required. After seeking funds from many sources, Lee finally secured a special government loan of 180,000 dollars to build the plant, while Korea Development Bank financed the remaining 20 million won in operating capital. He then imported equipment and machinery from Mitsui.

In August 1953, after many twists and turns, Cheil Sugar was finally established in Busan. It marked Lee Byung-chull's first formal step into manufacturing.

Things did not proceed smoothly. After Cheil Sugar was founded, the factory began trial operations. The instant the first product emerged, however, everyone's expression changed.

What came out was not sugar, but streams of black liquid.

Everyone's face darkened as well. Then Lee spoke.

“Did you all think we would succeed on the first attempt? If so, why conduct trial operations at all? We might as well have gone straight into production. Success will come only after several trials. Use them to study the machinery and its operation carefully and accumulate experience.”

But three days passed without any sugar being produced. The output remained a pitch-black liquid.

Today, one could telephone Japan and immediately ask what was wrong. At the time, however, making an international call was almost impossible.

Applying for an international call at the telephone office took three days. When the connection was finally made, the sound was so poor that no one could understand what the person at the other end was saying.

Japanese technicians should ordinarily have accompanied the imported machinery. Under the Syngman Rhee government, however, Korea and Japan had no formal diplomatic relations, making it impossible for Japanese nationals to enter Korea.

Everyone searched for a solution, staring at the machinery and thinking intensely. A welder passing by made an observation:

“Why are you putting so much raw material into it?”

At those words, Lee's eyes lit up.

“Try putting in only a little raw material,” he said.

The solution proved remarkably simple. When too much raw material was poured in at once, the machinery could not process it all. Following Lee's instruction, the workers added only a small amount. When they started the machine, it produced excellent sugar.

After more than a week of trials, they finally succeeded on November 5, 1953. Lee was delighted and designated that date as Cheil Sugar's founding day.

Sugar production began, but a new problem arose: there were no suitable bags in which to package it.

Today, plastic bags would naturally be used. Before plastic bags existed, however, sugar had to be packed in white cloth sacks. The greatest problem was that sugar crystals trickled straight through cloth produced in Korea at the time.

Sugar sacks had to breathe without allowing the sugar to escape. Korea did not yet possess the technology to make such material.

Cloth was so scarce that many people cut up discarded U.S. military parachutes to make clothing. If even parachutes were used as fabric, it is hardly surprising that there were no sugar sacks.

It is difficult today to imagine those hardships. Korea began producing cloth on a full scale only after Lee established Cheil Wool Textile in 1956.

In the end, technicians were sent to Japan to learn how to make fabric suitable for sugar sacks. Once the fabric had been produced, however, there were no sewing machines capable of turning it into bags. Korea lacked even the technology to produce the cloth, much less machinery capable of processing it.

Lee's eldest son, Lee Maeng-hee, ultimately found secondhand sewing machines in Japan and, after enormous difficulty, had them flown to Korea on a U.S. military aircraft.

Everything had to be built from nothing. At last all the necessary elements were in place, and Lee began full-scale sugar production.

In that era of acute shortages, demand for sugar was enormous. Only six months after production began, capacity had to be expanded to meet the market's needs.

At first, the plant produced twenty-five metric tons of sugar a day. Six months later, output had risen to

fifty metric tons a day. By 1956, Cheil Sugar's fourth year, daily production reached 150 metric tons, and in 1957 it rose to 200 metric tons.

Sales amounted to 722 million won in the first year and increased to 5.6 billion won by 1958. Net income was naturally also substantial.

The initial operating capital had been 20 million won, yet net profit that year reached 160 million won—eight times the start-up capital.

Lee's enterprise was expanding at tremendous speed. At that point, other sugar companies also began to appear.

Around 1953, when Cheil Sugar was founded, inflation drove prices up by 52.5 percent. Yet for the entire following year, Lee kept the price of one *geun* of sugar at 100 won. Even so, Cheil Sugar's net profit in its first year reached three billion won.[^TN-CH07-001] After production was increased in 1954, revenue in the second year reached eight billion won.

Demand for sugar had reached the point where supply could not keep pace. As demand rose, quality continued to improve until it was fully comparable to imported sugar, while the price remained less than half that of imports.

One day at a board meeting, the directors urged Lee to raise the price of sugar.

“Our research shows that raising the current price by another fifty won would have no negative effect on sales volume. In other words, our profit on each *geun* of sugar would increase from ten won to sixty, and the company's total earnings would rise dramatically.”

Lee interrupted the director.

“If, as you propose, net profit on each *geun* rises sixfold from ten won to sixty, total annual earnings will increase from eight billion to forty-eight billion won. What would be the point of making so much money?”

Lee's response was entirely unexpected.

Accustomed to hearing words of praise, the directors were baffled by his rejection.

After looking at them for a moment, Lee explained his thinking:

“Business has its own ethical principles. From the standpoint of business ethics, we cannot seek such excessive profits. I founded Cheil Sugar and began producing sugar because I wanted to invest the money earned by Samsung Moolsan in manufacturing and make my modest contribution to the national economy. I have not the slightest desire to become a monopoly capitalist or satisfy personal greed. Treat the proposal to raise the price of sugar as though it had never been made.”

Everyone present was filled with respect by Lee's words.

He had not finished.

“Building an enterprise is not a short-term undertaking but a long-term process. The most important thing on Cheil Sugar's long path of development is to put trust first. Winning another person's trust is very difficult; preserving that trust after it has been won is harder still. For an enterprise, trust means the confidence of the Republic of Korea. We must never betray the people's confidence for the sake of corporate profit.”

If Lee Byung-chull won the public's trust through business ethics, Chung Ju-yung built a positive public image through entrepreneurial spirit.

Chung Ju-yung was an entrepreneur who attached tremendous importance to “entrepreneurial spirit.”

What does entrepreneurial spirit mean for an entrepreneur? It is the final fortress, something that must be defended at all costs even if the sky itself collapses.

Lee refused to raise the price because he did not wish to become a monopolist; it was an expression of his character. Monopoly means pursuing maximum profit without restraint. In his view, however, ordinary people's economic circumstances and the practice of business ethics mattered more than personal gain. He was resolved to uphold ethical principles while doing business. Business ethics must be embodied in commerce, and commerce must begin with adherence to those principles. A business must know how to treat consumers.

“In business, we must always emphasize ‘mutual survival and prosperity.’ We should not compete excessively but accommodate and help one another. Our research institutes should lower production costs so that the resulting benefits can ultimately be passed on to consumers.”

This was an instruction Lee frequently gave his subordinates. He stressed that only companies that valued consumers' interests could survive.

The market is like flowing water. Block the current or muddy the water, and the fish will leave. A wise fisherman never dams the stream or clouds it. Only by respecting the market's

laws can one do business well and achieve shared development.

Through these efforts, Cheil Sugar secured more than half of the domestic market soon after its establishment. By 1960, it held a 69 percent share of Korea's sugar market.

By honoring business ethics, Lee Byung-chull led Cheil Sugar to success.

# Fine Fabric and the Goryeong Bridge Project

To complete the project, Chung Ju-yung took on debts greater than the value of the contract. He did so because he honored his commitments. If he lost his credibility, then everything truly would be over.

“This is a trial, not a failure. If I do not regard it as failure, then it can never be failure.”

This was how Chung encouraged himself when the Goryeong Bridge project failed.

Those who readily admit defeat lose everything. But those who grasp the root of a setback, persevere to the end, and wait for an opportunity to rise again will ultimately have hope.

In 1954, with Cheil Sugar firmly established, Lee Byung-chull entered the wool-textile industry.

The wool-textile industry began in Britain and developed rapidly in the United States and Japan.

After Korea's liberation, many gentlemen in Seoul wore suits made from British fabric smuggled through Macao. It was the era of the “Macao gentleman.”

A suit made from British fabric, however, cost the equivalent of three months' salary for a company employee—about 60,000 won.

Lee also wore suits made from pure wool obtained through Macao, including on business trips to Japan. Because he was always dressed in a fine pure-wool suit, waitresses in coffee shops called him “Mr. Pure Wool.” Lee was thus among the first Koreans to wear pure-wool suits.

He observed that textiles sold extremely well in the United States and Japan and felt that the industry would have equally strong prospects if introduced in Korea. He considered entering the field, but textiles required enormous capital and advanced technology.

Britain's world-renowned textile industry had a history of more than four hundred years, during which it had undergone continuous technological innovation.

Korea at the time could do little more than make cotton undershirts. How could it possibly establish a wool-textile industry?

Yet the examples of the United States and Japan made one thing clear: in the near future, Korea would develop market demand like theirs.

Lee calculated that textiles manufactured domestically could be priced at only 20 percent of the cost of imported goods.

At 20 percent of the price of imported woolens and with comparable quality, he believed the products would have excellent prospects once they reached the market.

The problem was that producing goods of such quality required a large, world-class factory. Lee consulted widely to learn about textile mills.

A director of an American company mocked him: “If Korea can produce a decent product under its own power within three years, I will fly through the sky for everyone to see.”

Lee did not retreat. He investigated textile-production technology and the requirements for building a factory in depth.

He ultimately decided to import only manufacturing technology from foreign companies and rely on Korean capabilities for all installation and operations.

The problem was money. Building the factory, purchasing equipment, and recruiting technicians all required astronomical sums.

Lee knew that the government was also deeply interested in establishing a domestic textile mill. With the help of Kang Seong-tae, then minister of commerce and industry, he obtained a one-million-dollar loan from the U.S. Foreign Operations Administration (FOA). Adding 100 million won of his own and 58.3 million won in financing from Korea Development Bank, he finally established Cheil Wool Textile.

The date was September 15, 1954.

A year and a half later, a complete production line was imported from the West German company SPINBAO. The factory was built with only Korean technicians and six technical workers from West Germany.

An incident during the construction of the Cheil Wool Textile plant is worth noting. Even while activity at the construction site was at its height, Lee devoted considerable effort to landscaping the courtyard in front of the female employees' dormitory.

One day, general manager Lee Chang-eop said to him, “Before the factory itself is finished, you are building employee dormitories and devoting so much effort to beautifying the grounds in front of them. What will people say? Installing heating, laying floors in the corridors, and carefully landscaping the courtyard all seem excessively expensive.”

Lee Chang-eop meant that money was being wasted on something of no practical use.

“It is far from enough merely to provide female employees with a place to work. They are all cherished daughters of their parents, and we must consider their health. Dormitory life should improve their cultivation and give them enjoyment. Factory life is monotonous; without care, they may grow weary of their work and even suffer harm to their health. We must therefore make every effort to create a good dormitory environment.”

“Even so, some of the expense is still wasteful,” Lee Chang-eop replied.

“In a poor working environment, people easily lose interest in their jobs and become weary. Efficiency falls, and many leave their positions. Ultimately, the company suffers. Anyone will feel happier and become dedicated to their work only in a pleasant environment.”

“But aren't we still spending a great deal of money needlessly?” Lee Chang-eop persisted.

“We are spending money, but in the long run it will benefit the company. If we can improve the female employees' efficiency, production costs will ultimately fall.”

Lee Byung-chull held firmly to his views. Regardless of the expense, he insisted on building the most modern employee dormitories and making their courtyards pleasant. The first buildings completed in the factory project were three elegant dormitories named “Sincere Heart,” “Kind Heart,” and “Graceful Heart.”

At the time, Cheil Wool Textile offered not only excellent dormitory conditions but also first-rate supporting facilities.

The factory provided employees with a laundry, beauty salon, bathhouse, ironing room, and reading room. When employees' families visited, a dedicated photographer even took commemorative photographs for them. Lee paid such close attention to these details because he believed that employees who produced high-value wool textiles deserved the finest treatment.

Because the accommodations were good and the benefits were considered the best in Korea, crowds of applicants appeared as soon as recruitment notices were placed in newspapers.

Having heard about all this, President Syngman Rhee attended the factory's completion ceremony and personally inscribed the words “Clothe the People.”[^TN-CH08-001]

With every preparation complete, full production began in 1956.

In its first year, Cheil Wool Textile produced enough suit fabric for roughly 500,000 suits. A suit made with Golden Wool fabric cost about 10,000 won, compared with around 60,000 won for fabric from Macao. It was extremely inexpensive, yet its quality was every bit as good.

Korean gentlemen at the time nevertheless continued to regard clothing from Macao as supreme. They remained con-

vinced that domestically produced suit fabric could never compare with imports.

Sales were poor, and Lee began looking for a solution.

To show that domestically produced fine suit fabric was superior to foreign goods, he ran an advertisement declaring, “Golden Wool suit fabric that surpasses imports.”

Lee also deliberately wore suits made from fabric produced at his own woolen mill.

Many government officials and businesspeople he met nevertheless assumed that he was wearing a British pure-wool suit.

“President Lee certainly knows how to choose a suit. That is no ordinary fabric.”

He often heard comments of this kind.

“Thank you. I take that to mean you are praising Golden Wool for matching British quality.”

Smiling, Lee would turn back his cuff to reveal the conspicuous words “Made in Korea,” proving that the fabric came from Cheil Wool Textile.

Yet in a society that favored foreign goods above all else, Golden Wool recorded a loss of 500 million won in its first year.

Korea at the time produced almost no domestic goods, and those that did exist were generally of very poor quality. Until the mid-1960s, people therefore relied mainly on American or Japanese products. President Park Chung Hee ordered schoolchildren to begin chanting “Use Korean goods” from elementary school onward, and the same slogan was often posted on roadside walls. Against this social background, domestically produced fabric inevitably faced difficult sales conditions.

Lee worked vigorously both to improve product quality and to promote it. In January 1958, the government prohibited imports of fine wool yarn.

When determining which items to restrict, officials found that six million pounds of fine wool yarn were being imported each year, causing a major outflow of foreign currency. Moreover, the yarn produced by Cheil Wool Textile was fully comparable in quality to imports.

This policy was, from another angle, an acknowledgment that Cheil Wool Textile's quality matched imported goods. It was also a protective measure intended to help the company's products meet future domestic demand. Such support had been won through persistent effort.

Although sales were disappointing at first, word gradually spread that Cheil Wool Textile's products were as good as imports, and the number of buyers steadily grew.

Sales became so strong that counterfeit products appeared on the market.

As Cheil Wool Textile prospered, new wool-textile companies such as Kyungnam Wool Textile, Korea Wool Textile, and Daehan Wool Textile sprang up one after another.

By then, however, Cheil Wool Textile already held 70 percent of the market.

To achieve complete self-sufficiency, the government banned imports of foreign wool textiles.

Korea's textile industry, which had begun with Cheil Wool Textile, developed rapidly. Its output rose thirteenfold, from 14.9 billion won in 1960 to 197.5 billion won in 1970. The industry's 247,191 workers accounted for 24 percent of all manufacturing employment.

Thirty years later, in 1991, total production reached 14.5355 trillion won—one thousand times the initial level.

Through the success of Cheil Sugar and Cheil Wool Textile, Lee Byung-chull came to account for 4 percent of the nation's total tax revenue and became Korea's richest man.

Cheil Wool Textile ultimately drove foreign fabrics from the Korean market and brought new vitality to people's lives. It saved Korea 2.5 million dollars in foreign currency each year.

While Lee Byung-chull's business was advancing from success to success, Chung Ju-yung was engaged in a bitter struggle.

After suffering enormous losses on the Goryeong Bridge project, a government minting bureau contract begun in April 1954, Hyundai Group faced a crisis of survival.

Goryeong Bridge connected Daegu and Geochang.[^TN-CH08-002] After the war, only its piers remained; everything else had sunk beneath the water. The government urgently needed the bridge rebuilt for military purposes.

It was the largest government project of the time. The total contract was worth 545.7 billion won[^TN-CH08-003] and had to be completed within twenty-four months. Chung lost more than 65 million won on the project.

Cheil Sugar's operating capital the previous year had been 20 million won, which meant

that Chung's loss amounted to more than three times that figure.

Because it was Korea's largest civil-engineering project at the time, Chung had approached it with great expectations. It was nominally a restoration project, but in practice it was more difficult than building a new bridge. In winter, the water receded and exposed white sand; in summer, the Nakdong River suddenly became deep.

Chung's equipment consisted of only one crane, one mixer, and one compressor. The machinery was primitive, and most of the work had to be done by hand.

The workers built one reinforced-concrete pier and then another in the same fashion. Unexpectedly, torrential rain arrived, the river rose sharply, and the piers were swept away.

In the end, Chung and Hyundai Construction failed to erect even a single pier, while prices rose 120-fold amid staggering inflation. Funds became desperately scarce.

A contract had already been signed, and because the work was not finished on time, no payment could be collected. Meanwhile, the workers demanded their wages.

Chung sold his younger brother Chung Soon-yung's twenty-square-meter property in Samseon-dong and his brother-in-law's twenty-square-meter property in Donam-dong to finance the project. Even this was insufficient, so he sold his own automobile repair shop and borrowed money at monthly interest of 18 percent to pay the workers. In all, he raised 100 million won.

Goryeong Bridge was finally completed two months later than the original agreement required.

Chung received the full contract payment, yet was left with even greater debts. The project was finished, but he was so exhausted that he no longer had the strength even to clear the site.

Creditors pursued him relentlessly, while others in the industry looked upon him with contempt.

People said that Chung Ju-yung, who had not even graduated from elementary school, knew nothing about inflation and was now finished.

To complete the project, Chung took on debts greater than the value of the contract. He did so because he honored his commitments. If he lost his credibility, then everything truly would be over.

“This is a trial, not a failure. If I do not regard it as failure, then it can never be failure.”

This was how Chung encouraged himself after the failure of the Goryeong Bridge project.

Those who readily admit defeat lose everything. But those who grasp the root of a setback, persevere to the end, and wait for an opportunity to rise again will ultimately have hope.

Chung was fond of a line from the *Caigentan* (*Vegetable Roots Discourse*): “At the moment of success, the sorrow of failure is already being born.” It means that the seeds of failure are planted even while a person is enjoying success. The force that encouraged him never to lose heart, even in failure, was “credibility.”

The following maxim hung prominently inside Hyundai Construction:

“Remain calm and composed; this will make you strong, rational, and wise.”

This was the lesson Chung wrote down after the failure of the Goryeong Bridge project.

Chung began a long struggle of patient endurance. It took him twenty years to repay the 65 million won in debt incurred by the bridge project.

Although Hyundai Construction suffered enormous losses on Goryeong Bridge, the reputation for keeping its word that it established there gave Chung the opportunity two years later, in 1957, to restore the Han River approach bridge—and achieve a brilliant success.

# Acting First and Responding Later

Outside working hours, Chairman Lee Byung-chull rarely met with his subordinates, whereas Chairman Chung Ju-yung often mixed freely with his employees.

Chung said, “I am not a successful entrepreneur, only a wealthy laborer.”

He cared little for outward form and valued practical action instead. In his eyes, employees were not subordinates but children.

Lee Byung-chull, by contrast, attached great importance to Confucian tradition. He believed that superiors and subordinates should strictly observe the proper order between senior and junior.

Chairman Lee once asked Hirotaro Higuchi[^TN-CH09-001] of Asahi Breweries, one of the giants of Japanese business, about the essentials of corporate management.

Asahi Breweries now has sales exceeding one trillion yen and recently displaced Kirin Brewery as the company with Japan's largest beer-market share.

Chairman Higuchi, now retired, told Lee that the crucial task was choosing the right manager—a professional corporate executive. He regarded this as the core of business management.

Lee had, of course, already been doing this before Higuchi shared his insight. Cho Hong-je, whom he appointed vice chairman of Samsung Moolsan, and Lee Chang-eop, who safeguarded 300 million won for him, were examples.

As the business continued to expand, however, Samsung grew beyond the point where one or two managers could oversee everything.

To select talent, Lee conducted the first open recruitment campaign in Korean history.

By coincidence, Samsung and Hyundai began cultivating professional managers at almost the same time: Samsung in 1957 and Hyundai one year later, in 1958.

A distinctive competition began in postwar Korea in 1957. Major national projects were springing up everywhere, and as their number increased, so did demand for employees.

When Lee recruited employees for Samsung, he broke with the prevalent practice of favoring relatives and connections and instead selected people on merit.

His thought was, “I will select Samsung's people myself.” Only talented people could enable a company to grow, and Lee consistently followed the management principle of “talent first.”

Exacting in his selection of people, Lee personally conducted interviews from the first open recruitment onward. He directly oversaw every subsequent Samsung interview as well.

Samsung's first open recruitment examination was held on January 30, 1957, in the auditorium of Seoul National University's College of Commerce in Jongam-dong, Seongbuk District, Seoul. In bitter cold of minus fifteen degrees Celsius, more than five hundred candidates from across the country took the examination while rubbing their frozen hands. The subjects were English, their professional fields, and general knowledge.

Because this was the first selection examination to disregard school background and region of origin, large numbers of confident applicants gathered from throughout the country.

Two weeks after the written examination, interviews were held on the fifth floor of the Bando Hotel, where Samsung Moolsan's headquarters was located. There were two rounds. The first chiefly tested academic and professional knowledge; in the second, Samsung Chairman Lee Byung-chull interviewed the applicants personally.

Lee asked questions such as:

“Are you married?”

“Where is your hometown?”

“How many brothers and sisters do you have?”

All this information was already set out in detail in the applicants' forms, yet Lee asked about it again.

These simple questions had no hidden meaning. Rather, they allowed him to observe how applicants spoke and how they carried themselves when entering and leaving, details that revealed their character.

Lee's ideal applicants were unassuming, presentable, and ordinary-looking.

The results surprised many people. Some who had been asked numerous questions and felt certain they had passed were rejected, while others who had answered only one question emerged with the joy of success.

Those ultimately hired included thirteen graduates of Seoul National University's College of Engineering, seven from its College of Commerce, three from its College of Liberal Arts, and one from its College of Law, together with two graduates each from Yonsei University and Korea University.

Among them were Kyung Joo-hyun, who later became one of Samsung's top executives, and Song Se-chang, who later headed the chairman's secretariat.

After intense competition, twenty-eight of the more than five hundred applicants were hired. Lee first assigned three to Cheil Sugar and two to Samsung Moolsan. The remaining twenty-three were sent to the Cheil Wool Textile factory for a month-long internship, a process designed to let them experience Samsung's corporate environment.

In the spinning, weaving, dyeing, pressing, and “fabric beautification” work-

shops, these new Samsung employees learned entirely unfamiliar machinery and terminology and greatly deepened their understanding of production.

Every day, the new employees learned on the shop floor and submitted reports. This was a way of familiarizing them rapidly with the business.

After completing their internship at Cheil Wool Textile, Lee sent them to Cheil Sugar for three months of additional on-site training.

They learned by loading and unloading sacks in the raw-sugar warehouse and carrying heavy bags of sugar.

The reason was the exceptionally high expectations placed on the company's first openly recruited employees.

Lee frequently offered them personal guidance and advice: “You must create Samsung's future”; “You need to correct that bad temper”; and “You must study harder.”

To these employees, Lee seemed less like a superior than a father.

Lee devoted such effort to employee education from the very first open recruitment because of his “talent first” philosophy. He understood that every talented person could bring tremendous growth to the company.

Samsung has now firmly established open recruitment. At the time, however, many people used connections to place their children at the company, and numerous petitioners approached Lee. Requests from senior government officials were especially difficult to refuse. Lee agreed to them all.

There was one condition: employees hired through open recruitment and those admitted through connections would differ in promotion prospects and salary.

Openly recruited employees received priority in promotions and raises over those hired through connections.

A graduate of a regular university could be promoted to grade three after six months, whereas an employee hired through connections had to wait a year. Their pay also differed. Eventually, fewer and fewer people entered through connections.

This was Lee Byung-chull's distinctive way of managing.

Lee's “talent first” management philosophy was fully inherited by his son, Lee Kun-hee.

Lee Kun-hee often said that one talented person could support two or three hundred thousand people.

The “talent first” philosophy was inseparable from cultivating people. Samsung never begrudged investment in its personnel, as shown by its expenditure on employee education and benefits.

Samsung spent twice as much on educating each employee as Japanese companies, three times as much as American companies, and four times as much as European companies. It could invest so heavily because it regarded developing people as a long-term investment.

Samsung spent so much on training because employees joining companies in developed countries were taught and mentored by experienced colleagues. Korean companies, by contrast, had such short histories that they lacked people able to pass business knowledge on to new employees.

Lee's emphasis on people attracted excellent talent and thereby produced high-quality products.

Lee Byung-chull's philosophy of employing people can be summarized in one sentence:

“Do not employ those you doubt; do not doubt those you employ.”

This was one of Lee's favorite sayings.

In 1982, Samsung established Korea's first comprehensive training center in Yongin, Gyeonggi Province, and began long-term, systematic employee training.

Today, no Korean company can rival Samsung in personnel management, from recruitment through hiring, selection, education, training, retraining, and evaluation.

Samsung invests 80 million won per person each year in talent development,[^TN-CH09-002] spending a total of 160 billion won to give more than two thousand employees opportunities to study throughout the world. This shows the importance it places on people. Lee spared no effort in cultivating and trusting talent.

For a time, Lee was deeply interested in collecting and appreciating records. In 1965 and again in 1970, he assigned employees to conduct market research on establishing a record company.

The reports submitted by the chairman's secretariat, however, reached a negative conclu-

sion: the research indicated that a record company would not generate much profit.

Lee was deeply disappointed. Although intensely proud, he accepted his subordinates' opinion because he fully trusted their judgment.

Hyundai introduced open recruitment in 1958, one year later than Samsung.[^TN-CH09-003]

It reportedly recruited only ten technical and managerial employees at the time. They are now elderly and have all left their positions.

Chung Ju-yung was also extremely careful in selecting people. In his view, talent was resource-poor Korea's only wealth. Both technological innovation and industrial automation depended on the power of capable people.

It was well known that Chung sought talent with the persistence of “three visits to the thatched cottage” and selected people as carefully as if choosing a son-in-law.

Once he had chosen someone, he granted that person the greatest possible authority—and the greatest responsibility.

Representative figures who joined Hyundai Group through open recruitment included Shim Hyun-young, who entered in 1963 and later served as chief executive of Incheon Iron and Steel; Lee Myung-bak, who joined Hyundai Construction in 1965; Eum Yong-gi, who joined in 1966 and became president of Hyundai Wood Industries; Park Se-yong, who joined in 1967 and later served as chief executive of Incheon Iron and Steel and Hyundai Merchant Marine; Kim Yoon-kyu, chairman of Hyundai Asan, who was active in inter-Korean cooperation; Lee Byeong-gyu, president of Hyundai Department Store; and Kim Jae-su, vice president of the Corporate Restructuring Headquarters.

These openly recruited Hyundai Group employees did not call Chung “Chairman.” They called him “Father.”

This global enterprise with more than two hundred thousand employees was like a single family, all using the same form of address for its patriarch.

Chung consistently followed the principle that theirs was “not an adversarial relationship, but the affection of people living under the same roof.” He treated every employee not as the owner of a company but as the elder brother of a large family. He wrestled with employees and drank with them through the night, demonstrations of his familial closeness to them. Having performed heavy manual labor himself, he understood their hardships.

In this respect, Hyundai and Samsung were entirely different.

Samsung Group board meetings chaired by Lee Byung-chull were once called “imperial audiences.”

In other words, if Hyundai was pervaded by the atmosphere of father and son, Samsung reflected the relationship between an emperor and his ministers.

Chairman Lee was a man of few words. Even when someone performed exceptionally well, he would say no more than “Not bad.” Chairman Chung adopted a more direct and active approach.

In 1995, when Hyundai Group launched Hyundai Satellite Television, Chung hosted all its employees at a dinner in the Yeongbin Hall in Seongbuk District.

He gave them one instruction:

“Work hard, everyone, and make lots of money!”

As an entrepreneur, he expressed his thinking with complete directness.

For an entrepreneur, creating the greatest possible profit is the ultimate goal, and he stated this proposition without qualification.

Chairman Chung had a distinctive way of criticizing subordinates.

If the subordinate was a managing director, Chung would suddenly address him as a director or department head. At first, the subordinate might think he had simply made a mistake. After a while, however, it became clear that this was a criticism of inadequate ability.

It meant: “Although you hold the position of managing director, your creativity is only at the level of a director or department head.”

What Chairman Chung disliked most was an employee who made a mistake but refused to admit it. If someone summoned to see him acknowledged the error and promised to do better next time, there would be no serious consequences. But anyone who persisted in making excuses received a fierce reprimand.

Outside working hours, Chairman Lee Byung-chull rarely met with his subordinates, whereas Chairman Chung Ju-yung often mixed freely with his employees.

Chung said, “I am not a successful entrepreneur, only a wealthy laborer.”

He cared little for outward form and valued practical action instead. In his eyes, employees were not subordinates but children. This was closely related to his upbringing as the eldest of six sons and two daughters. He always had to look after his younger siblings, and in practice he helped his brothers enter Hyundai Group or gave them companies to manage.

Lee Byung-chull, by contrast, was the youngest child in his family and grew up surrounded by affection. He also attached great importance to Confucian tradition and believed that superiors and subordinates should strictly observe the proper order between senior and junior.

During President Park Chung Hee's authoritarian rule, Lee became known for refusing to pay the president a New Year's visit. His reason was that he was seven years older than Park. In other words, although Park held the exalted office of president, he was seven years Lee's junior, so Lee could not go to offer New Year's greetings. One might speculate that he no longer trusted Park after the Korea Fertilizer incident, but the more important reason appears to have been the deeply Confucian outlook in Lee's mind.

Chung Ju-yung did not care about outward form. At government offices, he treated officials younger than himself with the utmost respect, even if they were mere clerks. He was well known for this.

This can be understood as an entrepreneur's wish to avoid unnecessary harm to his business, but also as Chung showing others the respect their positions deserved because of his own experience.

Chung strongly disliked the phrase “leading people in their work.” Although he owned the company, he saw colleagues as people working together. Paying their wages did not entitle him to say that he led them.

He often visited the homes of rank-and-file workers and personally observed their lives in rented rooms. Chung himself had once eaten only two meals a day and gone hungry at night with nothing but thin porridge. He had performed heavy manual labor at Incheon docks, the Korea University construction site, the Toyoda Malt-Sugar Factory, and railway works. He therefore understood workers' hardships better than anyone.

Lee Byung-chull was likewise an entrepreneur who devoted exceptional care to his employees' lives.

Today, Samsung is a company without a labor union.

In private conversations, Samsung employees express a high degree of job satisfaction and regard it as an excellent company.

Chairman Lee, however, never visited rank-and-file workers' homes, ate with them, or talked with them. This was closely connected with his personality.

Chairman Lee Kun-hee disliked appearing before crowds, and Chairman Lee Byung-chull likewise strongly disliked speaking before large groups.

Chairman Chung, by contrast, could stand before several thousand employees and sing his signature song, *Really*.

Chung liked simplicity and candor in employees because these qualities reflected his own character.

When he was younger, he enjoyed wrestling and playing volleyball with employees and never missed an orientation for new hires. He once even broke a rib while wrestling with younger workers.

In his eyes, valuing employees was the best way to produce competitive goods.

Lee Byung-chull was an entrepreneur who held “talent first” as his highest creed and had the courage to put his ideas into practice.

Both Chung Ju-yung and Lee Byung-chull placed great value on their employees and cared for them deeply; only their methods differed.

# Han River Bridge and the Tokyo Initiative

Although Lee Byung-chull drew inspiration for most of the industries he established from Japan and received extensive technical support from Japanese companies,

in semiconductors, for example, he surpassed many Japanese companies and demonstrated his creativity.

One of the strengths of Japanese companies was likewise that their automobile, shipbuilding, electronics, and semiconductor industries learned technology from advanced Western countries and ultimately surpassed them.

The same was true of Lee. Although he obtained technology from Japan, he ultimately surpassed Japan in fields such as semiconductors. His creativity must therefore be acknowledged.

After suffering enormous losses on the Goryeong Bridge project, Chung Ju-yung continually searched for an opportunity to rise again.

Opportunities always come to those who are prepared. When the Korean government announced bidding for the reconstruction of Han River Bridge, Chung's opportunity finally arrived.

Han River Bridge was Korea's largest postwar project.

The original bridge had been destroyed during the Korean War. Reconstruction was delayed for seven years because the project was so vast and the budget so large. After struggling for years to raise the funds, the government finally invited public bids.

The project had originally been intended for either Chohung Construction or Heunghwa Engineering Office. But a problem arose.

The minister of home affairs wanted to award it to Chohung Construction, while the Ministry of Finance, which held the contracting authority, favored Heunghwa Engineering Office. Neither company would yield. Their confrontation continued for a year beyond the budget schedule without a result, and when compromise proved impossible, the government resorted to competitive bidding.

The major companies submitted their estimates, and an unexpected development occurred.

Heunghwa Engineering Office, then one of the largest construction companies, bid only 1,000 won. That amount was one quarter of the taxi fare from downtown Seoul to the Han River, effectively offering to perform the work for nothing. Because the lowest bid would ordinarily win, the other companies could only smile bitterly and withdraw.

The incident caused a major public reaction. The minister of home affairs, who received the bids, then announced that Heunghwa's 1,000-won estimate showed no sincere intent to compete and could not be accepted even as a donation. It was not a normal commercial act.

The government consequently decided to award the project to the company with the second-lowest bid: Chung Ju-yung's Hyundai Construction, which proposed 230 million won.

This occurred in September 1957.

The struggle between Heunghwa Engineering Office and Chohung Construction ultimately allowed Chung to benefit from their dispute. It was another opportunity for him.

Chung did not intend to repeat the nightmare of Goryeong Bridge. Equipment would be crucial to completing Han River Bridge successfully, so he established a heavy-machinery shop inside the Hyundai automobile repair facility he operated.

Whenever new models became available, the U.S. Eighth Army disposed of its old equipment at low prices.

Hyundai Construction had earned the trust of U.S. forces through the UN cemetery lawn project using barley, so Chung was able to buy their surplus equipment cheaply.

He purchased heavy machinery they no longer used, bought the necessary parts on the market, and refurbished it at Hyundai's heavy-machinery shop.

Sometimes parts were simply collected and assembled into new machines. All this was the work of Chung's brother-in-law Kim Yeong-ju, the “doctor of machinery.”

Hyundai completed the project in only one year. After Goryeong Bridge had taken too long and left the company heavily indebted, this project was finished in the shortest possible time.

Han River Bridge was a project of immense scale, and the final profit reached 40 percent of the contract price.

The project carried Hyundai Construction into the ranks of Korea's five largest construction companies and put it on a powerful upward trajectory. Many new contracts flowed to the company.

In 1958, it undertook work at Osan Air Base. In 1959, it won the project for the U.S. military's No. 1 dock at Incheon Port, described as the largest project since the founding of the Republic of Korea.

By 1960, Hyundai Construction had become the leader among more than one thousand domestic construction companies. Five years after the nightmare of Goryeong Bridge, Hyundai had secured its position in the industry.

In December 1959, Korea produced its first domestically made radio. The first company to manufacture one was GoldStar.

Lee Byung-chull was also busily traveling at the time.

He had just landed at Haneda Airport in Japan when he heard that Korea had produced a radio.

Returning from the United States, Lee had stopped in Tokyo to change planes. He intended to catch a flight back to Seoul, but heavy snow there made landing impossible. With no alternative, he remained in Tokyo for several days, staying at the Imperial Hotel opposite the Imperial Palace.

Located in central Tokyo, the Imperial Hotel ranked then, as it does now, among the city's finest hotels alongside Hotel Okura Tokyo and Hotel New Otani Tokyo.

Lee had studied at Waseda University when he was young and conducted extensive trade with Japan, so he knew the country well.

In 1960, Japan was striving with tremendous energy to join the ranks of the developed nations.

After the Korean War broke out in 1950, Japan earned 6.2 billion dollars by selling supplies to the United Nations.[^TN-CH10-001] This ended the economic distress that had followed its 1945 defeat and produced its best economic conditions in history, the so-called “Jimmu Boom.” It was followed by a succession of prosperous periods, including the “Takamagahara Boom” and the “Iwato Boom.”

Electric washing machines, refrigerators, and black-and-white televisions were subsequently produced, and these advanced household appliances began entering people's homes.

The Japanese called this “the season of flowers after a harsh winter,” and people throughout the country were filled with hope and anticipation for the future.

Against this social background, a Japanese television station prepared a New Year's program for 1960, inviting leading experts to discuss and forecast political, economic, military, and social developments around the world.

The military discussion centered on whether nuclear weapons would be used again. Economically, the focus was on U.S.-Soviet rivalry, especially whether the Soviet economy would overtake that of the United States.

At the time, the U.S. economy was growing at 3 percent a year while the Soviet economy expanded at 7 percent, and many people wondered when Soviet competitiveness would surpass America's.

What most interested Lee in these discussions was the stated intention of ten developed countries—including the United States, Britain, West Germany, France, Italy, Canada, and Australia—to provide long-term, low-interest loans to less-developed countries with growth potential.

Lee was eager to establish a fertilizer plant but had always lacked the funds, so this news was extremely important to him. It later became a driving force behind his successful creation of such a plant.

At the beginning of every year, television stations and newspapers publish economic forecasts and information.

Lee later began visiting Tokyo at the start of each year. From this practice emerged the famous “Tokyo Initiative.”

Samsung Electronics Chairman Kang Jin-ku described the Tokyo Initiative as follows:

1. First, Lee paid particular attention to special programs planned and produced by Japan's major broadcasters, especially those in which prominent Japanese scholars and journalists reviewed the previous year's economic trends and offered forecasts for the new year. He watched them carefully.

2. He then invited respected or particularly insightful economic journalists from leading Japanese publications to lunch or dinner and spoke with them.

Rather than gathering many people at once, he met them individually. He asked for their views on the previous year's economy, their forecasts for the new year, and which industries had strong prospects. He also asked about the essentials of developing an enterprise.

Journalists did not look only at surface figures. They also understood underlying realities and could explain their causes clearly.

3. After learning about the direction of Japan's economic development from journalists, Lee selected industries that interested him and arranged meetings chiefly with university professors and prominent scholars. He did not seek pure theorists, but scholars with insight into economic trends. He likewise asked for their views on industries that had emerged during the previous year and their outlook for the next.

He questioned them in detail about how development could occur and what would drive it.

Because the scholars knew these subjects thoroughly, they could provide clear answers.

4. He then invited prominent entrepreneurs from the business community.

Lee had a wide circle of friends in business and knew many entrepreneurs. When he met them, he asked each one which industries had performed well over the past year and why they had done so.

The entrepreneurs each had their own detailed insights, providing extremely valuable points of reference.

5. After gathering information from different sectors of Japanese society, Lee informed the chairman's secretariat of his ideas for new industries or management systems that Samsung might introduce, so that they could be developed into concrete plans.

When launching a new project in particular, he personally directed and supervised every stage, from the initial preparations through implementation.

Checking and checking again was characteristic of Lee's way of working.

As his return approached, he always visited bookstores, selected useful reference works, and bought them in stacks to take back to Korea.

6. As soon as he returned, Lee organized the projects he had personally identified as promising into a table and gave it to the chairman's secretariat, instructing its staff to investigate whether they could be developed under Korean conditions.

Through this careful and complex process, Lee conceived the fields Samsung might develop. He ultimately selected insurance, papermaking, synthetic fibers, mass media, electronics, heavy industry, and petrochemicals.

In other words, the initial concepts for Samsung's main industries all germinated in Tokyo.

Japanese companies themselves had localized business models from the United States, Britain, Germany, France, and other countries.

Japan's major companies today, such as Mitsui and Mitsubishi, developed around the time of the Meiji Restoration in 1868. They combined the strengths of companies in advanced Western countries with Japan's team-oriented approach to management. Lee understood this well.

He had his own secret when putting this knowledge into practice. An industry that achieved great success in the United States would not necessarily succeed in Korea. But if a successful American industry also succeeded in Japan, Lee believed it had a strong chance of taking root and growing in Korea. Why? Korea and Japan were similar in several respects, including food habits and patterns of daily life. An industry successful in Japan was therefore more likely to succeed in Korea. In this way, these emerging industries achieved enormous success together.

This might lead people to say that Lee succeeded in Korea by imitating Japanese compa-

nies, but that is not what actually happened.

Although Lee drew inspiration for most of the industries he established from Japan and received extensive technical support from Japanese companies, in semiconductors, for example, he surpassed many Japanese companies and demonstrated his creativity.

One of the strengths of Japanese companies was likewise that their automobile, shipbuilding, electronics, and semiconductor industries learned technology from advanced Western countries and ultimately surpassed them.

The same was true of Lee. Although he obtained technology from Japan, he ultimately surpassed Japan in fields such as semiconductors. His creativity must therefore be acknowledged.

Lee Byung-chull's Tokyo Initiative remains a Samsung Group tradition today.

# Two Men in the Face of Power[^TN-CH11-001]

Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung shared one trait: neither yielded to pressure of any kind, and both stood by their

positions. Lee would set out reason after reason to persuade the other party, while Chung was as stubborn as an ox and refused to give an inch.

At seven in the morning on May 16, 1961, Lee got into a car outside Tokyo's Imperial Hotel to go golfing.

Lee went to bed at ten every night and rose at six. At 6:40 he bathed and ate, and at eight he began work. He maintained this habit throughout his life.

That day, too, the incident occurred after he rose and set out.

His Japanese driver, Kuwabara, asked, “Have you heard the news about the military coup in Korea?”

Until that moment, Lee had known nothing about it.

Although the sudden news threw his thoughts into turmoil, he continued on to the golf course.

The May 16 military coup had broken out in Korea. After the coup, the military government arrested eleven business leaders on suspicion of illicit accumulation of wealth.

Based on corporate sales, eleven people ranked first through eleventh were designated illicit profiteers. Lee headed the list. Because he was in Tokyo, however, he temporarily avoided arrest.

Public opinion criticized the military government for ignoring Lee, “Japan's leading illicit profiteer,” while imprisoning people whose offenses were less serious.

The military government sent two people to Japan to urge Lee to return. After learning about the situation in Seoul, he decided to go home.

At ten in the morning on June 24, 1961, Lee issued a statement at Tokyo's Imperial Hotel to correspondents from the Associated Press, United Press International, and other foreign news agencies: “To help eliminate poverty, I intend to donate all my property to the nation. After returning home, I will carry this out gradually as needed and await the government's arrangements.”

By the time he arrived at Seoul's Gimpo Airport, it was pitch-dark and pouring with rain.

Expecting to be taken straight to prison, Lee climbed into a waiting jeep.

The vehicle sped away, but not toward a prison. It headed in the direction of Shinsegae Department Store, taking him to the Metro Hotel in Myeongdong, where he spent the night.

Deeply concerned for his father, Lee's eldest son, Lee Maeng-hee, feared he might be subjected to brutal interrogation. Throughout the night, from a building opposite the Metro Hotel, he watched his father's every movement through binoculars.

The next day, Lee went to the office of the vice chairman of the Supreme Council for National Reconstruction and met Park Chung Hee. The atmosphere in the room was severe. Deputy Chairman Park[^FB-F017] gave an immediate impression of uncompromising integrity.

Although his own position was precarious, Lee carefully observed whether Park was a leader of virtue and standing.

The meeting brought together the two leading figures of the Republic of Korea's business and political worlds.

“When did you return? Is everything all right?”

Unexpectedly, Park addressed Lee in a very gentle tone.

After a brief exchange of greetings, Park switched to a soldier's direct manner and asked, “How do you think the eleven illicit profiteers should be dealt with?”

Lee himself was one of the eleven—and ranked first among them.

“I do not believe these people are guilty of any crime.”

Lee answered Park honestly according to his own convictions.

Park appeared surprised by the response, and his expression darkened.

Lee continued:

“In my own case, if I evaded taxes, then you may call that illicit enrichment. But the current tax law requires companies to pay taxes equal to 120 percent of sales. If taxes are paid that way, no enterprise can avoid bankruptcy. Any company that does survive would be a miracle.”

Even in the military government's lion's den, standing before its supreme leader, Park Chung Hee, Lee showed no fear and set out his argument.

“Every company strives to increase profits and expand its scale. In other words, these men entered the group of eleven because they ran their businesses well. Yet that success has now become illicit enrichment, while those who lacked ability or opportunity and failed to rank among the eleven are treated as innocent. I do not believe this can serve as a standard of judgment.”

In Lee's view, it was absurd to convict people not for tax evasion or embezzlement, but simply by ranking companies and labeling those in first through eleventh place illicit profiteers. He had immediately identified the weakness in the military government's policy.

Park nodded and asked:

“Then what do you think should be done?”

“An entrepreneur's task is to run an enterprise, provide employment, pay taxes to the nation, and expand investment. It takes at least twenty or thirty years to cultivate a successful entrepreneur. I hope you will make full use of businesspeople and treat them well.”

As an entrepreneur, Lee did not retreat before power. A long silence followed in the tense room.

Park said, “If we do that, the people will not accept it.”

Lee replied, “If it benefits national development, then the people must be persuaded. Is that not what politics means?”

The discussion of how entrepreneurs should be handled ended there. Park asked whether he could meet Lee again and where Lee was staying.

Lee replied that he was under house arrest at the Metro Hotel. Park looked astonished and immediately ordered that all the detained entrepreneurs be released the next day.

Lee, too, left the Metro Hotel and regained his freedom several days later, on the twenty-ninth.

In the spring of 1980, Chung Ju-yung was likewise summoned by Chun Doo-hwan's newly established authoritarian military leadership. The atmosphere surrounding the new military authorities was equally severe.

In the name of restructuring the economy and industry, the new military leadership was imposing a unified plan on private enterprises.

The National Security Emergency Measures Committee summoned Hyundai Chairman Chung Ju-yung, his younger brother Chung Se-yung, an executive at Hyundai Motor, and Hyundai Construction president Lee Myung-bak.

When Chung arrived, he found that Daewoo Group Chairman Kim Woo-choong had also been summoned. Calling the two men together meant that the committee intended to consolidate the automobile and heavy-industrial sectors.

The plan, in other words, was to exchange Kim Woo-choong's Daewoo Motor for Hyundai Group's Changwon[^FB-F018] Heavy Industries. In a capitalist country, companies may buy and sell businesses between themselves, but the state does not order corporate restructuring.

Under the committee's plan, Daewoo Motor would be absorbed into Hyundai Motor, while Hyundai's Changwon Heavy Industries would be incorporated into Daewoo Heavy Industries, supposedly increasing the competitiveness of the entire industry.

After explaining the plan, the committee official asked in a tone that assumed Chung's agreement, “Chairman Chung agrees to this proposal, doesn't he?”

Chung answered in a single sentence: “I do not agree.”

What kind of place was the committee? It could arrest anyone who displeased it. Those who lived through the 1980s all remember the atmosphere of terror it created.

How could anyone say “I do not agree” in such a place?

Only Chung Ju-yung dared to say it.

The committee official's expression changed at once. His face seemed to ask Chung: the nation is in a state of emergency and pursuing reform—how dare you speak so casually?

But Chung Ju-yung remained Chung Ju-yung:

“What I have to say may take some time, but please hear me out. From the day I began my business, I built it piece by piece, starting with the selection of each factory site. I have never sold a company because of an economic downturn, and not one of my companies has gone bankrupt.”

Enraged by Chung's composed attitude, the committee official shouted, “What kind of place do you think this is? What are you saying?”

As a supposed concession, the committee allowed Chung to choose between heavy industry and automobiles.

Its official told Chung to choose first and leave the remaining business to Kim Woo-choong, using the offer of priority to pressure him.

Chung firmly refused and continued to resist.

The committee then adopted a divide-and-conquer strategy, taking Chung Ju-yung, Chung Se-yung, and Lee Myung-bak into separate rooms and threatening them.

After an arduous exchange, Chung asked for two weeks to consider the matter, but the committee gave him only one.[^TN-CH11-002]

Several days later, Chung met committee chairman Chun Doo-hwan somewhere in Ahyeon-dong. He was meeting the supreme ruler.

Faced with Chun's lengthy explanation, Chung finally decided to relinquish Changwon Heavy Industries and choose Daewoo Motor.

But the final result was that Changwon Heavy Industries went to Daewoo, while Daewoo Motor also remained with Daewoo Group.

Chung suffered a crushing defeat.

Before a military government, this was the only possible outcome. In the new military leadership's lion's den, Chung managed to say what he wished, but in the end he was one man against many. When revolution comes, entrepreneurs always lose.

Deeply dissatisfied with Chung's attitude, the new military authorities demanded that he resign as chairman of the Federation of Korean Industries (FKI).[^FB-F019]

Chung replied, “The members elected me chairman, so I cannot resign arbitrarily,” and rejected the demand. In the end, the new military authorities could do nothing with him, and he continued as FKI chairman.

Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung shared one trait: neither yielded to pressure of any kind, and both stood by their positions.

Lee would set out reason after reason to persuade the other party, while Chung was as stubborn as an ox and refused to give an inch.

# Building Factories and Exporting Abroad

While American and European businesspeople were visiting Korea to explore investment opportunities, Chung Ju-yung turned his gaze overseas and planned an even greater undertaking.

Beginning with the Han River Bridge project in the mid-1950s, Hyundai Construction grew steadily and, by the 1960s, had become the leader of Korea's construction industry.

Taking on new fields was one of Chung Ju-yung's particular strengths.

“For resource-poor Korea to survive, its only path is exports,” Chung said.

“Looking at the situation now, it seems that, no matter how I consider it, I was wrong to launch a military revolution. Our country is like a thatched hut that has been looted. Fifteen years have passed since liberation, yet why are things still this way? It is because our politicians are corrupt to the core, because they are consumed by self-interest. I do not possess much learning, so I have asked those said to be our country's famous economists how we can make the nation prosperous and strong. They say that ours is fundamentally an agricultural country, with no resources, no technology—nothing. We lack the foundations for economic development. Does our country truly have no hope now?”

After the May 16 military coup, Park Chung Hee spoke these words to Chief Secretary Lee Dong-won, who was then exercising presidential authority.

Although the military government formulated a five-year economic development plan, reduced farmers' high-interest debts to alleviate poverty, and carried out currency reform to rebuild the economic system, the poor economic situation unsettled public sentiment.

The currency reform intended to raise funds ultimately failed to do so and instead aggravated inflation. When President Park Chung Hee announced an “extension of military rule,” the United States government threatened to “suspend aid.” If this continued, the Korean economy was certain to face a grave crisis.

As President Park put it, Korea was struggling even to feed and clothe its people. At the time, Korea's annual per capita income was only 78 dollars.

A revolution had been necessary to enable the people to live well, but even after its success, no path forward was yet in sight.

As Park himself said, he consulted numerous renowned economists and officials, but none of their answers satisfied him. The economy continued to deteriorate.

Before the revolution, GNP grew by 6.1 percent in 1959; the growth rate fell by 2.3 percent in 1960 and by another 2.8 percent in 1961.

Lee Byung-chull and other business leaders, whom the May 16 military government had arrested on suspicion of “illicit profiteering” and later released, founded the Korean Businessmen's Association, the predecessor of the Federation of Korean Industries.

At a meeting held at the Bando Hotel on August 16 that year, business leaders including Choi Tae-seop, president of Korea Glass; Kim Yong-wan, president of Kyungbang[^FB-F020]; Jeon Taek-bu, president of Cheonil-sa; Lee Han-won, president of Daehan Flour Mills; Shim Sang-jun, president of Jedong Shipping; and Kim Sang-mok, president of Pyeongan Textiles unanimously selected Lee Byung-chull, chairman of Samsung Trading, as the association's new chairman.

At a time when the military government was at a loss over Korea's economy, Lee Byung-chull, the newly elected chairman of the Korean Businessmen's Association, resolved to find a direction and a breakthrough for the country's economic development.

At the time, two people proposed paths to national prosperity. One was President Jeon Taek-bu, and the other was Chairman Lee Byung-chull.

Jeon argued that exports were Korea's only way forward, while Lee believed that industrialization was the country's only path. Their views converged on exporting abroad through industrialization.

Under the title “A Path to National Prosperity,” Lee Byung-chull published a five-part series in the *Hankook Ilbo* setting out his vision for economic development.

I believe per capita income must reach 178 dollars. That would be comparable to levels in countries such as the Philippines and Turkey and higher than the Korean government's target.

If Korea could successfully attract 2.1 to 2.3 billion dollars in foreign capital over the next ten to twenty years, the factories built with it would begin generating returns in only three years. If the annual return were 200 million dollars and that money were invested in private enterprises, the country could secure 1.5 to 2 billion dollars in factory-construction funds within ten years.

An investment of 4 billion dollars could build one thousand factories, each capitalized at 4 million dollars. If investment grew at 70 percent of the annual total—that is, by 2.8 billion dollars—per capita income could reach 100 dollars.

If each such factory employed five hundred workers, they would create five hundred thousand jobs and support 2.5 million people. Including related industries and distribution, they could be expected to provide employment for five million people. This would draw rural people into factory work—in other words, it would absorb one-third of the fifteen million farmers into industry. The cultivated area per person could then

rise from the existing 420 pyeong to 630 pyeong, while industrialization would also make fertilizer and farm implements available at lower prices.

As factories paid more in taxes, civil servants' incomes could multiply, thereby eliminating corruption and making society harmonious and healthy.

The core of Lee Byung-chull's thinking was to attract foreign capital and build factories. He envisioned establishing one thousand factories capitalized at 4 million dollars each, creating five million jobs, and raising the country's per capita national income from just 78 dollars to 178 dollars within ten years.

After the articles appeared, Lee Byung-chull and Namgung Ryeon, president of Far East Shipping, went to see President Park Chung Hee almost every day to persuade him.

They stressed, “Industrialization is the only way to escape poverty. If we miss this opportunity and delay economic development, we will never emerge from the shadow of poverty and disorder. Korea has no time to follow the classical pattern of development that Britain followed before the Industrial Revolution. We must boldly reverse the sequence: industrialize first, then develop from large enterprises toward small and medium-sized businesses.”

Lee identified the key industries that should be developed first: cement, fertilizer, electrical goods, steelmaking, synthetic fibers, and oil refining. Park agreed.

Yet building a steel mill would require 130 million dollars, more than the Korean government possessed even if it exhausted all its foreign-exchange reserves. Park looked troubled.

Lee said the problem could be solved by attracting foreign capital; there was no other way. The military government accepted his plan. The question was how—and by whom—the enormous sum of 4 billion dollars needed to develop the key industries could be obtained.

A plan for attracting foreign capital was therefore drawn up:

1. Because it was extremely difficult for individual enterprises to attract capital, delegations should be sent to the United States, Japan, and Europe to seek foreign investment.

2. The government should be able to guarantee private-sector loans.

3. Commercial officers should be posted to overseas consulates to support private-sector economic diplomacy.

4. Private enterprises should be allowed to invite foreign technicians and businesspeople freely to discuss factory construction.

5. When factories were built with foreign capital, domestic financing should also be raised to the greatest extent possible, and

a comprehensive guarantee system should be established.

6. Enterprises relying on foreign capital to import machinery and equipment should be allowed to import the raw materials they required, and their profits should serve as operating funds.

7. Investment should be sought from foreign long-term financial institutions.

8. Laws and agreements such as the Foreign Capital Inducement Promotion Act, the Foreign Exchange Control Act, and a treaty for the avoidance of double taxation should be enacted.

Vice Chairman Park Chung Hee accepted these proposals and enacted them one by one. All that remained was to attract 4 billion dollars in foreign capital. The government assigned the task to an Americas investment delegation headed by Lee Byung-chull and a European delegation led by Lee Jeong-rim, an executive at Korea Cement. In November 1961, Lee Byung-chull departed for the United States, leading the Americas delegation to Washington.

General James Van Fleet, who had commanded the US Eighth Army during the Korean War, gave Lee considerable assistance. Although Van Fleet had already retired, his extensive connections in American business enabled him to introduce Lee to many leading figures in the economy.

Lee explained to them, “Korea will need 2.6 billion dollars over the next ten years. Of that, 1.3 billion must come from foreign capital, 1 billion from the World Bank or export-import banks, and the remaining 300 million from private investors overseas.”

The American businesspeople said that Korea must first create an environment conducive to investment. Their conditions were as follows:

1. To attract foreign capital and industrialize, Korea must first secure transportation and financing and establish “special industrial zones” along the coast or inland. In selecting these areas, it must carefully examine transportation, electricity, access, water resources, labor, factory sites, and other conditions so as to improve its international competitiveness across the board.

2. It must formulate a sound Foreign Capital Inducement Promotion Act and create favorable political, economic, and social conditions for investors.

3. It must clearly distinguish investment from technical cooperation and specify each project's details, terms, revenues, and expenditures.

At the time, American businesspeople approached developing countries with a chivalrous desire to help and a sense of mission.

Lee Byung-chull responded to their conditions with great humility. Korean

businesspeople had no experience attracting foreign capital, and this was their first venture abroad, so they needed to approach it as an opportunity to learn.

The central demand made by the American businesspeople was that Korea establish special industrial zones in coastal areas so that raw materials, machinery, and other goods could be transported by ship. They knew how weak Korea's transportation infrastructure was. Such zones would also need electricity, water, labor, and factory sites.

Lee promised that as soon as he returned home, he would select a suitable location based on the conditions the American business community had laid out. Korea had already been considering the construction of industrial zones for several years, and Lee was among those who had conceived the idea. He hoped to establish an industrial zone containing a world-scale fertilizer plant. Because he had long held this vision, he had also been searching for a suitable place. Ulsan interested him most.

Ulsan was not only a coastal city; the Taehwa River ran through its center, giving it abundant water resources. With the necessary power and labor, Ulsan would become an ideal industrial zone. The government could provide electricity, and unemployment meant that labor would be readily available.

The Korean businesspeople visiting the United States recommended Ulsan to their American counterparts. The Americans responded positively: “We will send a delegation to inspect the site.”

After gathering materials in the United States on potential investment and cooperation projects, Lee and the other business leaders returned home in early December. Meanwhile, the delegation to Europe visited companies in West Germany, including Krupp Steel and Siemens, to seek loans. The West German and American business communities did not impose especially harsh conditions, and matters proceeded smoothly.

Six months after the European delegation returned, 25 million dollars in capital attracted from West Germany was used to establish companies including GoldStar, Hanil Cement, and SsangYong Cement. Lee Byung-chull's theory of attracting foreign capital had proved successful.

On February 3, 1962, the government and business leaders held a groundbreaking ceremony for the “Ulsan Industrial Center.”

Five days after the groundbreaking ceremony, Lee Byung-chull, chairman of the Korean Businessmen's Association, and Vice Chairman Namgung Ryeon led a group of experts to the United States. Traveling around the country, they promoted Korea's Ulsan Industrial Center to American businesspeople and urgently pressed them to send an investment delegation to inspect Korea as soon as possible.

At last, three months later, on May 11, a twenty-eight-member “Delegation of Prominent American Businessmen,” headed by General James Van Fleet, former commander of United Nations forces in Korea, arrived at Gimpo Airport.

The delegation included representatives of world-class corporations such as General Motors, Ford, Westinghouse Electric, and Dow Chemical, as well as Texaco, Hanwha Industrial Bank, and American Standard.

That day, the American businesspeople paid courtesy calls on Chairman Park Chung Hee and Prime Minister Song Yo-chan, then attended a briefing at the Economic Planning Board on Korea's economic conditions, including the Five-Year Economic Development Plan. The next day, they traveled to Ulsan by military aircraft and special train.

They were accompanied by Kim Ip-sam, vice chairman of the Federation of Korean Industries. According to his memoirs, the Ulsan industrial zone at the time amounted to little more than flags planted in a desolate barley field. The members of the American delegation, who had arrived full of hope, were bitterly disappointed.

The members asked General Van Fleet, who had served as a commander during the Korean War, to explain Korea's circumstances. The portly general stepped forward. Pointing to the barley field, he told the American businesspeople that Korea was different from the United States: its territory was small, and not a single patch of land could be wasted.

Encouraged when the American guests nodded in understanding of Korea's circumstances, the official in charge began explaining Ulsan's advantages as an industrial base.

First, Ulsan was a strategic hub for both sea and land transportation. Pohang lay to the north and Busan to the south, while the surrounding stretch of the southern coast had the potential to develop into an industrial belt.

He went on to explain that numerous small and medium-sized cities surrounded Ulsan, so labor would not be a problem. As for water, the Taehwa and Imjin rivers were nearby,[^TN-CH12-001] and if necessary, water from the Nakdong River could be brought in by pipeline. The American businesspeople were more concerned with the port city's location and terrain. After personally inspecting the surrounding land, they were highly satisfied with Ulsan.

The American delegation left Korea on May 26, having spent the period before its departure consulting with Korean businesspeople about investment cooperation.

Ultimately, several American companies exchanged letters of intent with Korean enterprises. The day before the delegation departed, the Korean government, the Korean Businessmen's

Association, and the American business delegation signed a preliminary investment agreement at Prime Minister Song Yo-chan's residence.

Chairman Lee Byung-chull and delegation leader Van Fleet issued a joint statement emphasizing the need for economic exchange between the two countries.

As the Korean people watched the American investment delegation's activities, they began to believe that economic development could be achieved by attracting foreign capital.

Korean businesspeople also benefited greatly from the effort to attract investment: they learned a great deal and gained confidence.

It was after the May 16 military coup that Korea began to find the direction of its economic development.

Lee Byung-chull's advocacy of foreign investment provided an opening for Korea's economic development. His target of “178 dollars in per capita income” was achieved in 1969, three years earlier than the 1972 date he had envisioned.

While American and European businesspeople were visiting Korea to explore investment opportunities, Chung Ju-yung turned his gaze overseas and planned an even greater undertaking.

Beginning with the Han River Bridge project in the mid-1950s, Hyundai Construction grew steadily and, by the 1960s, had become the leader of Korea's construction industry.

Taking on new fields was one of Chung Ju-yung's particular strengths.

“For resource-poor Korea to survive, its only path is exports,” Chung said.

In 1965, Chung secured the Narathiwat Highway project in Thailand, defeating twenty-nine companies from sixteen developed countries. It was the first time a Korean construction company had ventured abroad. For a Korean construction industry with no experience building highways, this was a momentous undertaking. Working overseas also placed immense pressure on Hyundai Construction, and industry insiders at home criticized the project. Chung, however, found it enormously attractive. The contract was worth 5.22 million dollars—more than Hyundai Construction's total annual project value and more than 65 percent of the combined value of all domestic and overseas construction contracts awarded to Korean firms in 1965.

Faced with a project of such enormous scale, Chung devoted all his energy and effort to it. He dispatched his finest technical team, frequently sent his younger brother Chung In-yung to supervise the site, and flew to Thailand himself whenever he had time.

“We must honor the contract. No matter what difficulties arise, we must finish the project by the stipulated deadline. That is our obligation to the Thai government!”

Chung said this at the construction site.

Yet the project was extraordinarily difficult. To begin with, Thailand's weather was entirely different from Korea's. In Korea, most rain fell during the monsoon season; in Thailand, it could rain at any time. Sometimes a sudden storm swept through during the day, but more often, torrential rain fell without stopping. Work naturally had to cease during a downpour, not merely for a few hours but sometimes for four or five days. The project was inevitably delayed, while workers still had to be paid when it rained, driving costs beyond the budget.

The soil was also very different from Korea's. In Korea, digging into the ground would turn up either stones or bedrock.[^TN-CH12-002] In Thailand, no matter how deep they dug, they found only soil. Construction therefore had to begin by spreading crushed stone over the soft ground. Because the soil was loose and yielding, even the gravel would sink. In short, the materials required differed enormously from what had been budgeted based on Korean conditions.

Equipment posed another serious problem. Chung had brought conventional machinery, but conditions in Thailand were utterly different from those in Korea. The forests were tall and dense, and rain caused the rivers to overflow. Access roads laboriously laid to transport materials could vanish without a trace after a single night of heavy rain. The equipment brought from Korea was wholly inadequate for the project.

The local hired workers also presented difficulties; they were extremely indolent. Once the sun set, they would do no more work. In Korea, by contrast, workers would continue by torchlight to keep a project on schedule.

Compared with Thailand, Korea's climate, land, and human resources seemed exceptionally well suited to construction.

At the site, Chung had people build the vibratory rollers, cranes, mixers, and other equipment they needed, and he purchased additional machinery.

Yet even the latest equipment they purchased sometimes proved useless, because the locally hired workers did not know how to operate it and repeatedly broke the machinery.

A lack of technology and experience, a weak project-management system, and unforeseen

weather and soil conditions continually obstructed the project.

Some employees began arguing that the project should be abandoned. But Chung Ju-yung was Chung Ju-yung: he was determined to see it through. In the end, he completed the project despite suffering a massive loss.

Yet the Thai highway project did not leave him empty-handed. He had been the first in Korea to venture abroad to build a highway, and just one year later he successfully completed the Kyungbu Expressway project. He gained something else as well: an employee named Lee Myung-bak.

Lee Myung-bak had not trained in construction. At the Thai project site, he worked as an accountant. In 1966, Thai laborers rioted over pay. They smashed machinery and surged toward the site office. A Thai accountant shouted urgently to Lee, “Mr. Lee, run for your life!” But Lee could not abandon the safe and flee. Everyone else ran; he alone remained.

The rioters were startled to find him there alone. One of them brandished a knife and said,

“If you know what's good for you, hand over the key to the safe.”

Lee refused and stood his ground.

“Do you want to die?”

The rioters held their knives before Lee's face.

Lee backed up several steps until he was against the wall. He seemed certain to die. For an instant, he considered handing over the key. In fact, the safe held only a small amount of petty cash.

But as the accountant, it was his duty to protect the safe. He clasped it to his chest.

“Let go!”

The rioters seized him by the collar, but he never let go. Countless kicks struck his back. Fortunately, the police arrived promptly after receiving a report and rescued him.

News of the safe incident traveled to Hyundai Construction's Bangkok branch and its Seoul headquarters. It also reached Chung Ju-yung.

From that point on, Chung took notice of the twenty-six-year-old Lee, setting him on the path of a meteoric rise. Lee became a managing director at Hyundai Construction at twenty-nine, a senior managing director in 1974, and company president in 1975, when he was thirty-five.

He joined the company in 1965 and, in just ten years, rose from an entry-level employee to president.

News that Chung Ju-yung had suffered heavy losses on the Thai highway project reached the Blue House.

President Park Chung Hee was then contemplating the Kyungbu Expressway. He summoned Chung to the Blue House and said,

“President Chung, I hear you lost a great deal in Thailand this time?”

Chung replied,

“Rather than a loss, I would call it rather expensive tuition. The current project will be completed by next March. Another project will follow, and I will earn back the tuition I paid on that one.”

“You must recover in Thailand what you lost there!”

President Park said this from the standpoint of the national interest.

“Please do not worry. How could I go to Thailand for a project and return at a loss? I will be sure to come back having made money.”

Chung did not regard himself as having failed. Although the contract revenue he had expected had evaporated, the knowledge he gained was considerable: the areas urgently requiring improvement were experience and technology. Applying what he had learned, he successfully completed the Kyungbu Expressway project in Korea.

# Vietnam War Projects and Korea Fertilizer Company

If you are always chasing after others and merely playing the role of a troubleshooter, you will forever lose in competition. Whatever the undertaking,

I hope you will not always think of doing it alone. Cultivate unity among the people in the company, and bring everyone's intelligence, wisdom, and moral standing into play.

—Lee Byung-chull

“What a beautiful morning—let us move forward.” The song came over the radio.

In the mid-1960s, the sky over Seoul was a clear blue. Environmental pollution did not yet exist.

The fountain in front of Seoul City Hall sent water spraying into the air, and a banner reading “Beautiful Seoul, Population 3.5 Million” hung before the main hall.

Across the intersection, in Deoksugung Palace, stood a “Domestic Products Exhibition Hall.” It displayed and sold sneakers, clothing, and even salt made by Korea's emerging enterprises. On occasion, it exhibited whales caught off Pohang in North Gyeongsang Province.

In March, as construction began on the Export Industrial Complex[^FB-F022] in Guro-dong,[^FB-F021] Koreans began to feel confident that they could succeed if only they set to work.

In 1965, Korea dispatched two thousand noncombat troops of the Dove Engineering Corps to Vietnam, followed later by the Tiger Division.

Students flocked to Busan Harbor holding the Taegeukgi and sang to the soldiers departing for Vietnam: “Onward to Vietnam, unafraid of mountains high or waters far...”

In January 1966, Chung Ju-yung also became involved in the Vietnam War. While Korea provided military assistance by dispatching troops, it also gained the right to participate in construction work in Vietnam.

Korean construction companies mainly took part in Vietnam's reconstruction, particularly civil engineering and building projects. Hyundai Construction chiefly undertook dredging, while Daelim Industrial handled harbor work. Many Korean laborers were also hired by American companies and sent to work in Vietnam. In addition to construction and civil engineering, Koreans participated in loading, unloading, and overland transport.

Although Chung had suffered a huge loss on Thailand's Narathiwat Highway, the experience he gained there gave him complete confidence that he could succeed in Vietnam.

Hyundai's first contract was to dredge the US military base at Cam Ranh Bay. Facing the South China Sea, Cam Ranh Bay is an excellent natural harbor and was a strategic supply point for the US military.

The United States needed to unload enormous quantities of supplies at Cam Ranh Bay to win the Vietnam War, but the harbor's unloading facilities were severely inadequate. The US military needed to dredge the bay on a large scale and build it into a larger military base so that freighters of more than ten thousand tons could enter and leave freely.

At the time, twenty-one dredgers from the United States, Australia, Singapore, Japan, France, and other countries were working on the development of Cam Ranh Bay.

Hyundai's vessel was assigned to work in a group with ships from Australia and two other countries, because a project of such magnitude was beyond the capacity of any one country's company.

Working around the clock with a 2,550-horsepower dredger, Hyundai brought the already delayed project to completion ahead of schedule. Its capabilities immediately drew the US military's attention. Hyundai next received a housing-construction contract in one city. While that project was underway, the US military also entrusted Hyundai with dredging in the Mekong Delta.

The Mekong Delta was Vietnam's great granary. One-third of Vietnam's territory was mountainous, and the delta was its broadest agricultural region. It was also an area the US military had to secure for strategic reasons. Because of the war, however, the delta had become overgrown with weeds.

At the time, the US military's 8,000-horsepower dredger *Jamaica* churned up sand and soil in the Mekong Delta and spread it over the weeds. In other words, it first cleared the land and then constructed a navigable channel through the Mekong.

The area was also a granary for the Viet Cong, who had long sought to retake the Mekong Delta from the US military. One day, they shelled the *Jamaica*, sinking it and dealing the Americans a severe blow.

General Westmoreland, commander of US forces in Vietnam, ordered a rear admiral to resume work immediately. Because Hyundai had demonstrated exceptional ability on the Cam Ranh Bay project, the US military strongly recommended that it continue the work.

Even Chung Ju-yung, who never backed down, hesitated this time. Viet Cong firepower had been able to sink the *Jamaica*, and Hyundai's workers wondered whether they would return alive

from such a mission. Under threat from armed US troops, Hyundai had no choice but to accept the Mekong project, for this was a granary that could not be allowed to fall into Viet Cong hands under any circumstances. In the end, the *Hyundai No. 1* dredger headed for the Mekong Delta.

As expected, US and Viet Cong forces were engaged in fierce fighting in the Mekong Delta. Viet Cong troops hid in the weeds and attacked around the clock; at night, flares rose without pause as the battle continued day and night. Amid this maelstrom of danger, Hyundai's workers turned the soil by day to cover the weeds. Equipment was severely lacking, so Chung added the 4,000-horsepower dredger *Hyundai No. 2*.

Work continued. By the time the Lunar New Year arrived, Hyundai employees who had originally been working in Vinh Long,[^FB-F023] three hundred kilometers from the Mekong, found themselves caught in the Viet Cong's Tet Offensive.

Viet Cong and South Vietnamese forces fired at one another from opposite banks of the river, filling the air with bullets. Hyundai's workers on the delta between them were a step away from death. The US military dropped food by helicopter. Still, the work continued.

After fifteen long days, the Tet Offensive ended, and Hyundai's workers were able to withdraw from the Mekong Delta.

Hyundai earned enormous profits from the Vietnam War and once again accumulated experience working overseas. That experience was applied to the Kyungbu Expressway project the following year.

Let us return to the bitter, bone-chilling cold of the late 1950s. After the great success of Cheil Sugar and Cheil Wool Textile, Lee Byung-chull conceived a fertilizer plant that would be the largest in the world, or at least in East Asia. It was a dream he had long cherished. He had begun considering it late in President Syngman Rhee's administration, but because it was an enormous project, it required government support and approval.

As noted earlier, it was in Tokyo in 1959 that Lee formally resolved to begin planning the fertilizer plant—what became known as the Tokyo Initiative.

Watching a New Year's interview program on television at Tokyo's Imperial Hotel, Lee learned that ten developed countries—including the United States, Britain, West Germany, France, Italy, Canada, and Australia—intended to provide long-term, low-interest loans to developing countries with growth potential. It was exhilarating news.

Lee had long wanted to establish a world-class fertilizer plant but lacked the funds. After making detailed inquiries about the program, he learned that it offered not aid money but private commercial loans: a sound plan could qualify for long-term, low-interest financing. For Lee, who had been struggling with the funding question, the information was a timely lifeline.

At the time, Korea received 250 million dollars in aid each year, of which approximately 100 million dollars was used to import fertilizer. In other words, fertilizer accounted for the largest share of the goods imported with aid funds.

The government knew this well. It began building the Chungju Fertilizer Plant in 1955 and the Naju Fertilizer Plant in 1958. Yet even with both factories operating at full capacity, their combined annual output was only sixty thousand tons.

Korea's fertilizer demand was three hundred thousand tons in 1960 and four hundred thousand tons in 1966.

In short, Korea would need six more fertilizer plants like those in Chungju and Naju to meet domestic demand.

Lee was determined to establish a fertilizer plant capable of competing in export markets. To achieve this, he believed he would have to import large quantities of the most advanced equipment. He planned a plant with an annual capacity of 350,000 tons, producing urea and compound fertilizers suited to Korea rather than ammonia fertilizer.

As soon as he returned to Korea, Lee visited President Syngman Rhee, who knew him personally. Lee's father, Lee Chan-woo, and Rhee had participated in a Christian youth movement together when they were young.

Lee had come to present his plan to build a fertilizer plant and request government approval.

President Rhee asked how much money would be needed.

Lee replied, “Approximately forty to fifty million dollars.”

“What? Forty to fifty million?” President Rhee exclaimed, his face

showing his astonishment.

At the time, Rhee was so frugal that he personally scrutinized expenditures of even a single dollar. It was no wonder that hearing a figure of forty to fifty million dollars came as a shock.

Lee explained that he planned to raise the money through loans from Europe. Only then did President Rhee look satisfied, and he immediately promised vigorous support. With the president's approval secured, Lee moved actively to attract financing.

On February 1, 1960, President Syngman Rhee embarked on a visit to Europe.

Through Hertz, the West German ambassador to Korea, Lee arranged to meet West German economics minister Ludwig Erhard. At the time, West Germany was an economic power with 8 billion dollars in foreign-exchange reserves.

Introduced by West German officials, Lee met a vice president of Krupp. Founded in 1801, Krupp was a steel company and one of the great industrial conglomerates of its day. During the First Sino-Japanese War in 1894, the Qing dynasty's main warships—the 7,335-ton *Dingyuan*, then the largest warship in East Asia, and the *Zhiyuan*—had both been manufactured by Krupp.

Lee came straight to the point: he wanted to build a fertilizer plant with an annual output of three hundred thousand tons and sought a loan. To his surprise, the Krupp vice president agreed on the spot, having already received the details from Minister Erhard. West German policy at the time was to devote approximately 300 million dollars of its 8 billion dollars in foreign-exchange reserves to aid for developing countries, and loans had already been extended to India. The vice president promised that the loan would be provided as soon as a bank guarantee was issued.

Lee sent out the project proposal and proceeded to his next destination, Italy, where he intended to visit a fertilizer company in the Montecatini group. Montecatini was a major corporation with more than one hundred chemical plants, fertilizer works, power stations, mines, and other enterprises across Europe. Its manager, too, was very friendly.

In 1930, Montecatini's fertilizer company was the first in the world to develop a process for producing nitrogen and ammonia, and the first plant built using that process was the Hungnam[^FB-F024] Nitrogen Fertilizer Plant. The company did not share its proprietary technology with anyone, so the fact that the Hungnam plant in Korea obtained it demonstrated the sincerity of the cooperation.

Cooperation with West Germany and Italy proceeded unexpectedly smoothly, and the loan formalities were simple and trouble-free. Lee spent several days sightseeing in Italy. It was then that he received shocking news: the April 19 Revolution had broken out in Korea, and President Syngman Rhee had stepped down. When Lee returned home, it felt as though the world had changed.

The fertilizer-plant loan, which had been proceeding so smoothly, ran aground over the issue of so-called illicit profiteering after the April 19 Revolution. Public opinion strongly demanded that illicit profiteers be punished. Samsung employees and Lee himself were investigated for illicit profiteering, and Lee was summoned by a minister for questioning. Tax evasion was the focus of the investigation. Lee said that he had evaded taxes, while his employees each claimed responsibility themselves, both sides trying to take the blame for the other.

The investigation continued for several months. In the end, Samsung's six companies were ordered to pay a total of 5 billion won in back taxes.

Lee handed the fertilizer-plant plans he had labored over to Kim Young-sun, the company's finance director. A few days later, he flew to Japan with a heavy heart, beginning more than a year of life abroad.

The fertilizer-plant issue resurfaced after the May 16 military coup. Seeking to revitalize the national economy and raise incomes by building factories, the military government encouraged the attraction of foreign capital. As noted earlier, Lee played a crucial role in that effort.

Once foreign capital began to arrive, each entrepreneur chose the field he wanted to pursue. Lee Byung-chull, Chung Jae-ha of Samhwa Textile, and Kim Ji-tae of Chosun Light Textiles chose fertilizer; Namgung Ryeon chose light oil; Lee Yang-gu of the Tongyang Group and Seol Gyeong-dong of Daehan Industrial chose ironmaking; Koo In-hoe chose electric cables; and Kim Sung-kon of GoldStar Textile and Lee Han-won of Daehan Flour Mills chose electric power.

In May 1962, when General James Van Fleet led a large delegation of twenty-eight American businesspeople, including representatives of Westinghouse Electric, to Korea, a preliminary agreement on investment in steelmaking, fertilizer, and other industries was signed at Prime Minister Song Yo-chan's office. It was an agreement between the government and the business community.

Under this agreement, Lee Byung-chull, Chung Jae-ha, and Kim Ji-tae formed the Ulsan Fertilizer

Investment Consortium and returned to Japan and Europe to seek investment.

As a result, Kobe Steel, Fuji Electric, and Mitsubishi Shipbuilding of Japan, Weecho of the United States, Krupp of West Germany, and other companies submitted investment proposals. Kobe Steel's terms were the most favorable.

Lee's enthusiasm soared. He planned to attract 55 million dollars in foreign capital, add 5 billion won in domestic financing, and build a large fertilizer plant with an annual capacity of three hundred thousand tons.

Yet the Korean government, whose annual exports amounted to only 100 million dollars, ultimately abandoned the astronomically expensive fertilizer plant. Everything returned to where it had begun, and Lee suffered another emotional blow.

Another opportunity arose later. In October 1963, Park Chung Hee was elected president and pressed for construction of the fertilizer plant to resume. But Lee did not accept rashly; he had already learned from two failures.

President Park sent Deputy Prime Minister Chang Key-young to persuade Lee. Chang approached him with great enthusiasm, but Lee did not commit himself immediately, having grown disillusioned with the government's constant policy reversals.

Chang began telephoning Lee and later visited his home several times.

“I will back you on the administrative side. There will be no problem, so please begin building the plant as soon as possible.”

In the end, the government actively supported administrative approvals, foreign financing, and every other aspect of the project, and construction of the fertilizer plant was placed on the agenda. Lee had decided to build it.

Lee traveled to Japan to raise funds, carrying an official letter stating that “the Korean government has entrusted Samsung with the construction of a large-scale fertilizer plant.” He met with the president of Mitsui & Co., with whom he had long maintained close relations, President Inayama of Yawata Steel, and President Kaijima of Kobe Steel to discuss the project.

President Inayama of Yawata Steel was especially enthusiastic.

“Let us give it everything we have and work together.”

Word of these contacts spread rapidly, causing considerable alarm in Japan's fertilizer industry. Korea had always been an important market for Japanese fertilizer; no one had imagined that it might one day become a competitor.

At the time, Japan's largest fertilizer plant produced 180,000 tons a year; most others produced fifty to sixty thousand tons, or at most one hundred thousand. News that Lee intended to build a plant with an annual capacity of three hundred thousand tons placed immense pressure on them.

A representative of Japan's fertilizer industry came to see Lee.

He proposed, “Do not build the fertilizer plant. From now on, we will supply fertilizer to Korea for the long term at the lowest possible price.” Lee, of course, refused.

People in Japan's fertilizer industry believed that if Lee completed a plant capable of producing three hundred thousand tons a year, they would be unable to compete with it. Fortunately, the Japanese business community remained very friendly.

With the help of President Inayama of Yawata Steel, an agreement was finally signed under which Mitsui & Co.'s fertilizer division would provide the plant equipment and financing. Annual fertilizer output was set at thirty-three tons, with 42 million dollars in foreign capital to be repaid over eight years at annual interest of 5.5 percent. An average annual output of 330,000 tons would make it the largest facility of its kind in the world.[^TN-CH13-001]

At the time, fertilizer in Korea cost approximately 86 to 90 dollars per ton, while Lee's Korea Fertilizer could sell it for 56 dollars per ton. Once completed, the plant would therefore both create jobs and provide inexpensive fertilizer. After many twists and turns, construction of the Korea Fertilizer plant in Ulsan finally began. Yet the difficulties were not over.

On September 16, 1966, when the Korea Fertilizer plant was 80 percent complete, the so-called Korea Fertilizer smuggling incident erupted. It began when a chemical called OTSA, stored in a bonded warehouse, was sold on the market without government permission.

OTSA was a special chemical developed by the Italian company Montecatini and was required for a carbon-dioxide absorption and regeneration unit, one stage in an ammonia-fertilizer plant. Put simply, it was a raw material for saccharin. At the time, saccharin was used in the food industry to add sweetness.

Sugar was expensive then, so saccharin was widely used as a sweetener. It was added to bread and fruit juice alike, and was regarded as a daily necessity.

Someone sold six tons of the saccharin stored in the bonded warehouse for fifty thousand dollars, and it entered the market. Newspapers traced its origin, and rumors spread that Samsung's eldest daughter had smuggled it from abroad. The papers ran prominent

stories accusing Korea's largest conglomerate of smuggling saccharin. An investigation began, and the origin of the saccharin eventually came to light. It was connected to the 42-million-dollar loan provided by Japan's Mitsui & Co. Not all of that loan had been supplied in cash; part had taken the form of machinery needed for the plant. In the process, Mitsui offered a one-million-dollar rebate as thanks for the purchase of its machinery. It was an unexpected windfall, but the problem was how to bring the money into Korea through normal channels. Lee wrestled with the issue.

According to the autobiography of Lee's eldest son, Lee Maeng-hee, Lee informed President Park Chung Hee about the money. President Park allegedly instructed that, because the money could not enter Korea in cash, it should be used to purchase one million dollars' worth of goods. After those goods were sold, one-third of the proceeds would go to political funds, one-third to construction funds, and one-third to the operating funds of Korea Fertilizer.

Following Park's suggestion, the one million dollars was used to import OTSA, refrigerators, air conditioners, telephones, stainless-steel sheets, and other goods in secret. If Lee Maeng-hee's account is factual, then President Park himself was involved in the smuggling. The incident remains unresolved to this day, and Lee ultimately returned Korea Fertilizer Company to the state because of it.

The completion ceremony was held, and the Japanese friends who had helped the project hosted a celebratory banquet at Tokyo's Imperial Hotel. Yet Korea Fertilizer Company, into which Lee had poured ten years of effort, vanished from his hands. It was his greatest misfortune. After the affair ended, Lee once again withdrew into travel and seclusion.

Korea Fertilizer Company rejoined Samsung in 1994. Under the Korean government's privatization policy for the company, an open tender was held with an opening price of 130 billion won. Samsung won with a bid 100 billion won above that price—a total of 230 billion won—and thus regained control of Korea Fertilizer after twenty-seven years.

# The Pony Car and Tongyang Television

I do not know whether others regard me as a capitalist running a world-class corporation. I have

never thought of myself as a capitalist. I am merely a prosperous laborer—a worker who creates wealth through labor.

—Chung Ju-yung

From Ordinary to Brilliant

At the beginning of 1967, the government set an export target of 300 million dollars for the new year. At the time, a cup of coffee cost 40 won and a geun of beef cost 200 won.

President Park Chung Hee titled his New Year's state-of-the-nation address “An All-Out Advance toward an Industrial Nation.” Like the heading of a military proclamation, it announced preparations for a march toward industrialization. Korea, too, would produce high-value-added goods for export.

To leap ahead in the story, following the path designed by President Park, Korea's electronics exports reached 10 billion dollars in 1987, twenty years later, and 20 billion dollars in 1992.

Twenty-five years after President Park issued his 1967 declaration that Korea would become an electronics power, the country's cumulative electronics exports had reached 133 billion dollars. Korea had finally become a major force in the industry.

In April that year, Lee Byung-chull's Korea Fertilizer plant in Ulsan was completed. In May, in Korea's sixth presidential election, Park Chung Hee was reelected by a margin of 1.1 million votes over opposition candidate Kim Dae-jung. That year also saw publication of Korea's first nationwide report on parasite infection, showing that 80 percent of the population was infected with eleven types of parasites, including roundworms, whipworms, and tapeworms. Another extraordinary event occurred when miner Yang Chang-sun was rescued after being buried for sixteen days in the Gubong Mine in South Chungcheong Province. Radio carried the rescue effort, leaving people throughout Korea anxiously awaiting the outcome. The story was later dramatized in the play *Escape from Danger*.

Meanwhile, something new awaited Chung Ju-yung. Hyundai Motor was established that February, marking his entry into the automobile business.

There are many explanations for Chung's decision to enter automobile manufacturing. One was that in 1943, at the age of twenty-seven, he had opened the Ado Auto Repair Shop. Another view was that construction clients always bought on credit, preventing funds from circulating promptly, whereas automobiles could be sold entirely for cash.

“Construction cannot continue because payments are always overdue. Cars are cash transactions, so we need to manufacture automobiles.” Chung said this to Yoon Jun-mo, then chairman of the Korea Automobile Industry Association.

Chung had acquired considerable automotive knowledge early in life. When a car malfunctioned, he could even open the hood and diagnose the problem. Yet manufacturing and repairing automobiles were entirely different undertakings. A car contained twenty-five thousand parts, and production required the capacity to manufacture most of them directly. At the time, only Shinjin Industrial and Saenara Motor were producing automobiles in Korea.

Shinjin Industrial produced 215 cars in 1964 and 106 in 1965. In 1966, with technical assistance from Toyota of Japan, it produced 3,117 vehicles. Most automotive parts were then imported. Saenara Motor, after a difficult start, imported complete sets of parts for Nissan's Bluebird from Japan and assembled them in Korea, making the cars virtually indistinguishable from Japanese-made vehicles. To put it bluntly, for other cars, only the body shell was made in Korea.

In 1966, the domestic content rate of Korean automobiles was 21 percent, consisting mostly of minor components apart from tires and batteries. Korea had almost no capacity to manufacture automotive parts.

At the time, Ford sought to enter the Korean automobile market. In April that year, a Ford Motor Company vice president responsible for international affairs came to Korea for discussions. Hyundai did not even qualify to participate: from Ford's perspective, it was merely a construction company with no connection to automobiles. The Ford representative contacted several Korean automakers and then returned to the United States.

Chung's younger brother Chung In-yung was then in the United States seeking a loan to expand the Danyang[^FB-F025] Cement Plant. Chung suddenly instructed him to return only after securing a technical agreement with Ford.

Chung In-yung went to Ford, first explaining that Chung Ju-yung had once operated an auto repair shop, then persuading the company to enter talks with Hyundai. Ford agreed to add Hyundai to its list of candidates. To select a partner, it conducted a detailed investigation, even drawing on the intelligence resources of the US Embassy in Korea. Hyundai and Chung Ju-yung ranked first in credibility.

Ford again sent an executive responsible for international affairs to Korea for an interview. Four days had been allotted for his meeting with Chung, but it ended after only two hours. Chung explained the structure of an automobile engine and named more than ten thousand components, from the transmission to the brakes, one by one. The American representative concluded that no further examination was necessary.

On its second day in Korea, Ford signed a technical-assistance agreement with Hyundai Motor. The joint-venture shares were 21 percent for Hyundai and 79 percent for Ford. Chung then began building the Hyundai automobile plant in Ulsan.

Ford took three years after its founding to produce its first car; Hyundai needed only one year to produce its first Cortina, in 1968.

The Cortina entered the market immediately after production began, with film actor Ha Myung-joong[^FB-F026] serving as the advertising model. Trucks and buses followed, including the Ford 20M and the Ford R-192 bus.

As he ran the automobile business, Chung sought to end Hyundai's reliance on imported Ford parts as quickly as possible and localize vehicle production. At the time, 30 percent of the parts in Korean cars were made domestically, but all major components were imported. Localization would benefit both the country and Chung himself: imported components accounted for 70 percent of a car's price, while assembly represented only 9 percent. Domestic production of components would therefore yield much greater economic returns. Ford, however, was interested only in assembling cars in Korea. It did not want Korean parts to be localized and wished to pay only the 9 percent assembly cost.

As soon as the agreement expired, Chung proposed that Hyundai and Ford change their joint-venture shares from 21–79 to 50–50. Ford accepted.

But when would component localization actually be achieved? Chung wanted to produce a car whose parts were mostly made in Korea and then sell Hyundai vehicles through Ford's distribution network. In practice, this was extremely difficult.

The engine is an automobile's most important component, and producing engines is inseparable from producing cars. Ford was never going to divulge its technical secrets. Hyundai and Ford sat down to negotiate the establishment of an engine plant, but the atmosphere was tense. In the end, they discussed only finance, exports, and management, never reaching the substance of the issue.

Chung no longer wished to cooperate with Ford, because he had seen the true nature of the multinational corporation. Ford had no intention of giving Hyundai access to export markets, or even of helping it.

What, then, could he do? Chung decided that, whatever the time and expense, Hyundai would develop its own technology and independently design and manufacture its own automobile. He saw this as the only way to break beyond Korea's small domestic market and enter overseas markets: a 100-percent Korean-made car. Chung embarked on another challenge.

Yet to compete with imported foreign cars at the time required annual capacity of fifty thousand vehicles and an investment of 100 million dollars—an astronomical sum. All his subordinates strongly opposed the plan because of the risk.

Hyundai Construction's annual revenue was only 5 billion won, Hyundai Motor had capital of just 1.7 billion won, and Korea as a whole produced only twenty thousand vehicles a year. His subordinates had good reason to object.

Which way should he go? When faced with such questions, Chung always chose the riskier path. Rather than sit and wait, he preferred to charge forward and solve the problem.

On such occasions, he often liked to say, “Try it first, then talk.”

At the time, the Korean government was vigorously promoting the chemical and heavy industries. Its analysis was that a better standard of living required an emphasis on heavy rather than light industry. The overall coordinator of the drive was Oh Won-chul, the Blue House's second-ranking economic official.

Treating the automobile industry as part of its chemical and heavy-industry program, the government announced an automotive policy and a plan for mass production of small cars. It was deeply concerned because Korea spent large amounts of foreign currency each year importing automotive components. Expenditures were 3.29 million dollars in 1966, nearly tripled to 9.13 million dollars in 1967, and reached 22.98 million dollars in 1968

and 42.58 million dollars in 1969. Like a snowball, the figure increased thirteenfold in three years.

Dollars were extremely precious then. Even when civil servants traveled abroad, President Park Chung Hee personally took the required dollars from a safe. Faced with soaring import costs for automotive parts, the government could no longer stand by. Its mass-production plan arose from this context. Hyundai submitted a proposal, financed entirely on its own, to produce fifty thousand cars a year. Yet Korea then had only 170,000 automobiles in total. Even if fifty thousand cars could be made each year, who would buy them?

The Cortina that Hyundai had produced with Ford a few years earlier sold only about five hundred units a month. Fifty thousand cars, then...

Hyundai's proposal for annual production of fifty thousand vehicles was startling at the time, but Chung did not see it that way. To him, fifty thousand was merely the minimum. He had already recognized the limitations of the domestic market and set his sights overseas.

Seeking to make Korea an industrial power, the government supported automobile production and promised the greatest possible tax relief. Seizing the opportunity, Chung established four principles and submitted a plan for a factory capable of producing fifty thousand cars a year. His characteristic stubbornness again came into play.

1. Develop an existing automobile, with exports as the principal target of development.

2. Accept no capital assistance from foreign enterprises.

3. Build an automobile factory whose vehicles would be produced entirely with domestically made components.

4. Build the factory on a scale capable of international competition.

He incorporated everything he had felt during the partnership with Ford into these principles, particularly the second: no capital assistance from foreign companies. Chung began the preparations.

In September 1973, the new car's design was commissioned from an Italian firm for one million dollars. Giorgetto Giugiaro handled the interior, while Mantovani designed the body. Giugiaro was a prodigy who had designed Volkswagen's Golf, Passat, and Scirocco in West Germany, Alfa Romeo cars in Italy, and Isuzu models in Japan, earning him the sobriquet “the second Michelangelo.” An agreement for the engine was signed with Britain's BLMC.

Determined to manufacture his own car entirely with Hyundai's capital, Chung formally began construction in January 1974 on a large automobile plant with annual capacity of fifty-six thousand vehicles. He devoted himself completely to developing a domestically produced car.

When he heard that Hyundai intended to produce automobiles, H. W. Benki, a senior executive at GMK, the predecessor of Daewoo Motor, laughed: “It is utterly impossible for Hyundai to develop and manufacture a car based on its own model.” His view was not without reason. European and American automotive technology had nearly a century of history and could not be mastered overnight. The idea that a company with Hyundai's short history would produce and export cars inevitably invited ridicule.

On October 30, 1974, Hyundai exhibited its independently developed Pony and a sports version of the Pony for the first time at the Turin International Motor Show, where the world's newest cars were unveiled. The name Pony was chosen through a public naming contest. Sixty-five companies from sixteen countries displayed 245 vehicles, including Ford, Mercedes-Benz, Citroën, Peugeot, Toyota, Honda, Fiat, and Moskvitch. Making its debut, the Pony unexpectedly proved immensely popular. More than three hundred journalists and automotive experts crowded around to see it. Chung's younger brother Chung Se-yung, then president of Hyundai Motor, was inundated with interview requests.

The Pony attracted so much attention because it was well designed and economical in its fuel use, and because it was Korea's first independently manufactured car. Korea thus became the sixteenth country in the world—and the second in Asia after Japan—with the ability to produce automobiles independently.

Having won praise abroad, the Pony also attracted public attention at home. In its first year on the market, it overtook GMK's Camina and Kia's Brisa to capture 43 percent of the market. Six units were exported to Ecuador for the first time that June, launching the car into the world. Markets in Africa and the Middle East followed, and ten thousand vehicles were exported in the first year. By the late 1970s, Korea's automobile market was growing rapidly, and annual output rose from fifty thousand to one hundred thousand vehicles.

The Pony 2 was developed in 1982 and sold forty thousand units domestically as soon as it entered the market. Chung then turned his attention to Canada. In November 1983, fifteen hundred Pony 2s were shipped there. Orders suddenly surged because

the car was inexpensive and performed well. Twenty-five thousand units were sold in Canada in the first year and eighty thousand in 1986, the second year. The Pony 2 took first place in a Canadian market previously dominated by Japan.

Hyundai next turned toward the United States, the kingdom of the automobile. It established a sales subsidiary there in 1985, built a nationwide distribution network, and launched a large-scale promotional campaign. Hyundai advertised on the three major American television networks under the striking slogan “The Car of Harmony.” Korean companies had almost never appeared in American television advertising before. Korean Americans were tremendously excited. They had believed their homeland's economy was still struggling to its feet; seeing advertisements for Korean cars on American television and watching those cars enter the US market moved many of them to tears.

A poor country that had survived on relief supplies after the Korean War was now manufacturing and exporting automobiles. Americans, too, looked at Korea in astonishment.

Sales surpassed fifty thousand units just four months after Hyundai entered the American market. In 1986, its first full year, the company sold 170,000 vehicles.

That year, Hyundai's Excel ranked third in sales behind Nissan and Honda of Japan. In 1987, Hyundai sold 260,000 Excels in the United States, compared with Nissan's 240,000, Honda's 170,000, and the Corona's 120,000. Thereafter, Hyundai maintained a 5-percent share of the US automobile market.

Today, Korea exports more than three million automobiles each year to the United States, Japan, Germany, France, and other countries. It has become the world's fifth-largest automobile producer, overtaking Britain and Italy, where automobiles were first manufactured. Korea has become an automotive power, and Chung Ju-yung deserves great credit for that achievement.

Chung circled above the Korean Peninsula in a small plane, binoculars in hand, looking down at the country's mountains and rivers. Departing from Seoul, he repeatedly flew over Daejeon, Yeongdong, Gimcheon, Daegu, and Busan. It was all for the Kyungbu Expressway project.

The 429-kilometer expressway intended to connect Seoul and Busan was a major national project. To realize Korea's Second Economic Development Plan, President Park Chung Hee decided to build it.

President Park had long been interested in expressways. In 1964, at the invitation of West German president Heinrich Lübke, he visited West Germany and traveled the twenty-kilometer autobahn between Bonn and Cologne. Twice along the way, he asked the driver to stop so that he could get out and inspect the road surface, median, interchanges, and other features. He questioned the German official accompanying him closely about expressways. Fortunately, the official was trained as an economist and knew a great deal about them. Unfolding a map, he explained West Germany's expressway network in detail: “Germany's expressway network radiates across the country from West Berlin, making it possible to reach any place within thirty minutes.” Park asked detailed questions about expressway construction management, the time required, methods of raising funds, and the necessary equipment and personnel.

During his talks with German chancellor Ludwig Erhard, Park again raised the subject of the autobahn. Erhard, a renowned chancellor, had served as economics minister during West Germany's economic recovery, the “Miracle on the Rhine.” He said that the autobahn had played an enormous role in Germany's economic development and that every time he traveled on it, he felt profound respect for the expressway. President Park fully understood and shared his feeling.

Before Park's visit to Germany, Korea's inadequate transportation system had been impeding economic growth and adversely affecting prices. Park concluded that expressways were essential to rapid economic development.

The government subsequently offered the following rationale for building the expressway:

1. Seoul, Gyeonggi Province, Chungcheong Province, and Gyeongsang Province contained 63 percent of Korea's population, while the Seoul–Busan corridor accounted for 66 percent of gross national product and 81 percent of industrial output. The Kyungbu Expressway would connect these regions, improve transportation, and provide the conditions necessary for economic growth.

2. At the time, 81 percent of Korea's automobiles traveled on roads in the Seoul–Busan region.

3. The Seoul–Busan railway was heavily burdened, and road transport should relieve the pressure.

4. The port cities of Busan and Incheon handled 55 percent of Korea's seaborne trade. Connecting them by expressway would make imports and exports more efficient.

Building the road, however, would require far more money than imagined. Japan's Tokyo–Nagoya Expressway had cost 800 million yen per kilometer. At that rate, the estimated 438 kilometers from Seoul to Busan would require an investment of 350 billion won. This was more than twice the national budget of 164.3 billion won for the year. The immense cost was daunting, yet the project could not be abandoned.

How, then, could an expressway be built cheaply? To determine the actual cost of the Kyungbu Expressway, President Park summoned Chung Ju-yung, who had built a road in Thailand and undertaken projects in Vietnam, to the Blue House. He issued the same instruction to government departments, ordering each to calculate the required construction cost.

No one had ever built an expressway before. The Ministry of Construction estimated 65 billion won; the Seoul city government, 18 billion; the Army Corps of Engineers staff office, 49 billion; Hyundai Construction, 38 billion; and the Ministry of Finance, 33 billion. The Economic Planning Board declined to make an estimate.

With his experience building a highway in Thailand, Chung was confident he could calculate the necessary funds. After studying the terrain on a 1:50,000 map, he flew over the site in a small plane and concluded that construction would not present excessive difficulty. He also had the project diagrams he had brought back from Thailand.

President Park compared the varying estimates, from the low of 18 billion won to the high of 65 billion, including figures of 31.5 billion and 38 billion. He ultimately judged 30 billion won realistic. Adding a 10-percent contingency brought the total to 33 billion won—one-tenth the cost of Japan's Tokyo–Nagoya Expressway.[^TN-CH14-001]

As soon as the expressway was announced, criticism poured in: the policy favored the rich; the government cared only about economic development in Gyeongsang Province; the time was not yet right; and so on. Great undertakings always provoke disagreement and competing views. None of this shook President Park's determination. Korea's freight traffic was increasing daily, and an economic artery was needed to serve development. Chung agreed. He had experience in Thailand and Vietnam, and President Park

trusted him deeply.

Thirteen construction companies, including Samhwan, Daelim, Samboo, and Kukdong, participated. Hyundai Construction was responsible for 133 of the total 429 kilometers: 105 kilometers from Seoul to Osan and 28 from Daejeon to Okcheon. This was roughly two-fifths of the total route, but the Daejeon–Okcheon section was the most difficult part of the entire expressway.

On February 1, 1968, an explosion sounded near the first tollgate of the Kyungbu Expressway, marking the groundbreaking ceremony. President Park and Chung were both elated.

Chung resolved to finish the project in the shortest possible time without permitting any decline in quality. Because funding was tight, rapid completion was essential.

Once work began, Chung went to the site himself and lived there to supervise construction. The project stretched over such a long distance that inspections often left him short of sleep, and he would close his eyes for a few moments in a jolting jeep.

The most difficult section was the tunnel at Okcheon. The four kilometers between Susan-ri in Okcheon-myeon[^FB-F027] and Myogeum-ri in Yongsan-myeon, Yeongdong County, lay within the Sobaek Mountains. A tunnel had to be bored through the range. After about twenty meters had been excavated, the tunnel suddenly collapsed, leaving no time to escape. The geology consisted of extremely soft soil that caved in easily. Three laborers were killed and one injured. Rocks frequently fell from the mountain, and at times groundwater burst out like a fountain, sweeping away the tunnel workers. Progress was no more than two meters a day and, in difficult terrain, only thirty centimeters.

The work was grueling beyond measure, and this was the hardest section of the entire project. Only two months remained before the deadline. The tunnel's uphill lane totaled 590 meters and its downhill lane 530 meters, while 240 meters of the uphill lane remained to be excavated. At a rate of two meters a day, the work would require 120 days. The project was in crisis.

The minister of construction came from Seoul to the site every week, the director of the Civil Engineering Bureau visited every four days, and Chung simply took up residence there. President Park was constantly pressing for progress and anxiously awaiting news.

Chung made his decision. This was no time to consider whether the company would profit; the most important thing was to finish as quickly as possible. Whatever equipment the project required would be purchased regardless of price. Heavy machinery worth a total of 8 million dollars was acquired. At the time, Korea possessed only 1,400 pieces of heavy equipment, yet Chung purchased 1,900 for the expressway project.[^TN-CH14-002] The more new equipment he bought, the more unbalanced the company's finances became, but this was no longer the time to worry about that. In the end, work expected to take three months was completed in twenty-five days.

At eleven o'clock on the night of June 27, 1970, cries of “Manse!” rang out at the tunnel's southern end. The final and most difficult section of the Kyungbu Expressway had at last been broken through. This section took 290 days, completing the entire 429-kilometer route. The road cost 42.9 billion won—100 million won per kilometer. Although this was approximately 10 billion won over the government's original budget of 33 billion, it was only one-eighth the cost of Japan's Tokyo–Nagoya Expressway.

On July 7, 1970, two years and five months after construction began, the Kyungbu Expressway was completed. Built at the lowest cost and in the shortest time in the world, it had required a cumulative workforce of nine million and 165 pieces of equipment. President Park Chung Hee, government ministers and vice ministers, representatives of the construction companies, and all the on-site commanders attended the completion ceremony. Park decorated the site commanders, who wept without restraint. For two years, through wind and snow, they had disregarded danger to their own lives for the road. The tunnel section handled by Chung's Hyundai Construction suffered thirteen collapses, and many people lost their lives.

President Park, too, was overwhelmed with emotion. He poured champagne onto the roadway and thanked the construction workers.

Seventy-seven people lost their lives building this national transportation artery. The road was constructed with the Korean people's blood, sweat, and lives, as well as the spirit of entrepreneurs like Chung Ju-yung. A monument on the Chupungnyeong section bears these words: “This expressway was built with the full financial, material, and human resources of our impoverished country, and was completed in the shortest time in the history of expressway construction anywhere in the world.”

Korea's distances thereby shrank dramatically. The seeming miracle of eating breakfast in Seoul and lunch in Busan became reality. For people of that era, it had previously been unimaginable: before the expressway, even traveling by bus from Seoul to Daejeon took eight hours.

Chung and Hyundai deserved great credit for completing the Kyungbu Expressway. They were responsible for one-third of the route and for its most difficult section.

The achievement greatly strengthened Korean society's confidence that “if we do it, we can succeed.” The targets of 10 billion dollars in exports and 1,000 dollars in per capita income, originally set for 1980, were reached two years early.

By successfully building the Kyungbu Expressway, Chung won the government's absolute trust. Projects for Korea's power stations, dams, and other infrastructure now awaited him.

Lee Byung-chull founded the *JoongAng Ilbo* in 1965 and Tongyang Broadcasting Company in 1966.

The five-story building constructed in Seosomun for the *JoongAng Ilbo* and Tongyang Broadcasting may not sound tall today, but at the time it was among the most beautiful and technologically advanced buildings in Korea.

In the mid-1960s, many Koreans felt a surge of pride when they stood on Seosomun Boulevard and looked at the buildings lining both sides.

When a foreign president visited Korea, students lined the roads waving the Taegeukgi and the visitor's national flag in enthusiastic welcome. The place students visited most often for such occasions was Seosomun Boulevard.

Whenever the motorcades carrying a foreign president and President Park entered Seosomun Boulevard, students waved the two countries' flags together while confetti fell from rooftops. The author, too, once went to welcome visiting heads of state, including German president Lübke,

and waved a small national flag.

Seosomun was the road with the greatest concentration of tall buildings in the Republic of Korea. It was there that Tongyang Broadcasting was established.

The establishment of Tongyang Broadcasting Company, or TBC, transformed Korea's broadcasting landscape. At the time, Korea had no television station other than KBS.[^FB-F028] With only one broadcaster, viewers had no choice.

TBC's overwhelming ratings showed how compelling its programs were. One of its most popular shows was *Show Show Show*, hosted on Saturdays by the late Kwak Kyu-seok. The streets would empty as everyone stayed home to watch. Television sets were still rare, and whenever an entertaining program aired, people gathered at the home of anyone who owned one.

Beginning in 1966, when Tongyang Broadcasting was founded, the number of television sets in Korea increased sharply. Soldiers and technical workers returning from the Vietnam War brought American-made Zenith or Hunpin sets and Japanese Sanyo televisions. When bouts featuring boxer Kim Ki-soo or wrestler Kim Il were broadcast, homes with television sets became packed with people from the neighborhood. Yet even in Seoul, only one or two households in a neighborhood owned a television. Children unable to watch at home went to “television shops,” where two or three won bought admission to a Kim Ki-soo boxing match or a Kim Il wrestling bout. Whenever a match aired, the shops were packed.

Besides *Show Show Show*, Tongyang Broadcasting had several other popular programs, including *The Blue-Eyed Daughter-in-Law*, *Come Wearing Straw Sandals*, and *Song of Longing for Mother*.

After Munhwa Broadcasting Corporation began operations in 1969, competition for ratings intensified. Tongyang Broadcasting nevertheless remained in first place.

One year before establishing Tongyang Broadcasting, Lee founded the *JoongAng Ilbo*. Its launch also marked an advance for the newspaper industry.

While Chung Ju-yung worked on the Kyungbu Expressway to build the nation, Lee Byung-chull was advancing into cultural industries.

Trucks carrying Lee's sugar, woolen textiles, and other daily necessities also raced along the road Chung had built.

Korea was then alive with activity. The expressway had been completed; heavy industries such as shipbuilding and steel needed to be launched, while light industries producing the necessities of daily life also had to develop.

After the Meiji Restoration of 1868, Japan built steelworks in Nagasaki while also developing textile industries in Kobe and elsewhere—advancing light and heavy industry together. Using this as a model, Korea likewise moved forward on both fronts.

# Chung Ju-yung's Ships and Lee Byung-chull's Color Television[^TN-CH15-001]

Skilled workers, however, posed a serious problem. Having previously built only ships of twelve thousand tons, they now had to construct a

shipyard more than ten times that scale without the slightest experience.

Many people scoffed: how could they build ships when they did not even have a shipyard? They were mocking Chung Ju-yung for overestimating himself.

Chung's first task was to steady his people's nerves. He addressed his employees: “I will take responsibility for everything. Anyone who is afraid can go home and lie down.”

On August 24, 1999, Chung met Stavros Niarchos, chairman of the Greek shipping company Niarchos Group, at Hyundai Group's guesthouse in Seongbuk-dong after twenty-seven years apart. They enjoyed a cordial conversation. Niarchos, brother-in-law of the renowned Greek shipping magnate Aristotle Onassis,[^FB-F029] was himself a giant of Greek shipping and the first customer to order ships from Hyundai Shipbuilding. In 1972, he had contracted for two 260,000-ton oil tankers on the strength of nothing more than a photograph of a beach in Ulsan that Chung carried and plans for an oil tanker borrowed from someone else.

Niarchos had come to Korea to attend the naming ceremony the next day for Hyundai Heavy Industries' new oil tanker, the *Christina*. To thank the benefactor who had helped him thirty years earlier, Chairman Chung hosted a banquet for Niarchos, and they reminisced about the original ship order.

In September 1971, Chung decided to establish Korea's first shipyard. Hyundai Construction employed many specialists in machinery and electrical engineering, and he believed it would be safer to have them build ships worth hundreds of millions of dollars at home than to send them abroad as contract labor to earn foreign currency.

As a construction expert, Chung believed shipbuilding could not differ greatly from building construction. To him, it meant shaping steel plates into a hull and then installing equipment inside to propel the vessel.

Turning decisions into action was one of Chung's strengths. He began the preparations.

Building the shipyard would require approximately 80 million dollars. That may not seem an enormous sum today, but at the time it was astronomical. Korea's dollar market was in poor condition, and there was nowhere to borrow the money. Nor did Chung's proposed shipbuilding business arouse anyone's interest.

Chung planned to borrow from Mitsubishi Corporation in Japan. With Japan and China then approaching the establishment of diplomatic relations, Mitsubishi declined his request.

Never retreating once he had made up his mind, Chung flew to London in search of financing. London, New York, and Frankfurt were the places where the greatest concentrations of money in the world

were gathered.

All Chung brought with him was a photograph of Mipo Harbor, where he planned to build the shipyard: a desolate beach with only a few pine trees. In London, a financial center, attracting capital with a single photograph was impossible. He hurried from place to place seeking a loan.

Careful observation convinced him that Britain's major banks, which commanded global finance, followed Barclays Bank's lead. Barclays influenced London's bankers, and persuading it was therefore crucial.

Banks do not lend money lightly. Securing a loan required a project proposal and recommendation that met British standards. Chung commissioned the London firm A&P Appledore to prepare them, for a feasibility study and recommendation were the keys that could open the door to financing. A&P Appledore was itself doubtful about this obscure company from the Far East. Ultimately, its chairman, Lombardon, met Chung, and his distrust of the company from a developing country gradually faded.

“It will be difficult to apply for a loan if you do not yet have a buyer prepared to place an order. Barclays has serious doubts that Korea can actually build ships,” Chairman Lombardon said.

Chung pulled a 500-won banknote from his pocket.

“A turtle ship is printed here. It proves that we Koreans built an ironclad vessel in the sixteenth century. Britain's shipbuilding industry is generally said to have begun in the nineteenth century, so Korea was more than three hundred years ahead. This shows that Korea possesses great ability in shipbuilding. Our policy of national isolation merely slowed industrialization; in fact, the Korean people are diligent, resourceful, and full of potential for development.”

Moved by Chung's words, Lombardon arranged a meeting with a vice chairman of Barclays Bank.

They agreed to meet over a meal, leaving the question of the loan until last.

As soon as he sat down at the table, the Barclays vice chairman asked Chung, “What is your field of specialization?”

The question came without warning. Chung had attended only elementary school, yet he was being asked about his academic specialty.

“Vice Chairman, have you read the proposal we submitted?”

“Of course. I read it very carefully. It is extremely comprehensive and complete.”

“That is my specialty. In fact, I went to Oxford University yesterday and submitted this report as my application for a doctorate. After reading it, the professors awarded me a doctorate in economics on the spot. That proposal is my doctoral dissertation.”

Such was Chung Ju-yung's disarming humor.

Rather than explain that he had only completed elementary school, he demonstrated the breadth of his management experience and asked the other man to recognize it. Such was his confidence.

The vice chairman nodded. “It seems to me that your specialty is humor. Our bank will submit your proposal to the Export Credits Guarantee Department. Good luck.”

He had won this battle.

The story is now widely told, but according to the recollections of former Hyundai Group president Lee Myung-bak, it was only a small episode. A great deal of work had already gone into raising the funds and producing a detailed proposal, which ultimately won the bank's approval.

While the bank carefully read and examined the proposal, it also conducted a thorough investigation of Hyundai Group. The inquiry found Hyundai to be a group with enormous potential for development. With a leader as resourceful and courageous as Chung Ju-yung, it concluded that the company deserved the loan.

One final hurdle remained: approval from the loan-guarantee authority.

In Britain, a domestic bank could lend to a foreign enterprise only with a guarantee from the Export Credits Guarantee Department, or ECGD. To obtain that guarantee, the applicant had to present a ship-sale contract.

“Your shipyard proposal is excellent. But one question remains. If I wanted to order a ship, I would naturally go to a world-renowned shipyard, not to an inexperienced Korean yard. Without orders, to whom will you sell the ships you build? If they cannot be sold, how will you repay the money Britain lends you? Bring us proof that you can sell the ships. Until then, we cannot grant the loan.”

The head of Britain's Export Credits Guarantee Department had made a compelling point.

“Please do not worry. I need only bring you a purchase contract, do I not?” Chung declared boldly before leaving. In truth, the man's argument was so reasonable that Chung had been persuaded.

Where could he obtain an order?

The shipyard existed only on paper and had never produced a vessel. Who would sign a purchase contract with him? At one point, Chung even considered abandoning the shipyard project.

Then he gathered his thoughts. He had already arranged to borrow from Barclays and could not break his word. He had also promised President Park Chung Hee that he would establish the shipyard. This was a national undertaking and could not be abandoned. Hardship had been expected from the outset; whether success seemed possible or not, he had to persist to the end.

Carrying a photograph of several pine trees on a beach, Chung approached a succession of world-class shipping companies. Their owners thought him mad, which was an understandable reaction.

Then one man became interested in Chung's passion: Greek shipping magnate Stavros Niarchos. He offered to order two 260,000-ton vessels.

British-educated Niarchos was then thirty-six and in the prime of life. Having taken over the shipping business after his father's death, he was at the height of his momentum.

“It may be nothing but a beach now, but I will turn it into a successful shipyard and provide vessels at lower prices than other yards.”

Niarchos smiled at Chung's words. “Perhaps I am gambling, too. But your passion has moved me, and I have decided to sign the contract.”

Niarchos ordered two 260,000-ton vessels at a price of 30.35 million dollars each, to be delivered five years later. Chung received a deposit of 1.4 billion won.

The terms Chung promised Niarchos were also substantial. He

pledged to build high-quality ships and, if he failed to fulfill the contract, to return the deposit with interest.

Until then, the largest ship built in Korea had been only 17,000 tons. Now two 260,000-ton giants were to be produced at a Hyundai shipyard that had yet to be built.

Chung sent the order to the Export Credits Guarantee Department in London and thereby secured the funds needed to build the shipyard.

With financing in place, construction could begin. Skilled workers, however, posed a serious problem. Having previously built only ships of twelve thousand tons, they now had to construct a shipyard more than ten times that scale without the slightest experience.

Many people scoffed: how could they build ships when they did not even have a shipyard? They were mocking Chung for overestimating himself.

Chung's first task was to steady his people's nerves. He addressed his employees: “I will take responsibility for everything. Anyone who is afraid can go home and lie down.”

That was the essential spirit; nothing more needed to be said.

When the commander had placed himself at the front, what more was there to discuss? Construction of the Ulsan shipyard began. They learned while working, studied what they did not know, and stayed up through the night when time ran short. In this way, the shipyard formally began to take shape.

Everything began from zero. To deliver two 260,000-ton vessels by the date promised to Niarchos, they had to finish the yard quickly while leaving enough time to build the ships. Funds for the shipyard were borrowed through Barclays Bank from four countries, including France, West Germany, and Spain. The financing arrived; the docks and quay walls were completed; factory buildings covering 140,000 pyeong were erected; and housing for five thousand people had to be constructed. Everything proceeded at a frantic pace.

After one year and three months, the shipyard was finally completed on March 22, 1972.[^TN-CH15-002] Occupying six hundred thousand pyeong, it had annual capacity of seven hundred thousand tons and two docks in the seven-hundred-thousand-ton class. In the shortest possible time, Chung had built the world's largest shipyard.

While building the yard, Hyundai also studied how to construct the two 260,000-ton vessels. Now that the shipyard was complete, all that remained was to build the ships Niarchos had ordered. Yet Chung did not stop there. In its first year after completion, Hyundai Shipbuilding received orders totaling 518,000 tons and immediately captured 1.75 percent of the world shipbuilding market. Chung's business acumen was remarkable.

Conditions improved further in 1973, the second year. Orders in the global shipbuilding industry increased two and a half times over the previous year. It was a year of abundance for shipbuilders.

Maritime transport naturally expanded sharply as well. The world's major shipping companies mainly ordered oil tankers, the most profitable segment. Hyundai Shipbuilding accordingly moved toward tanker construction and received orders for twelve very large crude carriers, or VLCCs.

In 1973, it completed 1.947 million tons and increased its share of the world market to 2.64 percent. This was attributed to the oil crisis, which had relatively lowered prices in the principal shipbuilding countries.

In 1974, Hyundai next received orders for two 260,000-ton VLCCs as well as smaller multipurpose cargo ships, raising its market share to 3 percent. Six of the orders came from Japan, accounting for more than half the total.

Having won so many orders from Japan, then the world's largest shipbuilding nation, Chung saw even greater prospects and began expanding the yard. His premise was that only mass production could maximize competitiveness. Hyundai Shipbuilding formally began its expansion in 1973.

The new third dock was ninety-two meters wide, designed in preparation for even larger vessels. The shipyard's factory buildings expanded from an initial thirty or forty thousand pyeong to eighty-eight thousand pyeong, roughly doubling in size. Steel-processing capacity rose from two hundred thousand to five hundred thousand tons. This was more than an expansion; it amounted to building another shipyard. The government had just declared its ambition to make Korea a heavy-industry power, providing substantial support for shipbuilding.

Just as Hyundai Shipbuilding was expanding at full speed, the first oil crisis struck and the world economy suddenly stalled. Trade between countries plummeted, plunging the shipping industry into crisis. In 1974, global shipbuilding faced overcapacity. By the end of 1973, tankers, which accounted for 40 percent of world shipping tonnage,

were already in surplus. After the oil crisis, the world's major energy-importing countries introduced measures to curb petroleum consumption, sharply reducing the volume of oil transported. The global shipbuilding industry, centered on tankers, consequently faced severe difficulties. Hyundai Shipbuilding, which had devoted itself to tanker production, suffered enormous losses. It possessed experience only in building tankers and had no other path available. The company was beset by crisis.

As the world economy faltered, many customers refused delivery of ships already under contract. The principal cases were vessel 7302, a 260,000-ton tanker ordered by Niarchos of Greece, and vessels 7308 and 7310, ordered by Ireland Shipping Company of Hong Kong.

Vessel 7302, ordered by Niarchos, was launched and named in June 1974 and fully completed that November. Because of the oil crisis, however, Niarchos decided not to accept delivery: if he bought it, no customer would charter it.

Niarchos began pointing out problems here and there to delay completion. If the vessel could not be delivered by the contract date, he could cancel the agreement and recover his deposit with interest.

Every time he identified a defect, Hyundai's workers labored through the night to satisfy his demand. Just as delivery seemed imminent with no problems remaining, he pointed out another deficiency that could not possibly be corrected before the deadline.

After confirming that this was Niarchos's final demand, Chung began the correction. The ship was completed one day past the contract date, and Niarchos refused delivery on the grounds that the deadline had been exceeded. Hyundai appealed to an international arbitration commission. Refusing a 260,000-ton vessel over “a delay of only one day” seemed absurd to anyone who heard it.

The completed ship Niarchos had ordered remained moored at the quay. Oh Won-chul, the Blue House's second economic secretary, believed that leaving a finished vessel idle represented a national loss, so he put it to use carrying oil from the Gulf region. Hyundai then won its case against Niarchos. Thirty years later, Chung invited him to Korea. The two men met again at Hyundai Group's guesthouse. Niarchos, once a thirty-six-year-old young man, was now approaching seventy, while Chung was over eighty. They talked for a long time.

Most people would never resume contact after relations had been broken off, yet Chung was able to invite Niarchos back to Korea. He was a giant not only in business but also in human relationships.

# Special Transport to the Middle East and Samsung Electronics

Business is grounded in reality and operates through action. Clever people cannot build anything great through ideas alone; they must act first.

—Chung Ju-yung

If Hyundai Group's principal businesses today are automobiles, construction, and shipbuilding, Samsung's main fields are Samsung Electronics, Samsung Trading, and Samsung Insurance. Samsung Electronics in particular has recently grown tremendously and achieved remarkable results.

Samsung Electronics is the most profitable Korean enterprise. Its net income in 2000 was 9 trillion won, and it made an enormous contribution to Korea's exports. Semiconductors accounted for a considerable share, but the basic achievement belonged to Samsung Electronics.

While Hyundai chairman Chung Ju-yung was working tirelessly to establish shipbuilding, Lee Byung-chull formally entered the electronics industry.

After founding the parent company Samsung Electronics on January 13, 1969, Lee invested 50 percent of the capital, Japan's Sanyo Electric invested 40 percent, and Sumitomo Corporation invested 10 percent. With total capital of 50 million dollars, Samsung-Sanyo Electric was established on December 4 that year and merged into Samsung Electronics in 1977.

After the Korea Fertilizer incident of 1967, Lee often traveled abroad, gathering information about new business opportunities as he went.

During a year and three months of seclusion in Yongin, he considered how to recover from the loss of Korea Fertilizer and conducted meticulous research to create a new opportunity.

Upon returning to active management, Lee immediately added a development division to Samsung Trading and began promoting an electronics venture. A master of management, he had found his breakthrough after a year and three months of reflection. Once he set the direction, he devoted all his strength to achieving the goal.

The development division's research showed that by the late 1960s, Japan's electronics industry was rapidly catching up with Europe and the United States, while Taiwan was also advancing strongly. Several Korean companies had entered the field, but the country still depended chiefly on imports and remained at a low level of development. Quality was poor and prices extraordinarily high. Black-and-white televisions cost more than ordinary wage earners could contemplate. Mass production of electronic goods to meet popular demand had become a practical necessity.

The study concluded that electronics best matched Korea's development needs in every respect: technology, labor, added value, domestic demand, and export prospects. Lee resolved to enter the market, popularize electronic products at home, and prepare for overseas exports.

Having made his decision, Lee traveled to Japan to see its electronics industry firsthand. Accompanied by Sanyo Electric chairman Toshio Iue, he visited an electronics industrial park in Tokyo. Iue was the brother-in-law of Konosuke Matsushita, chairman of the Matsushita Group, one of Japan's largest corporations. The two businesses had originally been one company before going their separate ways.

The Matsushita Group was among Japan's most distinctly Japanese enterprises and one of the world's outstanding corporate successes. Sanyo Electric, which had separated from it, was equally characteristic of Japan.

Sanyo's electronics industrial park in Tokyo covered four hundred thousand pyeong, a scale unimaginable in Korea at the time.

Iue said, “The raw material for silicon is sand. Chips made from silicon are installed in television sets. Electronics truly creates something from nothing—it is an industry with 99-percent added value.”

Lee was already considering how to build an electronics complex larger than Sanyo's. Though normally cautious and thorough, he could sometimes be extraordinarily bold. The field matched his prior research: in technology, labor, added value, domestic demand, and export prospects, electronics suited Korea's economic conditions.

Although a few Korean companies already worked in electronics, they merely imported foreign components for processing. Technological innovation and mass production could therefore transform the field into a high-value-added industry.

The Korean government also believed the electronics industry had to be fostered for export.

President Park Chung Hee's 1967 New Year's address was titled “An All-Out Advance toward an Industrial Nation.” Like the heading of a military proclamation, it announced a march toward industrialization, with full employment and one billion dollars in exports as the goals for the 1970s.

Immediately after returning from Japan, Lee purchased four hundred thousand pyeong of land outside Suwon. Public opinion claimed that Samsung, after failing with Korea Fertilizer, was turning to real-estate

speculation. Even within Samsung's development division, people wondered how large a factory could require so much land.

Lee, however, had already calculated the factory's scale in detail. He merely smiled bitterly at accusations of real-estate speculation. In his youth, he had suffered a huge loss in that very field.

He had once purchased four hundred thousand pyeong on the Gimhae Plain from a Japanese owner named Amano, earning the name “lord of ten thousand seok.” He then borrowed from banks to continue investing in real estate. When the Second Sino-Japanese War broke out in 1937, bank lending ceased and he suffered enormous losses. From that experience he learned the valuable lesson that “sudden wealth brings misfortune.”

He also came to understand the essence of “management.” The Chinese character *gyeong* meant drawing a line with a cord or thread; *yeong* meant building up beside the line. Management thus meant defining the scale and making a plan before constructing a building or road.

Lee thereafter managed and applied this principle in every enterprise. Knowing the limits of one's ability and never undertaking business beyond them became an iron rule.

Building a factory on 450,000 pyeong of land was therefore no extravagance. On January 13, 1969, he established Samsung Industrial Co. Even then, he foresaw the future of semiconductors. Ten years later, in 1984,[^TN-CH16-001] his prediction became reality with completion of Samsung's semiconductor plant. Another decade after that, Samsung Semiconductor became Korea's leading exporter.

Lee possessed eyes capable of seeing the future, and this ability led him always to prepare detailed plans in advance.

At a January 1968 gathering of employees from across Samsung, Lee said, “Two years ago, when Korea Fertilizer became state property, Samsung employees who had labored day and night experienced disappointment and pain. Samsung has faced hardship ever since. But no one other than we ourselves can overcome it. This year we take the first step. To restore Samsung's strength and find a new path, we need every employee's cooperation and effort.”

Within the government, Oh Won-chul, the Blue House's second economic secretary, reported an electronics-promotion plan to President Park. On June 19 that year, the government announced an eight-year program to foster the electronics industry.

Lee nevertheless encountered resistance when entering electronics. Fifty-nine existing firms, united in the Korea Electronics Industry Association, petitioned the government against Samsung's move. Their stated reason was that “the Samsung Group's proposed joint investment in electronics will not develop the industry but remain an assembly operation; we oppose this form of joint venture.” On the surface, their objection concerned technical support from Japan's Sanyo. In substance, they did not want to surrender domestic market share to the late-arriving Samsung Group.

Samsung proposed selling 15 percent of its output domestically and exporting 85 percent, but the existing firms refused to accept even the 15 percent. The government ultimately allowed Samsung-Sanyo Electric to be established on condition that all its products be exported.

Although a late entrant, Lee was determined to create a world-class electronics company.

The factory sites covered 450,000 pyeong in Maetan-dong, Suwon, and 700,000 pyeong in the Gacheon area of Ulju County, South Gyeongsang Province. Samsung's vast land purchases fueled rumors of speculation. Korea's largest factories then occupied only tens of thousands of pyeong. Lee bought so much space because he intended to build a factory larger than that of his partner, Sanyo.

In 1972, after two years of construction, Lee and twelve hundred workers began producing electric fans and black-and-white televisions.

Samsung's early electric fans often broke in the middle when moved, prompting consumer protests. In the mid-1970s, electronic watches made under Chairman Lee's direction also frequently malfunctioned because of their transistors.

After many difficulties, Samsung exported fifty-two thousand black-and-white televisions to the United States. Its early operations lost money, and Samsung Electronics became profitable only after it was allowed to sell domestically at the end of 1973. It subsequently expanded from black-and-white televisions to

color televisions, refrigerators, washing machines, vacuum cleaners, and other home appliances, becoming one of the world's leading electronics companies.

Entrepreneurs possess the quality of finishing well what they begin. Once Lee started something, he insisted on bringing it to a perfect conclusion.

There are only two ways to accomplish this: conduct exhaustive research and formulate a perfect plan before beginning, or persist through every difficulty until final victory. In truth, both are indispensable to any undertaking.

In temperament, Lee and Chung each possessed both qualities. Lee, fond of meticulous investigation, tended to formulate a complete plan before acting. Chung's stubborn character made his ability to overcome hardship more conspicuous.

When the Korea Fertilizer incident erupted in September 1966, Lee declared, “I will give Korea Fertilizer to the nation and no longer manage any enterprise.” He then secluded himself at Yongin Nature Park,[^FB-F030] but for an entrepreneur this was no vacation; he was thinking and searching for another opportunity.

Lee Chang-eop, then president of Cheil Sugar, visited Lee at the park. After they had toured the grounds, Lee said,

“I am testing my vitality and creativity. I work without pause to preserve them. I hate idleness. That is why I enjoy challenges and discipline. An entrepreneur's ability does not lie in how much money he earns, but in whether he can plan a competitive company. That is what has the greatest value.”

If Lee began every enterprise with meticulous planning, Chung struggled continuously in the real world to overcome difficulties.

The first oil crisis erupted in 1973.

A barrel of crude oil that had cost only 1.75 dollars rose to 10 dollars in less than two years, an increase of more than fivefold.

For Korea, which produced not a drop of oil, the situation was especially severe. Streetlights and shop neon signs went dark. Nighttime streets were black, people hurried home, and each household lit only a single lamp.

The oil crisis caused the world economy to deteriorate sharply, and Korea was no exception. Inflation followed. By 1975, enterprises had lost the ability to repay foreign debt and faced bankruptcy. Hyundai was no exception.

Hyundai Construction had invested heavily in the Ulsan shipyard, with money flowing in and none returning because no ships had yet been completed. Its finances were dire, and Chung needed a new way forward.

To catch fish, one must cast the net where fish are plentiful. Where was that? The Middle East, which produced and sold crude oil. Oil prices there were equally high, but dollars were abundant. Petroleum producers such as Saudi Arabia held vast dollar reserves and were investing heavily in ports, roads, housing, and other expansion projects.

Chung could not miss the opportunity; wherever money was plentiful, he would go. “The way forward is in the Middle East. To earn dollars, we must go there.” He believed that “only in deep water can one catch a big fish.”

He first opened Arabic classes within the company and screened Arabic-language films for employees. They began learning the language, for money could be earned only if they could communicate.

Chung assembled his senior aides and proposed entering the Middle East. His younger brother Chung In-yung, then vice president for international affairs, strongly objected: “I oppose it. The Middle East is dangerous, and its construction market is controlled by a handful of the world's largest contractors. Our company is unknown; there will be little room for us to survive.”

This was true. Construction in the Middle East was dominated by large corporations from the United States, the Netherlands, Britain, France, Japan, and other countries. Hyundai then handled projects worth only ten or twenty million dollars in total. Middle Eastern projects were enormous and competition fierce, demanding exceptional strength.

Chung In-yung was cautious and believed it was too early to enter. Chung Ju-yung also

knew clearly that Hyundai Construction was not yet strong enough to contend in the Middle East.

Hyundai had previously received invitations to bid on several projects in Kuwait, but the cautious Chung In-yung was pessimistic. On his advice, Hyundai declined, fearing a repeat of the Narathiwat Highway losses in Thailand. Yet abandoning the bids solved nothing.

Billions of dollars were moving through the Middle East, and the opportunity could not be surrendered again, especially while the oil crisis was placing the company under severe financial pressure. Entering any region required first preparing the way through personal relationships. The Middle East was no exception.

“Did I not tell you to leave for the Middle East at once? Why are you still here?” While the shipyard was under construction, Chung telephoned Seoul headquarters from the site whenever he had time and erupted in anger. Strangely, no one moved. He later learned that Chung In-yung had strictly forbidden subordinates to go, declaring that anyone who did would be removed from the company. Employees were caught between the brothers and did not know whom to obey.

“Are you truly not going to the Middle East? Leave immediately!” One day, Chung returned to Seoul headquarters and ordered Kwon Ki-tae, an executive responsible for international affairs, to depart. With no alternative, Kwon bought a ticket to Tokyo that day, intending to transfer there for the Middle East. As soon as he landed, however, a Hyundai employee stationed in Tokyo handed him a telegram instructing him to call Chung In-yung at once.

He called Seoul headquarters immediately. Chung In-yung asked urgently, “Are you in Japan now? Are you going to the Middle East?”

“Yes.”

“It is my elder brother's order, so there is nothing to be done. But even after you arrive, report everything to me. I will make the judgment and then report to the chairman.” After repeatedly cautioning Kwon, Chung In-yung allowed him to proceed. When Chung Ju-yung heard, he devised a response.

This was no time for hesitation; otherwise, no opportunity would ever arrive. Hyundai had more overseas construction experience than any company in Korea. It had built a highway in Thailand and worked amid the gunfire of the Vietnam War. It had continued through torrential rain, dense jungle, and flying bullets, yet no one knew Korean construction companies. That was absurd. If experience was insufficient, a solution could be found; if capability was lacking, they could work day and night. Try first and solve each problem after beginning. Such was Chung's thinking.

He began reorganizing personnel. He sent Chung In-yung to a newly established heavy-equipment manufacturing company under the military and removed every executive who opposed entering the Middle East, rebuilding the team. Chung personally assumed overall responsibility and appointed the obedient, effective Kwon Ki-tae vice president for international affairs.

After dispatching Kwon, Hyundai first won an 80-million-dollar contract for the Bandar Abbas training shipyard in Iran. In October, it secured the ASRY[^FB-F031] repair-yard project in Bahrain. A market believed to be dominated by world-class contractors did not seem impossible after all.

If so, why not challenge an even larger project? Hyundai had possessed no experience when it built the Narathiwat Highway in Thailand, nor when it worked in Vietnam. Construction sometimes brought losses. Nothing could be done if inadequate ability caused them, but after paying high tuition, one had to acquire knowledge and lessons in return.

Was there no larger project? As Hyundai actively searched for information, one appeared: the Jubail oil port in Saudi Arabia. Saudi Arabia planned a massive port on a beach called Jubail in the Persian Gulf. The project was worth 930 million dollars, equivalent to Korea's entire annual national budget. Until then, Saudi Arabia had signed only twenty-three construction projects in total, worth 780 million dollars. Jubail was a world-scale undertaking.

Jubail Port was to accommodate four 500,000-ton oil tankers at once. Five hundred thousand tons represented the world's largest tankers, each the size of five Jamsil Stadiums.[^FB-F032] The port would hold not one but four such vessels, requiring an immense wharf and super-large berthing facilities. In short, it was one of the twentieth century's greatest construction projects—a project builders said appeared only once in several centuries.

The greatest difficulty was drilling into bedrock thirty meters below the seabed and mounting enormous steel structures on it. Bidding would begin in seven months.

World-renowned contractors from the United States, Britain, West Germany, the Netherlands, and elsewhere eagerly awaited the opportunity. They had heard about it a year earlier and had long since begun lobbying.

Saudi Arabia's Ministry of Posts and Telecommunications oversaw the project and entrusted the selection of ten qualified bidders to the British consulting firm William Halcrow. The process was conducted in absolute secrecy.

Hyundai learned that nine companies had already qualified: Brown & Root and Raymond International of the United States; Costain Civil Engineering and Tarmac of Britain; Boskalis and Philipp Holzmann of West Germany; Ballast Nedam of the Netherlands; and Spie Batignolles[^FB-F033] of France. Japan's largest contractor, Taisei Corporation, was also pressing forcefully to enter.

Korea's foreign-exchange reserves were then at their most critical point. President Park Chung Hee took an intense interest in Hyundai's pursuit of the project because the nation needed foreign currency. Only one company could win the opportunity.

“You must secure qualification to bid!” Chung instructed Eum Yong-gi, a director in Hyundai's London office. A graduate in economics from Seoul National University, Eum had joined Hyundai Construction in 1966 and risen to director nine years later. In a word, he was capable and highly effective.

After later serving in construction, trading, heavy industry, general trading, and Mipo Shipyard, Eum became chief executive of Livart,[^FB-F034] a Hyundai Group company. He now serves as chief director of the venture company Innotive

Corporation.

Director Eum accepted the heavy responsibility and went to lobby William Halcrow.

“With your country's help, we built the world's largest shipyard in Ulsan. We are now working in the Middle East on the Bandar Abbas training shipyard in Iran and the ASRY repair yard in Bahrain. We hope you will give our company an opportunity this time.”

Fortunately, William Halcrow was British. Eum showed the firm materials describing the assistance A&P Appledore and Barclays Bank had provided when Hyundai built its shipyard. Halcrow wavered and admitted Hyundai to the ten qualified bidders. Then another problem arose.

The bid bond was 20 million dollars. With Korea's foreign-exchange reserves in dire condition, borrowing that sum from a bank was impossible. Raising it locally was extremely difficult, and because of the enormous amount, the process had to remain strictly confidential. Only four days before the deadline did Hyundai finally borrow a 26.4-million-dollar guarantee from Bahrain International Bank.

Meanwhile, a bidding team led by Jeon Gap-won, Park Se-yong, Eum Yong-gi, and others prepared the documents.

Park Se-yong was Hyundai Construction's sales director. After graduating in business from Yonsei University, he joined Hyundai Construction in 1967 and distinguished himself in overseas marketing and planning. He had an extraordinary memory and, once a decision was made, drove ahead like a bulldozer. He later chaired Incheon Iron and Steel, Hyundai Corporation, and Hyundai Merchant Marine. Work filled his mind completely. Strong commanders attract strong subordinates: Chung Ju-yung, Jeon Gap-won, Eum Yong-gi, and Park Se-yong prepared the bid day and night, forgetting both sleep and food.

Bidding on overseas construction was far from easy. It required a spirit of challenge, technical support, good relations with the client country, and financing capacity.

They knew this battle would determine whether Hyundai Construction could join the ranks of the world's renowned companies. Their objective was to win by every possible means.

In their Riyadh hotel, they forbade anyone to leave and prepared documents day and night. They ordered meals in, neither bathing nor cutting their hair. After a week, leftover food soured in its bowls, and the local heat combined with their unwashed bodies to fill the room with a stench. Like athletes who avoid bathing before a major competition, they treated it as a way to prevent themselves from relaxing. At last, they completed a bid of more than one hundred pages.

When the documents were ready, everyone, beginning with Chung, stepped over the bid in turn and then pressed it tightly against his body. They all believed this would bring good luck and success.

The project estimate was 1.2 billion dollars, and the contract would go to the lowest bidder. Hyundai needed the lowest price that would not produce a loss.

Chung considered 1.2 billion too high. He cut 25 percent, leaving 900 million dollars, then decided even that was excessive and reduced it by another 5 percent. The final planned bid was 870 million dollars.

Chung believed no company would bid as low as one billion dollars. Even if one did, Hyundai would still be 130 million lower, so he expected no problem.

Senior Managing Director Jeon Gap-won, who directed the planning, believed the bid was too low. Chung nevertheless ordered 870 million dollars and shortened the original forty-four-month schedule by eight months.

At ten o'clock on February 16, 1975, the Saudi Ministry of Posts and Telecommunications official responsible for the tender appeared and announced that the companies' bids would be accepted for five minutes.

Jeon entered the bidding room and submitted Hyundai's documents. When he emerged, his face was rigid.

“What happened, Senior Managing Director Jeon? Did you write the number incorrectly?” Chung asked.

“No,” Jeon answered evasively.

Suspicious, Chung continued questioning him.

Jeon hesitated, then finally explained: he had always believed 870 million dollars was too low, so when signing the final bid he changed it on his own to 931.14 million dollars. It was unimaginable—he had disobeyed the president's order and altered the figure without authorization.

“Then we are in danger...” But what was done could not be undone. All they could do was wait in silence.

The result came at three that afternoon. Everyone waited breathlessly. The announcement was: “The American construction company Brown & Root has won with a bid of 904.44 million dollars.”

Chung stood there devastated, and Jeon appeared almost unconscious with misery. Just then, the door to a small conference room opened. Jeong Mun-do of the financing team flashed Chung a V sign, his face beaming.

“What happened?” Chung demanded repeatedly.

“We won!”

Brown & Root's 904.44-million-dollar figure covered only the offshore tanker-berthing facilities, not the entire project. The announcement that it had won was immediately voided.

The result was announced again: “Hyundai has proposed a cost of 931.14 million dollars for the Jubail project, with complete documentation. In particular, it has offered to shorten the projected forty-four-month schedule by eight months. Hyundai wins the tender.”

Chung felt as though he could fly. Minutes earlier he had hated the sight of Jeon's face; now he wanted to embrace him. Jeon's loyalty had not only earned the company an additional 60 million dollars but also won the tender. That evening, Chung awarded special bonuses to every adviser who had labored over the bid.

One week later, an advance-payment check for 700 million riyals[^FB-F035] arrived in Korea. The country's foreign-exchange reserves reached an all-time high, and Hyundai became Korea's leading exporter.

All that remained was to execute the project carefully. Hyundai began reviewing the required equipment, materials, and workforce. Hiring labor presented no problem, but the volume of construction materials was enormous: the concrete alone would require two hundred thousand five-ton truckloads, while the steel would fill twelve ten-thousand-ton ships.

To ensure timely supply, Chung reviewed transportation and procurement within Korea and,

to guarantee the supply of steel structures and offshore equipment, required close cooperation from Hyundai Shipbuilding. The other imperative was strict adherence to the thirty-six-month schedule.

The greatest problem was manufacturing and transporting the steel structures required for tanker berths. Eighty-nine enormous units were needed. Their size was staggering: each was twenty meters long, eighteen meters wide, thirty-six meters high, and weighed 550 tons—the equivalent of a ten-story building. And there were eighty-nine of them.

They would be manufactured at Hyundai's Ulsan shipyard and then transported across the typhoon-prone Philippine Sea, through Southeast Asia and the monsoon-swept Indian Ocean, to the Persian Gulf. How to accomplish such a journey became an immense problem.

Manufacturing them in Saudi Arabia would require a large factory and imported steel, making production at Hyundai's own Ulsan yard preferable.

The final decision was to transport the structures from Ulsan to the Persian Gulf by barge. The distance to Jubail was twelve thousand kilometers. Distance alone was a problem, but the greater danger was passing through Southeast Asia, where typhoons could strike at any time. Nothing like it had been attempted in construction history, yet Chung decided to proceed.

Transporting all the units to Jubail would require nineteen voyages. A single failure could inflict enormous losses on the project or ruin it altogether.

Chung's advisers recommended marine insurance. But the undertaking was unprecedented even for renowned foreign insurers, and the danger led them to quote astronomical premiums. Insurance might cost more than the risk itself, and collecting a payout would take so long that the entire project would be delayed. Another solution was needed.

First, they planned how to prevent the steel structures from sinking. If a typhoon blew a vessel away, where would it go? Study of the currents indicated that it would eventually drift toward Taiwan, where it could be recovered and towed back.

Only the decision remained. “That is what we will do!” they concluded. It was unimaginable to ordinary people.

The operation ultimately required three ten-thousand-horsepower tugboats, three twenty-thousand-ton barges, and three five-thousand-ton barges. These vessels were built first and then

formed into fleets to tow the steel structures.

For safety at sea, the vessels were equipped with computer hardware and programs capable of forecasting waves and typhoons in Southeast Asian and Indian Ocean waters. The barges set sail.

Each voyage took thirty-five days, with one departure each month. The first seven reached Jubail safely. On the eighth, a barge collided with a Taiwanese merchant vessel near Singapore. Fortunately, only a pipe on one unit broke, and immediate action prevented a serious accident. On another voyage, a typhoon swept away a barge. Just as predicted, it was found off Taiwan, towed back, and returned to service. All nineteen voyages were completed safely.

Chung Ju-yung was Chung Ju-yung. He possessed the character and ability of a giant, as though he could hold the earth in his hands.

The Saudi client was astonished. Through this project, Hyundai Group advanced dramatically in transportation, civil engineering, underwater work, equipment installation, and heavy-industry technology. It also demonstrated the strength of Korean civil engineering to the world. Chung created both a legend and a miracle.

Hyundai's successful entry into the Middle East enabled Korea to earn vast amounts of foreign currency there. Contracts totaled only 750 million dollars in 1975, the year of the Jubail agreement, but tripled to 2.4 billion in 1976, then reached 3.4 billion in 1977, 8 billion in 1978, 6 billion in 1979, 7.8 billion in 1980, 12.6 billion in 1981, 113 dollars in 1982, 9 billion in 1983, and 5.9 billion in 1982.[^TN-CH16-002] There is no doubt that the foreign currency earned during this period played a major role in Korea's economy.

Between 1966 and 1980, Hyundai's overseas construction contracts totaled 7.26 billion dollars over fifteen years, accounting for 26.7 percent of Korea's total overseas construction and consistently ranking first.

Middle Eastern construction projects not only contributed enormously to Hyundai's development but also laid a solid foundation for Korea's entry into the international construction market.

Korean construction companies later undertook world-class projects including Malaysia's Penang Bridge, Singapore's Raffles City, expressways in Indonesia, and Malaysia's ninety-two-story Petronas Twin Towers.

# Think Before Acting and Act Before Thinking

If business were the game of Go, Lee Byung-chull would be a master of opening strategy. He designed the blueprint for an enterprise from the outset and advanced step by step, rarely making a major mistake.

Chung Ju-yung, by contrast, was the type who attacked from the beginning. He enjoyed taking risks: when he won, he won completely; when he lost, he lost disastrously.

Lee, the master strategist, built on a firm foundation; Chung, the master attacker, gathered tremendous strength as he advanced.

In business, Lee therefore seldom failed. Chung appeared weak at first, but grew stronger over time until he achieved success.

In managing enterprises, Lee preferred to conduct exhaustive advance research before beginning anything. Chung, as the Jubail Port project demonstrated, tended to achieve his goals through singular imagination, the power to break through barriers, and exceptional drive.

Lee leaned more toward logical thought than imagination, and more toward caution than forceful breakthroughs.

In May 1975, Lee published an article in the “Looking Back on Business” column of the *Seoul Economic Daily*, summarizing what he had learned from running enterprises.

“The first consideration in starting a business must be whether the nation and its people need it. Only then should one consider profits, capital, personnel, technology, and other factors, and undertake what lies within one's capabilities.”

This was Lee's view of enterprise. As he said, before beginning anything he preferred to think deeply and investigate repeatedly. Only after completing the blueprint did he put it into practice. His ability to envision an enterprise's future was extraordinary. He knew that a flawed blueprint would affect the whole business.

“The most important things in management are advance preparation and planning. If a failure to think matters through beforehand causes a cash-flow crisis or blocks sales channels, the manager can only be called incapable. I suffered losses in real estate because I failed to ‘manage’ properly from the beginning. One must also know the limits of one's abilities and never exceed them.”

Lee meant that had he understood the essence of management at the time, he would not have made those misguided investments. His theory was that an enterprise could not be called successful unless its financing and sales plans were sound from the outset.

Chung saw matters differently.

“Every aspect of life deserves affirmation. Whatever goal one pursues, making the effort itself means success.”

Chung was an optimist. His resolute entry into the Middle Eastern market despite opposition from his brother Chung In-yung and many senior advisers reflected that optimistic nature.

He viewed the Middle East optimistically and knew that the essential issues were “oil and dollars.” He made entry into that market his greatest objective and worked actively to realize it, seeking opportunities for ever larger

projects.

Lee was extremely cautious. Chung always said, “No problem.” If he could not find one solution, he searched for another.

Like an artist, he creatively drove many undertakings to completion in order to open new space for an enterprise's development.

Viewed as art, Lee favored a logical style, while Chung approached an objective style. Lee was “still”; Chung was “in motion.”

With business theory as a foundation, an artistic pyramid could be built upon it.

Seen this way, Lee possessed a horizontal mode of thought, while Chung possessed a vertical one. Where horizontal and vertical met, they formed a solid and beautiful triangular pyramid.

The Korean economy could not do without Lee and Chung precisely because horizontal and vertical combined in them. By producing daily necessities, Lee achieved popularization on the horizontal plane. Through construction, Chung created spatial structures. Together, these two structures transformed Korean life.

# Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung Compete in Semiconductors

Samsung's characteristic approach was for Chairman Lee Byung-chull or his employees to conduct long-term research and preparation. Hyundai's

was to issue an order, form a task force immediately, and work day and night toward the goal.

Some Japanese businesspeople described Hyundai's fearsome nature as resembling a rugby ball whose direction of flight could not be seen. Japanese companies were often left helpless because Hyundai's course was impossible to predict. This was Chung Ju-yung's drive and Hyundai's advantage.

Hotel Okura in Tokyo's Minato ward was one of Japan's “Big Three” hotels, together with the Imperial Hotel and Hotel New Otani. The expression originally referred to branches of Tokugawa Ieyasu's family; here, it meant the country's top three hotels. The founder, Kihachiro Okura, had been one of Japan's three great zaibatsu leaders. Hotel Okura formed a partnership with Samsung's Shilla Hotel, which modeled much of its service on the Japanese hotel.

In a deluxe suite at Hotel Okura, Chairman Lee worked through the night. He had lost count of how many nights it had been.

In March 1982, the previous year, he had visited the United States and recognized that world markets were shifting from large scale toward flexible, smaller-scale production. Upon returning, he instructed Managing Director Kim Kwang-ho, now vice chairman of Samsung Electronics, to prepare a study of semiconductors. Seven months later, Kim submitted a one-hundred-page report.

Lee was now reading it. Kim wrote, “In advanced semiconductor technology, Japan leads the United States in memory technology.” Lee reasoned that if Japan could surpass America, Korea could do so as well.

Memory-semiconductor production required exceptionally delicate manual skill. Koreans, like Japanese people, ate with chopsticks, so the author believed this posed little problem: thousands of years of chopstick use had made Korean fingers dexterous. Production also demanded an immaculate, dust-free environment. The author argued that Koreans could provide this because, unlike Americans, they customarily removed their shoes before entering a home. Production, then, was not the problem. Capital was. A single production line required one trillion won. Lee nevertheless decided to proceed.

Why did he risk so much on semiconductors? Lee had long dreamed of becoming the world's leading home-appliance manufacturer.

In 1982, Samsung Group's total sales were 6 billion dollars, while Philips alone sold 21 billion dollars in home appliances. In that field alone, Philips was four times Samsung's size. For Samsung Electronics to become number one in home appliances, it had to break through in semiconductors, the core technology of those products. Later events showed how farsighted Lee's move was.

Samsung's 1982 sales were only 25 percent of Philips's, but by 1995 Samsung reached 50 billion dollars, twice Philips's sales.[^TN-CH18-001] Semiconductors and electronics were Samsung's core businesses.

At dawn on February 3, 1983, Lee telephoned Seoul. He called Hong Jin-ki, chairman of the *JoongAng Ilbo*.

“I have decided to enter semiconductors. Nothing anyone says will sway me now. Announce it immediately.”

Lee delivered the instruction slowly but firmly. His voice carried strength as well as anguish. He had spent forty-five years painstakingly building businesses since founding Samsung Trading Company in 1938. He was an entrepreneur who grasped the pulse of the times and struggled forward without pause.

One ton of steel created only 200,000 won in added value; one ton of automobiles produced 5 million won. A ton of computers created 300 million won, but a ton of semiconductors generated 1.3 billion won.

Lee decided to enter semiconductors because he recognized that the twenty-first century would be the age of high technology.

Resource-poor Korea could survive only by exporting high-value-added products, which required extensive development and investment.

Lee therefore challenged the magic stone with the greatest added value—the semiconductor. This, too, was part of his Tokyo Initiative.

On March 15 that year, Lee published an article in the *JoongAng Ilbo* declaring Samsung's new direction.

“With prolonged economic stagnation around the world and strengthening trade protectionism, the era in which national strength could be increased through mass exports is nearing its end. Under these conditions, Samsung, as an enterprise in a resource-poor country, must develop high-technology, high-value-added products... The semiconductor industry not only has vast prospects but can influence other industries; it is a high-technology, high-value-added field.”

After the article appeared, American memory-semiconductor companies reacted coolly. Intel called Lee's plan “a flight of fancy.”

Yet semiconductors could be seen as an industry only those with such flights of fancy could enter, for without an aggressive approach, even entry into the market was impossible.

The initial investment required 5 billion dollars, while one memory-semiconductor production line cost one trillion won. These were astronomical sums, beyond anyone who was not prepared to entertain the impossible.

Despite the immense investment, semiconductor generations changed extremely rapidly. Falling one step behind made a product worthless.

Semiconductors required vision ten years ahead.

Some therefore said semiconductors were not an industry but a gamble.

But those who developed ahead of others and entered the market at the right time could earn enormous profits.

Samsung faced a fork in the road: undertake a vast challenge or maintain the status quo.

Lee's decision-making process lasted a full decade.

In early December 1974, Lee's third son, Lee Kun-hee, then a director of the *JoongAng Ilbo* and Tongyang Broadcasting, expressed his desire to enter the semiconductor industry.

Chairman Lee did not decide on the spot. Extremely cautious, he first gathered all available information and materials, compared and analyzed them, listened to others' market assessments, and reflected.

The person who helped bring him to a decision was Dr. Inaba of Japan, an economist who had worked on Prime Minister Shigeru Yoshida's economic-reconstruction plan. In spring 1980, Inaba visited Lee in Tokyo and advised him to enter semiconductors.

After the 1973 oil crisis, Japan was shifting its pillar industries from steel, synthetic fibers, shipbuilding, and cement toward semiconductors, computers, new materials, and optical fibers—in other words, toward lighter, advanced technologies. Exports and foreign-exchange earnings rose sharply as a result.

Over the next three years, Lee met countless experts in the United States and Japan and read hundreds of books on semiconductors.

His final conclusion was that the United States led in technological development while Japan possessed the greatest production scale. America developed the technology, but Japan earned the money.

Lee regarded Japan's mass-production system as highly advantageous. If Japan could do it, Korea certainly could. Korean manual skill was in no way inferior.

Once he decided, Lee acted immediately. The decision had taken a long time, but action afterward was swift and forceful. Every task proceeded at full speed.

Samsung recruited numerous experts from the United States, forming what became known as the Samsung–Stanford School. Drs. Chin Dae-je, Kwon Oh-hyun, Hwang Chang-gyu, and others joined Samsung. To keep Chin from returning to Korea—he is now a senior managing director at Samsung Electronics[^FB-F036]—IBM offered him a blank check on which he could write his own annual salary. Chin told an IBM manager, “I am returning to Korea to overtake Japan.” His decision to join Samsung sprang entirely from patriotism.

Samsung chose 64K DRAM as its first semiconductor-development target. At the time, it was produced only in the United States, not even in Japan.

Samsung first formed technical-assistance relationships with an American semiconductor venture and Japan's Sharp Corporation.

Semiconductors demanded extreme precision and cleanliness, so the factory site had to meet those conditions.

Five locations became candidates. Giheung was ultimately chosen for its abundant groundwater, convenient power supply, and readily obtainable land. Construction continued day and night, sometimes until two or three in the morning even at fifteen degrees below zero Celsius.

The project employed 361 bulldozers, 126 excavators, 674 dump trucks, and a cumulative workforce of 260,000. Holidays, New Year's Day, and the Lunar New Year made no difference; everyone did everything possible to accelerate progress.

Meanwhile, technicians received guidance from Sharp and worked day and night to develop the 64K DRAM.

When Development Director Kim Yoon-woo and his team finished studying semiconductor technology at Sharp in Japan, they telephoned Chairman Lee and prepared to return the next day on a Korean Air flight.

Lee shouted into the telephone,

“Have you all gone mad? Returning on the same plane? Every one of you is to come back separately.”

He had reason to shout. If everyone who had labored to master the technology in Japan took one aircraft and an accident occurred, semiconductor development could come to nothing.

Normally quiet and reserved, Lee devoted enormous effort to developing semiconductor technology.

Traveling on separate aircraft became a Samsung tradition. Even today, when dozens of Samsung managers or executives go abroad to study or conduct research, they use two planes so that any danger is divided.

Lee visited the factory frequently to encourage the technicians. If this battle failed, only half of Samsung's industrial base would remain.

When news spread that Samsung was entering semiconductors, Japanese businesspeople mocked it: “At Korea's level, producing one before 1986 would be good enough... It is a moth flying into a flame.”

Developing 64K DRAM normally required passing through the 4K, 16K, and 32K stages, a process said to take twenty years. But they were mistaken.

Samsung needed neither twenty years nor until the predicted 1986. It developed 64K DRAM in December 1983, that same year, before the factory was even complete.

“Samsung Semiconductor & Telecommunications has developed 64K DRAM, completing the entire production, testing, and inspection process in only six months. Korea's semiconductor technology once lagged more than ten years behind the United States and Japan. We have now reduced the gap to four years.”

Samsung's statement of December 1, 1983, inspired deep emotion.

Since Chairman Lee's sleepless night and his telephone call to Chairman Hong Jin-ki, only

ten months had passed.

The United States and Japan were astonished. Samsung's response was calm.

“We have only built a bridge.”

Twelve days after successful development, on December 12, 1983, Samsung began exporting 64K DRAM. The Giheung plant was completed.

Twelve years later, Samsung, Hyundai, and Anam together held 40 percent of the world semiconductor market, making Korea the world's largest producer of DRAM.

Samsung's technological level continued to rise. In 1992, it became the first company in the world to develop 64M DRAM, and in 2001 it was again first with 4G DRAM, making it a global technological leader.

For the past two years, Samsung earned annual net profits of 9 trillion won. Thanks to Lee's painstaking dedication, Korea's export industries have multiplied and the country holds an unassailable position in semiconductors.

In spring 1983, Chung Ju-yung stood at the entrance to Anam Semiconductor's plant in Yeoju.

He had recently read Lee's declaration in the *JoongAng Ilbo* that Samsung would enter semiconductors, and it had strongly stimulated him.

Chung had taken a strong interest in semiconductors years earlier, saying in 1979 that he wanted to enter the field. In 1981, Hyundai Group's General Planning Office created a new-business team and began drafting a semiconductor plan. In early 1982, Chung personally visited Silicon Valley, meeting many people and touring many factories. While there, he learned that his eldest son, Chung Mong-pil, then president of Incheon Iron and Steel, had died in a traffic accident. Chung himself was an eldest son and had placed great expectations on Mong-pil.

Chung Mong-pil and his driver were both killed in the accident on his morning commute. The news left Chung overcome with grief.

He telephoned instructions that his son's funeral be kept simple and that the driver's arrangements be handled properly. He canceled a golf engagement for the next day and continued working in the United States. This showed how seriously he regarded semiconductors and how firm his

resolve had become.

Because semiconductors required such enormous scale, an investor needed unwavering determination. No one matched Chung's relentless drive, yet he, too, thought long and hard before making an extremely cautious decision.

He visited Anam's plant to make that final decision—to see with his own eyes whether semiconductors could take root in Korea and had a promising future. Anam Semiconductor was the first Korean enterprise to introduce semiconductor technology.

In response to his questions, Anam officials repeatedly said only that the business was “exhausting, painful, and difficult.”

It is said that before entering semiconductors, Chung also asked Lee about the field. Lee reportedly advised him, “Money alone is not enough. It requires extensive research,” attempting to dissuade him. Chung muttered, “Then I will invest my own money,” demonstrating his determination.

After inspecting Anam, Chung told his subordinates, “Double the planned scale.”

The experts all said it was difficult and urged him to stop. He moved in the opposite direction and ordered the project doubled, revealing his characteristic temperament.

He would not abandon an undertaking because others said it could not be done. The more they said no, the more strongly his desire for a challenge was aroused.

As he emerged from Anam's main gate, he announced Hyundai's large-scale entry into semiconductors. It might have appeared impulsive, but it was not: he ordered his subordinates to prepare a meticulous proposal.

Hyundai then built an electronics plant in Icheon, investing more than five hundred employees and 10 billion won. The talent was elite: researchers recruited from AT&T Bell Laboratories in the United States, including Oh Kye-hwan, Kim Se-jeong, Hwang In-seok, Park Heung-seop, and Yoo Hee-joon. They worked day and night developing memory semiconductors.

Chung attached great importance to the work. He often visited Icheon and treated the five hundred employees to food and drink to raise morale.

One evening, he drank freely with the researchers and employees over dinner. They consumed so much that Icheon ran out of alcohol, and Chung ordered more brought from Seoul. No quantity of liquor was too much in pursuit of the goal.

At one word from “King Chairman,”[^FB-F037] trucks began carrying alcohol from Seoul to Icheon. Even Hyundai's drinking displayed its bold spirit.

When Hyundai entered semiconductors, Japanese companies said resentfully, “What was bound to come has finally arrived.” Some predicted that Chung's style could never succeed in electronics. Yet Hyundai did succeed, dispelling concerns at home and abroad.

Sixteen years later, in 1999, Hyundai Electronics—now Hynix Semiconductor—had twenty-one thousand employees and total assets of 2 trillion won, making it a world-class semiconductor company. It rose to first place in DRAM; Hyundai produced 19 percent of the world market, ranking second in share and first in production capacity.[^TN-CH18-002]

Lee and Chung entered semiconductors at almost the same time. On one occasion, Hyundai faced a decision over whether to expand a production line, which required an astronomical investment.

An employee brought documents dozens of pages long to Honorary Chairman Chung's office. Their contents were extremely complex.

Chung glanced at them distractedly and asked, “What is Samsung doing?” “Samsung has decided to expand its line.” “Then that settles it. Samsung must have conducted a very thorough investigation before deciding. We will expand too. Make the arrangements tomorrow.”

The review was that simple. Expansion began on Chung's order—a swift decision requiring no further consideration. He was the sort of person who, seeing a 10-percent possibility, would act and create the remaining 90 percent himself.

Another example was the acquisition of Maxtor, one of the world's five largest hard-drive manufacturers.

Samsung had originally intended to acquire Maxtor and spent two years repeatedly investigating and evaluating the deal. Hyundai Electronics ultimately bought it instead. After learning of Samsung's interest, Hyundai contacted Maxtor directly and completed the acquisition in only two months.

Hyundai's reasoning was simple: if Samsung was willing to spend two years examining an acquisition, did that not already prove the company's enormous value? There was no need to hesitate.

Samsung and Hyundai differed sharply in this respect. If necessary, Hyundai acquired first and dealt with the details later; Samsung investigated a company's finances, technology, prospects, and every other factor before buying.

Because of Lee Byung-chull's and Chung Ju-yung's decisions, Korea produced 28.5 billion dollars' worth of semiconductors in 2000 and exported 26 billion dollars' worth. The industry accounted for 15.1 percent of Korea's total exports and earned net profits of 5.91 billion dollars, making it the country's greatest export contributor.

Lee, who gathered broad information and analyzed it precisely before deciding, and Chung, who charged forward, made Korea the world's third-largest semiconductor producer and largest DRAM producer eighteen years later.

Both men entered the global semiconductor competition. Because of their judgment, Samsung Electronics and Hynix Semiconductor, formerly Hyundai Electronics, now rank first and second in the world by production scale.

Korean semiconductor technology has now reached the level of producing 4G chips: within a thickness one eight-hundredth that of a human hair, they can store the contents of a stack of two-hundred-character manuscript pages as tall as a four-hundred-story building.

“Bad news?” Chung was startled.

“What? Kim Yoon-kyu?”

“Bring Kim Yoon-kyu and every injured Hyundai employee back to Seoul immediately for treatment at Seoul Central Hospital.”

Chung knew the crash site and nearby hospitals must be chaotic and probably lacked even enough blood for transfusions. He therefore issued the order.

“I thought you had jumped out yourself when it happened. You have always been bold,” Chung joked.

“Nothing was wrong with me, and lying in the hospital was driving me mad. I still had the mission of meeting Libya's electricity minister, but hospital rules prohibited patients from leaving. I am going to Japan tomorrow. Come with me.”

Kim traveled to Japan with Chung. After completing their work, they recuperated at a hot spring and traveled together, drinking along the way.

On the flight home, Chung said, “Yoon-kyu, I was terribly worried when the accident happened. Now I am relieved. This trip has shown me that nothing is wrong with you. How could you fall from an airplane without suffering a scratch? When I visited you in the hospital and saw you spring up to bow, I thought it might be a temporary recovery after a massive shock. As he spoke, Chung clasped Kim's hand with relief.

Lee Byung-chull, too, once felt deeply moved by how well he had taught his employees.

After the April 19 military coup,[^TN-CH19-001] Korea entered the period of the Second Republic. The government decided to investigate those involved in the March 15 election fraud and illicit profiteering, and Samsung came under scrutiny. Investigators questioned Samsung section chiefs, managing directors, senior managing directors, and others one by one about tax evasion. Lee himself then underwent interrogation for the first time in his life.

“If Samsung has a principal culprit responsible for tax evasion, who is it?” the prosecutor asked.

“That is obvious. I am Samsung's chief executive, so naturally I am the principal culprit.”

The prosecutor was speechless. At most companies, executives and owners blamed one another during investigations. Samsung was entirely different; he had never expected Lee to name himself.

“I have investigated several companies, but Samsung is the only one where no one shifted responsibility and everyone acknowledged being a principal actor. You have taught your people remarkably well.”

The prosecutor's words greatly pleased and encouraged Lee. He replied, “Who created circumstances in which evading taxes became unavoidable? But I swear to heaven that we did not evade taxes with a guilty conscience; we knew it was illegal. Yet I believe an entrepreneur's greatest duty is to take responsibility for the enterprise and expand reproduction. If poor management causes a company to lose money and collapse, the harm to society is no less than tax evasion. A failing company inflicts losses on banks and many others and throws employees out of work. That is an even less forgivable error: a waste of resources and a dereliction of entrepreneurial duty.”

He then gave several reasons why tax evasion had been unavoidable. Corporate tax was 35 percent, business income tax 15 percent, and product tax—for synthetic fibers, for example—30 percent. With business tax, surtaxes, and other levies added, even paying all revenue in taxes would not have been enough. Such, he said, was the distorted high-tax system of the time. The prosecutor again had no answer.

Chung Ju-yung consistently placed great trust in his subordinates. Lee Byung-chull, who upheld a talent-first philosophy, dispensed rewards and punishments clearly.

Chung learned management through broad human contact. “Wide-ranging relationships help me retain my humor, avoid being blinded by prejudice, face life with passion, and deepen mutual understanding. Through exchange with others, a person becomes receptive to good advice. I learned many things through these interactions, and they became a driving force in managing my enterprises. A company is an organization created for people. It requires an unselfish heart that understands principle, and an untiring mind devoted to learning. Only with these can an enterprise develop...”

Through extensive social contact, Chung sharpened his eye for people and his ability to use talent. He often visited construction sites personally to teach subordinates.

He once told them, “It is my conviction that anyone trained by me and taught at a construction site can do well anywhere, at any task.”

In management he also stressed: “When you receive instructions from a superior or leave a meeting, you usually cannot complete the assignment alone. Accurate communication and cooperation are then essential. An enterprise operates smoothly only when its upper and lower levels are well coordinated. If orders from above cannot be conveyed accurately, the company will develop arteriosclerosis.”

Another distinctive quality of Chung's was his ability to follow the *Analects* and “not be ashamed to ask those below him.” He did not care whether the other person was younger or held a lower social position; he freely asked about anything he did not know.

He therefore always emphasized that even a superior must humbly ask and learn from subordinates whenever something was unclear or unknown.

Chung's management method was plain and direct. Lee's was more systematic.

“Putting talent first and people at the center has long been my creed and philosophy at Samsung. Entrepreneurs must spare no effort in developing people. When an entrepreneur's desire and passion for doing so take root and sprout in employees' hearts, the enterprise gains unlimited room for growth.”

In Lee's view, an entrepreneur unable to cultivate talent had failed. Part of an entrepreneur's responsibility was to develop outstanding people for the enterprise, society, and nation; failure to do so was almost a crime. Developing talent did not simply mean gathering capable people. It also meant fostering their unity and providing entrepreneurial leadership and personal magnetism.

“If a nation's development depends on outstanding statesmen, an enterprise's development rests on outstanding entrepreneurs. Samsung grew entirely because it hired many excellent people. Samsung was Korea's first private enterprise to use open recruitment, and it increased its contribution to society through personnel management. Without the united effort and inner sense of mission of every employee, corporate development is impossible.”

Lee continually emphasized that developing people was a hundred-year undertaking. He therefore believed employees must receive the greatest possible security and welfare.

An Eastern maxim says, “It takes ten years to grow a tree, but a hundred years to cultivate a person.” This, too, shows that an enterprise's success or failure lies with people and that outstanding talent determines its future.

To encourage employees to remain with the company for the long term, Lee provided fair opportunities for advancement and equitable compensation from the moment they joined.

Whether company president or ordinary employee, anyone with leadership ability received many assignments so that their capabilities could be fully exercised.

# Outstanding Entrepreneurs Have an Eye for Talent

Putting talent first and people at the center has been my creed for many years, as well as Samsung's management philosophy and supporting pillar.

An entrepreneur should spare no effort in developing people. If the entrepreneur's expectations and desire for talent reach every employee, the enterprise will enter a path of limitless prosperity.

—Lee Byung-chull

Entrepreneurs skilled at employing people possess a distinctive sense for judging them. Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung differed in how they did so.

Lee emphasized clear rewards and punishments. “What is a manager? Someone who conducts an enterprise. Some confuse a manager with a gentleman. A manager must possess ability, moral standing, and authority, while a gentleman possesses virtue. The difference appears in the clear administration of rewards and punishments. When an accident occurs, for example, was it caused by good intentions, by incompetence, or by chance? If someone investigated carefully and tried to do the job well but failed, that person should still be commended. An accident caused by negligence must be punished.”

Because Lee followed this principle, Samsung became a gathering place for talent. Discussing evaluation, he said, “People change as their abilities change, and abilities can improve. Education can raise ability, but not everyone can do so. The capacity for effort is an innate quality, and a person's development depends on possessing it. Evaluating the person's own qualities is therefore more important than education itself.”

When Lee recognized someone's ability, he assigned that person many tasks, providing ample opportunity to display it.

During development of a central processing unit, the managing director responsible for the project explained its importance to Lee. Development would require an enormous investment of 10 million dollars, but success could not be guaranteed.

“Stop. You are a managing director! Even if the entire ten million is lost, as a pillar of the company you should be able to proceed boldly. I do not recall ever appointing someone without confidence as a managing director.”

Another incident occurred at Cheil Sugar. A sharp increase in taxes on sugar-refining materials cost the company hundreds of millions of won. No individual was at fault for the tax increase, but Lee regarded the failure to anticipate it as a management error. Even so, he held no one responsible.

The employee in charge nevertheless accepted responsibility and submitted his resignation. Lee summoned and comforted him: “The loss of hundreds of millions saddens me, but it is nothing compared with the pleasure I feel at seeing you take responsibility so courageously.”

Lee not only returned the resignation but promoted the employee.

As noted earlier, Chung recognized Lee Myung-bak after Lee risked his life protecting a safe during construction of Thailand's Narathiwat Highway.

From then on, Lee earned Chung's appreciation and began a brilliant rise. He became Hyundai Construction's managing director for administration in 1972, senior managing director in 1974, and president in 1975 at the age of thirty-five. In only ten years after joining the company in 1965, he rose from an ordinary employee to president.

Whenever the company recruited new employees, Lee Byung-chull paid exceptional attention for fear of overlooking talent.

His selection standard assigned fifty points to education and fifty to personal character. Character included a respectable appearance, physical health, and creativity. This differed entirely from companies focused on written-examination scores.

“No matter how intelligent a person is, poor character makes him unsuitable. I also believe good character is reflected in a proper appearance. A person needs initiative and a cheerful, active disposition, as well as a healthy body that is never satisfied with the status quo and can continue advancing and striving.”

Using these standards, Lee selected outstanding people in most cases.

Chung likewise valued character more than experience or education. “Accomplishing something depends not on experience or schooling but on a person's character and attitude. When difficulties arise, resolving them requires total effort. There is no valid reason why a problem cannot be solved. For diligent people, such reasons are no reasons at all.”

Chung therefore valued the capacity for effort when judging and selecting people. Effort appeared chiefly as passion for the task. People with passion continued striving until they achieved their goals.

“A person's honesty in a small matter gives us reason to believe he will be equally honest in a large one. We can trust that someone who keeps a minor promise will also keep a major promise. Likewise, a person who gives his utmost to a small task will do the same for a great undertaking. That is what credibility means.”

Chung placed credibility above all else. He chose only those who were honest, trustworthy, and gave their utmost to every task.

# Trust and Scrutiny

“Whatever it is, if you think it is difficult, it will become infinitely difficult. If you think it is simple, it truly will become simple.”

Chung Ju-yung and Lee Byung-chull approached the assignment of work to their subordinates differently. Chung trusted his subordinates and entrusted tasks to them, whereas Lee trusted them while continuing to scrutinize their performance.

One incident occurred when Hyundai Engineering & Construction was selecting a site for a shipyard in Ulsan. Chung first bought land at Yeompo-ri in Ulsan's inner harbor and designated it for the shipyard, then sent a team to assess the soil.

The Hyundai Engineering & Construction executive in charge was Jeon Gap-won. Tests concluded that the soil there was unsuitable for a shipyard. When Jeon returned to Seoul, Chung gave him a severe scolding.

Jeon returned to Ulsan to withdraw the British technical team responsible for the soil tests. Physically and mentally exhausted, he ordered Bangeojin's[^FB-F038] famous whale meat with soju. After becoming slightly drunk, he walked to Ulsan's outer harbor.

Ulsan's three outer harbors—Jeonha Bay, Mipo Bay, and Ilsan Bay—lay side by side, amid picturesque beaches and fishing villages. As Jeon took in the scene, a thought suddenly came to him: this area was better suited to a shipyard than the inner harbor.

Jeon took the British technical team, which he had originally intended to send home, to Bangeojin. Beginning the next day, they assessed the soil and geological strata around Mipo Bay. He had not reported this to the chairman and was acting on his own initiative.

The investigation showed that the underlying strata were extremely solid. Geographically, the harbor was surrounded by mountains, so winds and waves were relatively mild. As an outer harbor, it also offered far better conditions for a shipyard than the inner harbor. It was closer than Yeompo-ri to Pohang Iron and Steel, then under construction, and its low shoreline gave it virtually limitless development potential.

After four days of surveying, the results showed that the strata were firm and the soil excellent. But Jeon began to worry. He had ordered the survey without seeking prior approval, and he had no idea how furious the chairman would be.

Back in Seoul, Jeon went to the chairman's office and reported what had happened. Chung flew into a rage when he heard the report.

“Who told you to come back like this?”

“What?”

Jeon did not understand what the chairman meant and stood there dumbfounded.

“Once you had the survey results, you should have stayed in Ulsan, negotiated the price, and bought the land. Why did you come all the way back to Seoul?”

Chung smiled as he finished speaking. Only then did Jeon realize that the chairman was praising rather than criticizing him, and a smile spread across his own face.

When Samsung built a paper mill in Jeonju, Lee Byung-chull considered the site's geographical conditions, but gave even greater priority to benefiting the local community.

When word spread that Samsung was going to build a factory in Jeonju, local residents competed to sell their land. Lee purchased 67,000 *pyeong*. A survey, however, found that the ground was clay and unsuitable for construction. A paper mill was a heavy-industrial facility and could not be built on clay, yet bringing in soil from elsewhere would entail enormous construction costs.

Jeong Su-chang, who was responsible for the project, reported to Lee: “Chairman, perhaps this is for the best. Why not take this opportunity to move the site to Yeri in Gunsan,[^FB-F039] where access to sea and rail transport and to water is much more convenient?”

Lee replied, “We knew from the beginning that Jeonju was at a disadvantage compared with other places. As I often say, a business must not pursue immediate profits. When running a business, the first consideration should be contributing to the nation and society. In particular, wasn't the main purpose of building this paper mill to fulfill the people of Jeonju's desire for economic development?”

“Yes, yes, it was.” Only then did Jeong understand Lee's thoughtful intentions, and he nodded repeatedly.

“To disregard the enthusiasm of the people of Jeonju and build the factory somewhere else would betray those who sold us their land. So put that idea completely out of your mind. Think instead about how to build the factory at the foot of Jeonju's renowned Hwangbang

Mountain.”

In the end, Lee's proposal was followed, and the paper mill was built on more than 100,000 *pyeong* of land in Palbok-dong at the foot of Hwangbang Mountain.

Chung Ju-yung often assigned work to his subordinates on the basis of seemingly sudden judgments. Yet those sudden judgments contained both singular insight and precise calculation. From the moment an employee handed him a report, his mind would race, producing what appeared to be an impromptu decision. Rather than agonizing over a problem, he treated it simply and then set about accomplishing the task.

“Whatever it is, if you think it is difficult, it will become infinitely difficult. If you think it is simple, it truly will become simple.”

Just as Chung himself said, whenever he began something, he simplified the problem and then worked to carry it out. Precise calculations naturally lay behind this approach, but there was another reason: he knew that simplifying a matter before tackling it made the goal easier to achieve.

“The success or failure of everything depends on the mindset and attitude of the person doing it. Taking on something new certainly involves great risk. Without risk, one can only stand still, and then retreat, until finally giving up.” This was Chung's view of the spirit of risk-taking.

Lee Byung-chull believed that large enterprises must sometimes rise above profit. He once put it this way:

“Every enterprise has social responsibilities of its own, and those responsibilities differ according to the size of the enterprise. Compared with large enterprises, small businesses operate on a smaller scale and with a narrower field of vision, so their first priority is to manage their own affairs well. That is not enough for a large enterprise. Especially given its close ties with numerous related businesses, a large enterprise cannot concern itself solely with its own development. It must broaden its perspective; in many cases, business activity must transcend the temptation of profit.”

Lee's insistence on the Jeonju site for the paper mill arose from his belief that the development of the regional economy was essential, even if the company had to incur some losses.

# Making Something of Play

Chung answered with the example of climbing a mountain: if you stare at the summit as you climb, reaching it will certainly seem extremely difficult. But if you follow the footprints of those who came before you and climb one step at a time, you can make it to the top.

In the summer of 1991, Chairman Chung Ju-yung attended the Summer Seaside School for Poets organized by the poetry magazine *Simsang*. The seaside school brought writers and readers together in summer to enjoy the beach during the day and hold poetry readings and discussions in the evening. Chung always took an active part in events of this kind, as well as in various Children's Day[^FB-F040] activities.

Once, when Chung attended a Children's Day event, a child asked him, “Grandpa, how did you make so much money?”

Although Chung may have seemed to us like a man of physical action, he was in fact a meticulous thinker with the romantic temperament of an artist. One important trigger for his departure from home as a boy was *The Dong-A Ilbo*, which he delivered every day to the district head's house. The newspaper brought him information about the outside world, and it was also serializing Yi Kwang-su's novel *The Heartless*, which Chung loved.

As a passionate teenager, Chung yearned to enter the wider world and pursue his dreams. For this reason, he ran away from home four times.

In his youth, Chung read many biographies of great figures, including a life of Napoleon, and drew considerable inspiration from them. In his later years, he interacted extensively with Korean artists.

He met television writer Kim Soo-hyun, novelists Park Kyongni and Lee Mun-yol, comedian Lee Ju-il, and others to discuss literature and art. His reasoning was that “anyone recognized by the public in a particular field must have something distinctive.” He associated with these prominent people in order to learn from them.

In 1993, Chung went to visit the novelist Park Kyongni at her home. That day, Park was holding a commemorative event to celebrate the completion of her epic novel *Land*. Chung had been traveling abroad in connection with the World Cup, and as soon as he returned to Korea, he went straight from the airport

to Park's home in Wonju.[^FB-F041]

It was Park and Chung's second meeting. At their first, Park had been struck by how Chung's face seemed at once childlike and elderly. At their second meeting, he was extremely humble and sensitive, yet also gave an impression of keen intelligence.

Why would Chung, despite being so busy, make time to meet a novelist who appeared unrelated to his business and attend an event celebrating the completion of her book? The explanation is that he went there to acquire knowledge and improve himself.

When Daewoo Group's Kim Woo-choong traveled abroad on business, he too often invited Korean intellectuals and men of great learning to accompany him, engaging them in marathon conversations on the plane. A representative example was his travel with the philosopher Kim Yong-ok[^FB-F042] and the novelist Choi In-hun. Kim spoke with them at length throughout flights lasting more than ten hours. An in-depth conversation with an expert in a particular field could provide not only knowledge but also insight into changes and trends in the world.

Chung shared this view. No one pursued learning more actively than he did. On one occasion, he and Kwon Ki-tae, a vice president of Hyundai Engineering & Construction, visited a Norwegian port city on a construction matter. When they went to a bar for a drink that evening, Norwegians were dancing the waltz. Chung did not know how to dance it, so he watched from the side all evening.

Not long afterward, Chung and Kwon traveled to Guam on business. When Kwon awoke in the morning and drew back the curtains, he saw someone dancing on the beach outside. It was Chung, practicing alone the waltz steps he had learned in Norway. At that moment, he looked like a thirteen-year-old boy. The elderly chairman, as curious as a child, practiced his dance steps on the sand. This was another side of Chung Ju-yung.

Why did Chung associate so closely with writers and artists? Through his contact with poets, novelists, and other literary figures and artists, he sought to expand the horizons of his imagination. Poets and artists possess freer imaginations than anyone else. Chung hoped to learn that imaginative power from them and put it into practice. The extraordinary act of driving five hundred cattle on a visit

to North Korea revealed the richness of his sensitivity and imagination. A French cultural critic praised the event extravagantly, calling it the greatest feat of the twenty-first century.

During the Seosan seawall project, Chung adopted an extraordinary method that no one else could have imagined. Because the tide was so powerful, it swept away every foundation stone. He therefore sank a disused oil tanker weighing more than 200,000 tons on the seabed to block the waves, filled in above it, and completed the seawall. His imagination had been put to work.

By contrast, rather than seeking the company of artists, Lee Byung-chull immersed himself in his own artistic interests. Two or three times a week, for example, he went with close friends to listen to traditional Korean music.

According to the recollections of Kyobo[^FB-F043] founder Chairman Shin Yong-ho, Lee once even organized a traditional Korean music concert simply so that he could listen to the music.

Because Shin did not understand music theory, he would sometimes grow bored and want to leave. Lee would gently persuade him to stay: “Oh, listen a little longer.”

Lee particularly loved performances of the *gayageum*.[^FB-F044] He would close his eyes softly and immerse himself in the music. After a while, tapping his knee lightly, he would exclaim, “Ah, how exquisite,” and savor the joy the music brought him. During sorrowful passages, his eyes often filled with tears.

Lee's artistic interests did not end there. The Painting of the Tathagata in his collection was another example. In 1979, Chairman Lee acquired this Buddhist painting from the Goryeo period from a cultural institution in Japan.

It was a cultural artifact that had found its way to Japan during the period of Japanese rule over Korea. Large numbers of Goryeo Buddhist paintings left the country during that period, leaving only a little more than one hundred in Korea. Even today, it is extremely difficult to view a fine Buddhist painting in Korea.

“The triangular composition formed by Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva, Avalokitesvara Bodhisattva, and the Tathagata both relieves tension in the viewer's mind and gives the whole work a harmonious and tranquil quality.”

This was the assessment made by Cho Yu-jeon, director of the National Research Institute of Cultural Heritage, when he saw the painting.

After the painting returned to Korea, it caused a tremendous sensation. Regarded as the most valuable Buddhist painting in the country, it was designated National Treasure No. 218.

Lee's aesthetic interests continued to develop. In April 1982, he used the artworks he had collected over many years to establish the Ho-Am[^FB-F045] Art Museum. Today, the museum in Yongin displays 1,336 works of art. Its collection of historic works includes twelve National Treasures and eight designated Treasures.

The National Treasures include a celadon gourd-shaped ewer with a lotus design in underglaze copper (National Treasure No. 133); a gilt-bronze triad of bodhisattvas (No. 134); a dragon-headed banner finial (No. 136); bronze artifacts excavated from Bisan-dong, Daegu (No. 137); a gold crown and its ornaments (No. 138); a screen depicting a gathering of immortals (No. 139); a mirror inlaid with mother-of-pearl designs of flowers, birds, and animals (No. 140); a celadon vase with an incised bamboo-joint design (No. 169); a bronze box with silver-inlaid designs of floral scrolls and phoenixes (No. 171); artifacts excavated from the tomb of Lady Jeong in Jinyang County (No. 172); and an ink-on-white-paper manuscript of the *Avatamsaka Sutra* from Silla (No. 196).

Thanks to the efforts of Samsung Group, the British Museum now has one. This deserves considerable praise.

From a national perspective, the quality of the artifacts in the Korean Gallery also needs to be raised. The discussion has strayed somewhat from the subject, but in any event, Lee Byung-chull was a farsighted connoisseur of cultural artifacts.

# By-the-Book Golf and Red Golf

Even when making a golf club, one must pursue perfection; only with that spirit can one produce something of the finest quality. That spirit

applies not only to golf clubs, but to one's entire life, including one's business.

—Lee Byung-chull

Both Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung were extremely fond of golf. Lee was more devoted to the game than Chung and played at a level comparable to that of a professional. He went golfing two or three times a week. Lee recorded three holes-in-one during his lifetime, an achievement that even a man as disinclined to boast as he was took pride in.

Lee first encountered golf when he was in his forties and traveling frequently between Korea and Japan. From his first exposure to the sport, he was captivated by its tremendous appeal.

“To understand golf is to understand the principles of the world,” Lee concluded.

He meant that when a powerful stroke was required, strength alone was not enough. One also had to calculate the angle, distance, wind direction, and other factors precisely, and to strike carefully rather than rashly. Yet at times one could not be excessively bold either.

Meticulous in everything he did, Lee began learning golf from a solid foundation. His instructor, Haruyoshi Kobari, was a pioneer of professional golf in Japan.

Because he had built such a firm foundation at the outset, Lee's swing was nearly flawless. His shots were powerful and accurate, and he was particularly skilled at reading the line between the ball and the hole on the green.

He generally averaged between 82 and 84 strokes. When someone told him that there was a problem with the rotation in his swing, he deliberately sought instruction from Yon Tok-chun and Han Jang-sang, two leading figures in Korean professional golf.

Lee's regular golfing partners included Chairman Koo In-hwoi of Goldstar Group, the predecessor of LG Group; National Assembly Speaker Lee Jae-hyung; and Shin Yong-nam, an adviser to the Korea Golf Association. Speaker Lee and Chairman Koo were brothers-in-law through marriage, and their meetings on the golf course deepened the affection between them.

Lee absolutely refused to play with anyone who ignored golf etiquette. A person who cheated on the score, lied, was late, made a loud commotion, or fell asleep snoring on the bathhouse floor after a round would never be invited to play with him again. Lee himself observed golf etiquette rigorously. When he arranged to play with others, he arrived before everyone else and waited for them; once on the course, he began without taking practice shots. He believed that making others wait imposed on them. His attitude was that “golf begins with etiquette and ends with etiquette.” Members of the Japanese business community greatly admired his conduct on the course.

When Lee played golf, he remembered only two things: his best shot and his worst shot of the day. Why had the good shot gone well? Why had the bad one turned out as it did? He remembered them clearly because he studied the reasons behind each.

Influenced by his father, Chairman Lee Kun-hee was also highly praised for his golf etiquette. He firmly believed that people without refinement or manners could not be trusted in life or in business. His mastery of golf theory left experts in admiration.

Lee Byung-chull studied theoretical books purchased in Japan and lectured other corporate chairmen on what he learned. He often said that golf was a better prescription for health than nutritional supplements. When business leaders used lack of time as an excuse not to play, he urged them to join him for the sake of both their health and their business.

Lee cared intensely about winning or losing at golf. A golf fanatic who detested coming second, he hated defeat as much in competition as he did in business—even in a friendly match among business colleagues. He especially hated losing to his business rival, Chairman Chung Ju-yung. Whenever Lee lost a round, he analyzed the reasons thoroughly before returning to the course, determined to win the next match. When evenly matched players compete, either may naturally win or lose, but Lee did not see it that way at all.

Why did Lee care so much about the result? Rather than valuing the outcome for its own sake, he could not tolerate failing to meet the competitive goal he had set for himself. He took the same approach when entering the semiconductor industry.

After losing a round, Lee repeatedly reflected on and studied his play. He corrected the problems before entering the next match. He took the same approach in business:

when a problem arose, he analyzed it thoroughly, identified its source, and sought to prevent it from recurring in the next undertaking.

Lee's golfing ability was unmatched in the Korean business world and had reached a remarkably high level. He generally shot between 82 and 84, driving the ball 220 to 240 yards—among the very highest standards for an amateur.

Lee did more than play golf well. He was also an expert on clubs, shoes, balls, courses, and other aspects of the game, and he always used the finest equipment. For wooden clubs, for example, he selected top-grade timber from the mountainous regions of northern Canada, specifically strips cut from the sun-facing side, said to yield the highest-quality wood. One of his favored club brands was Kennedy Smith. Each of its handmade clubs bore an engraved number; Lee used No. 57. At one point he owned more than five hundred clubs, all prestigious brands. He cared for these valuable clubs like works of art and polished them whenever he had time.

Lee could be said to possess an aristocratic temperament. Yet personal taste alone did not account for his purchase of so many prestigious clubs. Whenever he encountered a first-rate product, he analyzed why it had achieved that status, examining what had been made well and how the product had become first-rate. He collected not only golf clubs but also pipes. Once he had reached his conclusion, his attachment to the object diminished. If someone praised one of his clubs, Lee promptly gave it to that person as a gift. He did the same with other possessions. Chairman Lee Kun-hee later said that if his father had kept all the things he gave away, the collection would have been enough to open a museum. Lee Byung-chull merely smiled.

Lee Byung-chull's love of golf passed to Chairman Lee Kun-hee, who was also a golf fanatic. During practice, he could eat a sandwich while hitting more than 1,500 balls. Lee Kun-hee began learning the game because of his father.

In 1953, when Lee Kun-hee was in the fifth grade at Busan Normal Elementary School, he obeyed his father's instruction to “go to Japan,” moved there, and began learning golf. Lee Byung-chull purchased every item his son needed for the sport. As a result, Chairman Lee Kun-hee now averages a score of 70.

Lee Byung-chull and Lee Kun-hee later sponsored the female golfer Pak Se-ri, who went on to win major prizes repeatedly in international competition.

The father and son's love of golf went beyond an ordinary interest or hobby and became a tremendous source of publicity for Samsung Group.

Chung Ju-yung was rather different from Lee Byung-chull. Chung's extraordinarily rich imagination was widely known, and the “red golf ball” was one example.

On one occasion, Lotte Group Chairman Shin Kyuk-ho arranged to play golf with Chung. Beginning before dawn on the appointed day, heavy snow fell. Chairman Shin was deeply worried: how could anyone play with a white golf ball on a course blanketed in white snow? He telephoned Chung, who told him not to worry and to meet him at the course. Still full of misgivings, Shin arrived there.

As expected, the course was a vast expanse of white. Chung then produced a golf ball that had been painted red. Only at that moment did Shin understand Chung's ingenuity. A red ball would be perfectly visible against the snowy ground. Shin admired him inwardly and conceded his brilliance. That was Chung Ju-yung for you!

Whatever the circumstances, Chung always worked to turn them to his advantage. It looked simple, yet it was not something an ordinary person would think of.

Chairmen Shin and Chung happily spent the day playing with red golf balls on the snow-covered course.

On another occasion, Chung played golf with senior aides including Lee Myung-bak and Park Se-yong. As noted earlier, Lee was chairman of Hyundai Engineering & Construction, while Park was then president of the group. Chung and Park formed one side, with Lee on the other. They began the match with a wager of 10,000 won.

Chung's side lost the first round, so he too had to pay 10,000 won. Looking regretful, he handed over a 10,000-won note. Chung's side won the second round, which meant he could win the money back. Chairman Lee Myung-bak took a 10,000-won note from his wallet and handed it to Chung.

Chung studied the money for a long while and said, “Hey, the note I gave you just now was

new, but you've given me an old one. Give me back the one I gave you.”

Chairman Lee searched his wallet for a new note and gave it to Chung. Though it was a joke, the exchange epitomized Chung's lifelong thrift.

Ordinary people may imagine that anyone who earns a great deal of money must spend it lavishly, but the reality is otherwise: the more money people make, the more carefully they tend to spend it.

In his later years, when Chung's physical strength had declined, he would hit three balls from one position and choose the one with the best lie. This was a rule reserved exclusively for him.

If an opponent hit out of bounds or landed in a poor position, the shot counted; only Chung could choose the ball with the most favorable lie and play on. This naturally violated the rules of golf, but he stubbornly insisted on it. He did so, of course, only when playing with members of his family or his senior aides, who turned a blind eye and continued the game.

In this respect, Chung was adaptable and possessed a sense of humor. Because age and physical weakness limited the distance he could hit, he invented his own rules and enjoyed playing with his subordinates. This was one way in which he differed from Lee Byung-chull.

Until his death, Lee went to the golf course every week, playing by the rules and studying the game. Through that effort, he achieved three holes-in-one—a feat most people would struggle to accomplish even once in a lifetime.

In old age, however, Chung played by rules of his own making and could still sink nine shots each week. His golfing ability naturally became highly inconsistent after he turned eighty, but before then he had ranked among the finest golfers in the Korean business world. His sons Chung Mong-koo, Chung Mong-hun, and Chung Mong-yoon were all excellent golfers, and Chairman Chung Mong-hun was particularly renowned for hitting drives of three hundred yards.

At times, Chung's game even surpassed Lee's. Chung's best score was 81, while Lee generally shot between 82 and 84, making the two evenly matched. Lee, of course, had no desire whatsoever to lose to Chung. Yet judging by their actual wins and losses, both men were already advanced in age, and each had his strengths.

# The Thrift of Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung

When Chung Ju-yung went to meet National Defense Commission Chairman Kim Jong Il, he wore only a fedora, a worn jacket, and

a pair of leather shoes he had owned for more than ten years as he broke through the barrier between North and South. In his entire life, Chung never heard anyone praise his clothes. To Koreans, however, his figure stood taller than it would have in any expensive attire: he was a beloved entrepreneur with the presence of a father.

Wealthy people have all passed through periods of diligence and thrift. Contrary to rumor, Lee Byung-chull also had a frugal side.

Once on a golf course, Lee picked up a tee from the spot where the player ahead of him had just taken a shot. The caddie beside him saw that one side of the tee was broken and that it was very worn. Lee picked it up, flicked off the mud, wiped it clean, and carefully placed it in his trouser pocket, looking thoroughly satisfied. The caddie observed the entire scene. Lee gave a slightly embarrassed smile. “One must be economical in life,” he said, then walked toward the next position.

A new tee cost only one or two thousand won, yet finding such an old one brought a look of deep satisfaction to his face, and he put it in his pocket. The caddie was profoundly moved by what he had seen.

As Lee walked to the next point on the course, the caddie pulled the cart beside him. Whenever an incline made the cart difficult to move, Lee always helped pull it. This revealed something of his character.

Chung Ju-yung was also extremely sparing in daily life.

At the golf course, he would take out a tee so worn that the caddie could not imagine how long it had been used. Yet Chung still used it for his shot and never forgot to take it home afterward.

After Chung died, his home in Cheongun-dong was opened to the public, revealing white cloth spread across the rooms. He had often told employees that his home had never contained luxurious carpeting. After his death, people saw that it was exactly as he had said.

Instead of carpet, the floor was covered with a white cloth, washed perfectly clean and densely covered in patches. The television in his room was equally astonishing: an old, battered Goldstar set of indeterminate vintage, the kind that even an ordinary salaried household would no longer watch.

Another striking object in his room was a pair of leather shoes he had worn for twenty-two years. They were black shoes used for so long that their surfaces were covered in dents. Such was the life of Chung Ju-yung, the richest man in the Republic of Korea.

Anyone who hears the story of Chung's shoes can understand how frugally he lived even after becoming a *chaebol* leader.

Chung had large feet and could not wear ready-made shoes. They had to be custom-made, so he cared for them attentively and wore them until they fell apart.

One day, Adviser K of the Korean Olympic Committee attended an international conference with Chung and traveled on the same plane. By chance, they were seated next to each other.

Chung was often short of sleep, so he slept while riding in cars and airplanes. That day, too, he fell sound asleep as soon as he sat down.

Adviser K caught sight of Chung's shoes beneath the seat. “What? How could they be like that?”

Adviser K was so astonished that his mouth fell open. He rubbed his eyes and looked again. Each side of Chung's shoes had a hole where his big toe pressed against it. K could not bring himself to ask Chung directly, but he judged that the shoes had been worn for at least fifteen years.

Chung wore every pair of shoes for more than ten years.

His choice of cars offered another glimpse of his thrift. At all times, he preferred to ride in vehicles made by Hyundai.

On one occasion, Dr. K, honorary director of Hospital S, went to the nineteenth floor of the Publishing Center to attend a celebration for the publication of Dr. M's book. When the event ended and he entered the elevator, Chung walked in alone. Despite being chairman of a major corporate group, he had not brought a single attendant.

When they reached the ground-floor lobby, Dr. K asked Chung, “What is your license-plate number? I'll have someone bring your car around.”

Dr. K was calling for his own car and thought he would have Chung's brought around at the same time. But Chung firmly declined. “There's no need. I'll do it myself.”

About seven or eight minutes later, Dr. K's car arrived first. Thinking that he should not leave before Chairman Chung, he waited a little longer. But after a long while, Chung's car still had not appeared.

“What is your license-plate number? Let me have someone call again,” Dr. K asked once more.

“I'll do it myself.” Chung strode over to the building's security guard and asked him to call for the car over the public-address system.

After another three or four minutes, a car stopped in front of the lobby. To everyone's surprise, it was a

Stellar,[^FB-F046] and Chung immediately got inside.

At that moment, the security guard ran over and shouted, “Hey! Who told you that you could park here?”

The guard had not seen Chung get into the car. Fearing that the foreign cars belonging to other chairmen would soon arrive, he protested at the audacity of this Stellar in stopping directly in front of the lobby.

Realizing what had happened, Dr. K rushed over and shouted at the guard, “Hey, do you know who's in that car? That's Hyundai Group Chairman Chung Ju-yung. Look carefully at the guest before you speak. How can you shout merely on the basis of the car?”

Only then did the guard see Chung sitting inside, and he was terrified.

Though only a small episode from daily life, it showed how Chung, by riding in a car made by his own Hyundai Group, used his own face as a mobile billboard for the company.

Once, Chung went for a drink at a nightclub owned by comedian Lee Ju-il. Like anyone else, he ordered the minimum purchase—five bottles of beer and a plate of snacks—and watched the singers and dancers for a while. Then he rose to leave. Two bottles of beer remained unopened. At the counter, he said they should not be included in his bill.

The nightclub explained that the amount was the “minimum purchase” and had to be paid whether the beer was consumed or not. Chung insisted that he would not pay for what he had not drunk. Although this put the nightclub in a very difficult position, it ultimately followed his wishes and removed the price of the two bottles from the bill.

Chung would not waste a single coin. The incident illustrates his simplicity and thrift.

Lee Gwang-ha, age fifty-two, at Cheil Industries' Gumi plant received an order. It came from Chairman Lee Byung-chull: develop “the finest fabric in the world.” By then, Cheil Industries had developed for more than thirty years and had gained wide recognition in Korea. Lee's sights, however, were set on the highest standard in the world.

During his lifetime, Chairman Lee was the most fashionable figure in the Korean business community. Even as a young man, he wore the finest pure-wool clothing made in Britain, and he was exceedingly particular about his attire.

Cheil Industries assigned the task to Lee Gwang-ha, who immediately began preparing. Since joining the company in 1968, Lee had spent twenty years identifying and selecting wool and was considered the foremost figure in the field.

To produce the finest fabric in the world, he began by selecting the wool itself. First he took the best-quality fibers, from the sheep's shoulders, then selected the finest strands one by one by hand. From wool that ordinarily measured 18.5 microns, he chose only the finest fibers, measuring 16.5 microns. From three tons of wool, Lee ultimately selected two hundred pounds, a process that took ten days. Choosing fine wool by the feel of one's fingers was arduous work.

He spun those two hundred pounds into yarn, producing 100-count fabric. A 100-count yarn, the source explains, means that one gram of wool can be drawn out to a length of one hundred meters. The resulting thread was finer than the silk spun by a young spider.

Korea became the third country in the world to develop 100-count yarn, after the fashion leaders Italy and Japan. It was a remarkable achievement for Cheil Industries.

The next challenge was 130-count yarn: developing the world's finest 14.5-micron wool. When the 130-count fabric appeared, it was highly praised in Australia, a country renowned for the quality of its wool.

Australia's wool market is the most demanding in the world, dividing wool into as many as 917 types. Even wool of identical thickness is classified into seven grades. It was in this market that the 130-count fabric won recognition. Light, soft, warm, and highly breathable, it ranked among the finest fabrics in the world. Following Lee Byung-chull's instruction, Lee Gwang-ha had developed 130-count fabric of world-leading quality.

In 1987, despite being seventy-seven years old, Lee personally oversaw affairs under the conviction that every company he had founded should become a world-class enterprise.

Lee ordinarily favored American-style casual wear, with attractive-colored cashmere trousers. Possessing an exceptional sense of color, he wore pink T-shirts that were extremely rare at the time. His aesthetic sensibility directly influenced Cheil Industries' technological development.

Chairman Chung Ju-yung, by contrast, paid no attention to clothing. This may have been related to the industries he ran: in shipbuilding and heavy industry, excessive concern with attire could interfere with work. He was also personally unpretentious and did not wish to devote much thought to his clothes.

He wore the same jacket for more than ten years and did not care even when its lapels became damaged. The only fashionable item he owned was his hat. When he crossed Panmunjom to visit North Korea, television coverage invariably showed him wearing a fedora. He owned more than ten hats, but used his gloves and leather jackets until they wore out; only his fedoras suggested the slightest extravagance.

When Chung went to meet National Defense Commission Chairman Kim Jong Il, he wore only a fedora, a worn jacket, and a pair of leather shoes he had owned for more than ten years as he broke through the barrier between North and South. In his entire life, Chung never heard anyone praise his clothes. To Koreans, however, his figure stood taller than it would have in any expensive attire: he was a beloved entrepreneur with the presence of a father.

# The Two Giants' Final Days

I did not choose to come into this world. Countless hopes still fill my heart. I strive to find what I lack and search with all my might. Looking back, everything seems a dream. The years flow like water, leaving many regrets. Time races on and cannot return. I urge you: do not let the wheels of the years turn in vain.

A fine drizzle had been falling since daylight. Lee Byung-chull's health was poor. He had once stood 1.67 meters tall and weighed fifty-seven kilograms, with a well-proportioned build, and had enjoyed excellent health from youth. In old age, however, he underwent surgery once for stomach cancer and once on his brain. He was now seventy-seven, already an elderly man.

Lee spent his final years at 135-26, Itaewon 1-dong. The property covered more than three hundred *pyeong* and contained a traditional Korean house of over one hundred *pyeong*, along with two ancillary buildings. The residence was an orthodox Korean *hanok*. Lee loved *hanok*, and the house was built under his direction. Samoo Architects & Engineers and Samsung Construction completed its design and construction three years before Chairman Lee died.

After Chairman Lee's death, Chairman Lee Kun-hee named the *hanok* Seungjiwon, meaning a garden that carries on his father's will. The book Lee Kun-hee now reads most often is *Ho-Am's Management Philosophy*. Perhaps for this reason, his understanding of his father is distinctive.

Rather than working mainly from his office on the twenty-eighth floor of Samsung headquarters, Lee Kun-hee conducted much of his work at Seungjiwon, where he both received guests and attended to business.

This year, Seungjiwon underwent a large-scale renovation that transformed it into a residence equipped with cutting-edge technology. The standard was the private home of Microsoft president Bill Gates in Seattle. Completed in July 2000 after seven years of construction, Gates's house incorporated state-of-the-art technology, including an unmanned security system, voice-recognition devices, and a high-speed communications network. Guests carrying pins encoded with their personal information could enjoy their preferred music, fragrances, and images wherever they went in the house. Computers controlled the air-conditioning and adjusted the indoor temperature automatically, while an unmanned security system protected the property.

This was the network system of the home of the future. Yet Seungjiwon's exterior retained the authentic character of the *hanok* and was not altered at all.

Before the home network was installed, Lee taught his grandchildren *The Analects* there. He himself read widely, from novels to historical works, but *The Analects* affected him most deeply. He believed that it alone encompassed all the virtues a person should possess. Its contents help shape character, cultivate the self, and maintain human relationships, while also embracing the whole range of ethics, morality, and standards of conduct. As Lee managed his businesses, he continually reflected on the spirit of *The Analects* and put it into practice. Born into a family of eminent Confucian scholars, he had studied *The Analects* from childhood and lived by its truths.

In Lee's mind, anyone who wished to become an entrepreneur first had to possess the right attitude and upright conduct. This was also why he personally taught *The Analects* to his grandchildren.

Apart from spending time with his grandchildren, Lee went to the golf course whenever he had a spare moment. Before his death, however, he had grown too weak to play the game he loved. Even so, he continued to visit the course. Although unable to play, he enjoyed drinking tea and taking in the atmosphere of Anyang Golf Course, which he had personally designed and built.

Lee was deeply attached to Anyang Golf Course because it was one of the works into which he had poured his heart. He personally developed its Jungji turf. The course was originally planted with golden grass, but careful observation showed that it grew poorly and could not withstand harsh winters and the rainy season. Lee then imported turf from the United States and Japan before ultimately ordering a laboratory to cultivate a variety suited to Korea's climate and soil: Jungji turf. He even chose the trees at Anyang Golf Course one by one. Fast-growing conifers were planted first, but he found that they did not suit the course's layout or atmosphere. More than five hundred trees were therefore dug up and replaced with visually attractive specimens selected from across the country. To help the new trees grow, leftover cabbage leaves from kimchi-making were brought in as fertilizer. Crape myrtles were initially planted as flowers, but when they failed to thrive, all were replaced with daffodils. Every blade of grass, every tree, and every flower embodied Lee's painstaking care. He designed the course with the same dedication he brought to his businesses. Did he do so merely to indulge the personal taste of a powerful *chaebol* chairman? No. He did it because of his conviction that whatever he undertook must be the best, with quality first.

Anyang Golf Course held many happy memories for Lee. In the final autumn of his life, its golden turf rippled in the wind and autumn colors filled his view. Seeing the grass entirely yellow made him deeply sad. His eldest daughter, In-hee, was caring for him and was distressed to see her father this way. She instructed employees to dye the yellow grass

green, hoping to give her father hope, like the plot of O. Henry's short story “The Last Leaf.”

Lee's last visit to Anyang Golf Course came twenty days before his death. He arrived at about 4:30 that afternoon. Early winter was approaching and dusk had already begun to fall. Lee very much wanted to play. Those around him hurriedly prepared the equipment, and he went to the first tee. By then he could barely grip a club. He swung and missed his first shot. Ordinarily he was such a skilled player that he needed no practice motion after taking hold of a club, but that day was different. His golfing skill remained, yet his strength had completely deserted him. On his second stroke, the ball traveled only ten meters before stopping. He normally drove about 230 yards; now he seemed a different man. He asked the golfer standing beside him to tee off. That player hit a shot as well, and together they walked toward the next hole, beginning a formal match. The dusk deepened until the ball was no longer visible, but Lee insisted on continuing. They went to the third hole and played on to the fourth, a par-three. This was already too much exertion for a seventy-seven-year-old, yet Lee remained enthusiastic and continued through the eighth hole. By then it was completely dark and lights were needed. When the ball could no longer be seen, four golf carts, three motorcycles, and the car carrying Lee followed close behind with their headlights on. By this illumination they reached the eighth and ninth holes. In the end, Lee was left with regret because his body could no longer support him. He sat in a golf cart in front of the clubhouse and rode around it three times, his heart filled with emotion.

Starting with an inheritance of three hundred *seok* from his father, Lee's business life, which began with a grain-processing mill, had been full of hardship. While producing Star-brand noodles, he slept with his family in a cramped house. When the Korean War broke out, he lived under enemy rule. As a refugee in Busan, he founded Cheil Jedang. After the May 16 military coup, he was branded an illicit profiteer and announced that he would turn over his entire fortune to the state. Later, he suffered the pain of surrendering Korea Fertilizer, a company he had built through arduous work, to the government. Entrepreneurship in Korea had brought him untold difficulties.

In the spring of 1980, Chun Doo-hwan's new military regime confiscated his Tongyang Broadcasting Company, TBC. Lee had crossed countless obstacles and struggled without cease for a full fifty years, building dozens of companies amid adversity. Memories of those years must have passed through his mind like images in a revolving lantern. His regrettable round of golf ended, and Lee never returned to a golf course.

Twenty days after leaving Anyang Golf Course, on November 19, 1987, Lee reached the end of his seventy-seven-year life and fifty-year business career. The thirty-seven companies he personally established or acquired included Cheil Jedang (1953), Cheil Industries (1954), Tongyang Life[^FB-F047] (1957), Ankuk Fire and Marine Insurance (1958), Shinsegae Department Store (1962), Samsung Foundation of Culture (1965), Jeonju Paper (1965), JoongAng Development (1966), Korea General Hospital (1966), *JoongAng Ilbo* and Tongyang Broadcasting (1966), Samsung Electronics (1969), Samsung SDI (1970), Cheil Synthetic Fiber (1972), Samsung Electric (1973), Samsung Corning (1973), Shilla Hotel (1973), Samsung Petrochemical (1974), Samsung Heavy Industries (1974), Yongin Nature Park (1975), Korea Industrial (1978), and Samsung Semiconductor and Telecommunications (1980).

This twentieth-century business giant, a giant of the age, had left the world. He had started with three hundred *seok*. The legacy produced by fifty years of struggle was developed by his third son, Lee Kun-hee, into a world-class group with 166 affiliated companies and sales of 103.9918 trillion won.

Both Lee's life and his business career were extraordinarily successful. Even so, three concerns remained unresolved: Mi-pung's failure to defeat Miwon in the seasoning business, problems involving his children, and golf.

Throughout his life, Lee regretted that Mi-pung, produced by Cheil Jedang, had failed to defeat Miwon of Daesang Group. Countless times, he encouraged Cheil Jedang employees to unite and overcome Miwon, but Mi-pung never surpassed its rival even once.

Miwon was a representative Japanese seasoning product, its Korean name taken directly from the Japanese *Ajinomoto*. At the time, the concept of “seasoning” did not exist, and the term Miwon itself was used to denote seasoning, giving the brand an advantage from the outset. First impressions took hold, and Miwon became deeply imprinted in consumers' minds not as one seasoning but as seasoning itself.

Samsung's senior leadership, headed by Lee, concluded that defeating Miwon would require the passage of time; it could not be achieved by their generation. Mi-pung might overcome Miwon only after the children then in elementary school had grown up.

The competition later became a “war of taste,” and circumstances changed somewhat, but Lee did not live to see his goal realized.

Victory came in another form. Lee's grandson Lee Jae-yong, the eldest son of Lee Kun-hee, married the eldest granddaughter of the founder of his old rival Miwon Group, now Daesang Group. One wonders how Lee would react in the afterlife to becoming related by marriage to his lifelong competitor.

Problems involving their children troubled Chung Ju-yung as well as Lee Byung-chull. Lee had three sons: his eldest, Lee Maeng-hee; his second, Lee Chang-hee; and his third, Lee Kun-hee. In the end, he handed Samsung to Lee Kun-hee, though the process was highly complex.

In his autobiography, *Ho-Am: An Autobiography*, Lee wrote that he had considered the succession question for a long time. The book was published two years before his death.

In other words, he had decided who would inherit Samsung before he died.

He assessed each of his three sons: “I once tried entrusting part of the group's management to my eldest son, Maeng-hee, but in less than six months, the company's operations fell into complete disorder. He took responsibility and resigned.” This made clear that Lee would not hand Samsung to his eldest son.

On this question, Lee Maeng-hee said that his experience was not limited to six months of management; he had also participated in management for seven years. His eagerness to achieve results had undermined unity. Samsung had hundreds of thousands of employees and placed enormous importance on winning people's support. The same was true not only of Samsung but of every major corporate group. Lee Maeng-hee later inherited Cheil Jedang Group.

“My second son, Chang-hee, does not wish to manage a large enterprise with great numbers of people and a complex structure. He would rather

run a moderately sized company, and I have decided to respect his choice.”

Lee's second son, Lee Chang-hee, was therefore not Samsung's successor either. He later established Saehan Group independently. The right to inherit Samsung ultimately fell to Lee's third son, Lee Kun-hee.

“[Kun-hee's] interests and aspirations lie in business management, and he can participate actively and study diligently. . . . I hope that entrusting Samsung to him will become an opportunity for the group's further development, and I believe that giving Samsung to Kun-hee is the right decision.” After Lee's death, Samsung duly passed to Lee Kun-hee, who developed the group on an even larger scale.

The final source of dissatisfaction in Lee's life was golf. He generally shot between 82 and 84 and recorded three holes-in-one, an exceptionally high standard. Yet he did not play that well every time; occasionally he scored over 100. With people other than his close friends, he created a golfing organization called the Wednesday Club, principally a gathering where business figures played together and deepened their relationships.

They wagered 1,000 won and discussed economic issues as they played. Because everyone was well mannered, Lee could generally play at his own level and achieve a good score. Matters were entirely different when he played with people who ignored golf etiquette. Representative examples were Korean Central Intelligence Agency director Kim Hyong-uk and Presidential Security Service chief Park Jong-gyu. Park was notably deficient in golf etiquette, while Kim behaved almost like a scoundrel. He too was a golf fanatic, arriving at the course at five every morning, finishing his round, and reporting to his office at seven.

Kim's specialty was taking other people's money. If he lost a wager, he had a subordinate telephone to say that his superior was looking for him, then took back the money he had staked. If he won, he shamelessly sent someone to the other player's office the next day to collect. Lee found it difficult to play well with such men because he despised people of such low character. These were the three matters that troubled him.

Chung Ju-yung lived fourteen years longer than Lee Byung-chull. Between Lee's death in 1987 and 2001, Chung accomplished a great deal.

First among these achievements was the Seosan seawall project, completed three years before Lee's death.

One day in February 1984, Chung stood at the Seosan Area A seawall construction site, gazing from a distance at a 270-meter gap in the 6,400-meter embankment. Even from afar, the current looked so violent that it seemed capable of pulling a person in. Blocks of stone the size of automobiles disappeared without a trace when dropped into the water. Fifteen- and thirty-ton trucks at the site carried stone blocks made from gravel and reinforcing steel and tipped them into the gap, only to see the current sweep them away in an instant. The seawall had to be closed at this point, but the rushing water left the builders helpless.

Chung often found a way out when every route seemed exhausted. After racking his brain, he devised a solution. He remembered the *Waterwill*, then anchored in Ulsan, and bought the vessel from Norway for three billion won; it had been destined for scrap.

At 322 meters long, the *Waterwill* exceeded the gap that had to be closed by fifty meters. Weighing 230,000 tons, it could not be swept away as easily as ordinary stone blocks.

Chung immediately ordered the *Waterwill* towed to Seosan. On February 25, it was sunk across the 270-meter gap in the embankment. Just as he had expected, the current could not carry away this colossus. It worked!

By using a 230,000-ton obsolete tanker and inventing the “tanker closure method,” Chung completed the Seosan seawall and attracted attention in Korea and around the world. The construction method was unprecedented. It saved twenty-three billion won in costs and greatly shortened the construction period. More than that, completion of the seawall changed the map of Korea, creating thirty-three million *pyeong* of new farmland—an area larger than the Gimje Plain, Korea's largest, and thirty-three times the size of Yeouido.

After an initial period of salt production, experimental farming began in 1987. The farm's 550,000-*pyeong* pasture raised more than two thousand cattle. Its rice production was enough to feed Ulsan's population of five hundred thousand for an entire year.

In 1990, Chung met Soviet president Mikhail Gorbachev. On his first visit to the Soviet Union, he proposed a plan to develop Siberia. About a month after Chung returned home, President Roh Tae-woo visited the Soviet Union. The two countries established diplomatic relations and normalized their ties.

Following the normalization of diplomatic relations, Chung became chairman of the Korea-Soviet Economic Association and actively pursued the development of natural-gas and oil fields in the Russian Far East and Siberia.

From that time onward, Chung paid close attention to the North Korean question. On June 16, 1998, he drove five hundred cattle across Panmunjom on a visit to North Korea, attracting worldwide attention. Nine years after becoming the first private entrepreneur to visit North Korea, he arrived there once again with five hundred cattle. It was the first time in history that anyone had crossed the gates of Panmunjom, closed for half a century. Witnessing his extraordinary act, a French cultural critic called it a “world festival of art.” His visit broke the ice in relations between North and South and created an atmosphere of peace.

In October 1998, Chung visited North Korea as a private entrepreneur and met its supreme leader, Kim Jong Il. He proposed the Mount Kumgang tourism project. People already amazed by his cattle drive to North Korea acclaimed this new idea as another stroke of genius.

Chung subsequently visited North Korea many times in pursuit of new development for both sides. His next project was to connect a railway running through North and South Korea with the Trans-Siberian Railway.

He hoped to keep working until he was 120. “I will work until I am 120,” Chairman Chung told the German current-affairs magazine *Der Spiegel* in an interview two years before his death.

His remarks showed that he firmly believed a doctor's assurance that a person could live to 120. He added, “I still feel young. It is not yet time for me to retire.” He was already eighty-four, but remained intensely enthusiastic about work.

Asked what he intended to accomplish during his remaining forty years, Chung answered in concrete terms: enter the construction markets of North Korea and third countries, the segmented ship market, the automotive audio and video manufacturing market, the communications field, and the west-coast industrial complex, among other areas.

Finally, he said he would devote all his strength to the great cause of reunification and, once North and South were united, return to live in his northern hometown.

A little more than a year after the *Der Spiegel* interview, the health of the man who had said he would live to 120 suddenly deteriorated.

During a visit to North Korea in June 2000, he held talks and drank *makgeolli*[^FB-F048] with National Defense Commission Chairman Kim Jong Il for four and a half hours. Afterward, his mental condition deteriorated sharply. For a man in his eighties, four and a half hours of drinking was excessive. Chung subsequently felt tired often and was hospitalized several times. Even so, two months before his death, he went to a department store to buy bread and shared a lunch of pufferfish with Lee Byung-gyu, president of Hyundai Department Store.

Snowflakes drifted down in early March 2001. At his home in Cheongun-dong, Chung rested in bed for a while with stomach cramps, then went into the courtyard and said to his housekeeper, then already seventy-three, “You're still young. How did your hair turn so white?”

“The snow has turned the whole world white. I couldn't escape it either,” the housekeeper replied. Chung burst out laughing.

A few days later, Chung's health deteriorated rapidly. On March 22, 2001, he reached the end of his eighty-seven-year life at Asan Central Hospital.

Immediately after Chairman Chung died, his family hurriedly sent someone to Samsung Group to learn how his funeral should be conducted. Samsung had organized Chairman Lee Byung-chull's funeral in 1987 and that of Lee's wife, Park Du-eul, in 2000, giving it experience with two large funerals. Although the two groups ordinarily competed, in bereavement they displayed the virtue of supporting each other. Hyundai Group arranged Chairman Chung's funeral according to Samsung Group's advice.

At the mourning hall set up in Chung's Cheongun-dong home, former presidents Chun Doo-hwan, Roh Tae-woo, and Kim Young-sam, along with representatives of the governing and opposition parties, paid their respects. North Korean leader Kim Jong Il also sent a representative with a wreath. From the business community came Samsung Chairman Lee Kun-hee, LG honorary chairman Koo Ja-kyung, Hanjin Group honorary chairman Cho Choong-hoon, and others.

Standing on either side of the hall were Chung's sons Chung Mong-koo, Chung Mong-hun, and Chung Mong-joon, along with the subordinates who had weathered decades of hardship beside him: former Hyundai Engineering & Construction president Lee Myung-bak; former Hyundai Petrochemical chairman Lee Hyun-tae; former Hyundai Corporation chairman Lee Chun-rim; former Hyundai Development Company president Shim Hyun-young; Incheon Steel chairman Park Se-yong; former Hyundai Engineering & Construction president Lee Nae-heun; and the company's current president, Kim Yoon-kyu. Having shared Chung's trials for decades, their grief was no less than that of his children. Like a film, countless hardships endured over their many years following Chung passed through their minds.

Scenes from the Narathiwat Highway project must have risen in Lee Myung-bak's mind; Park Se-yong must have recalled the grueling week when he could not even bathe while preparing bid materials for the Jubail port project; and Kim Yoon-kyu must have remembered Chung coming to see him and saying, “You haven't got a scratch on you, have you? Come away on a business trip with me.”

Koreans watching the mourning hall on television were also overwhelmed with grief. Chung had continually given courage and conviction to Korea's forty million people, and the giant's death pained the nation.

The generation that had earned dollars for the nation—working amid artillery fire on Vietnam's battlefields and in desert regions where temperatures reached sixty degrees Celsius—had completed its mission and must also have wished Chung peace on his final journey. As if to bear this out, more than twenty-three thousand mourners came, and the line of wreaths extended 350 meters. It was effectively a state funeral. Chung's Hyundai Group comprised eighty-three companies and had generated 100 billion dollars, or 130 trillion won, in exports, placing it alongside world giants such as General Electric of the United States, at 110 billion dollars, and Toyota, at 115 billion dollars.[^TN-CH25-001]

Yet in daily life, Chung was an entirely ordinary person. The household items in his Cheongun-dong home fell far below the standard found in an average person's residence: collapsed old leather shoes, a cloth wallet worn through with holes, white cloth in place of carpeting, and a battered Goldstar television. The man who had stood at the forefront of Korea's construction never spared his own body.

The giant of the world economy who had risen on Korean soil was no different from an ordinary person in death.

I did not choose to come into this world.

Countless hopes still fill my heart.

I strive to find what I lack.

I search with all my might.
Looking back, everything seems a dream.
The years flow like water, leaving many regrets.
Time races on and cannot return.
I urge you: do not let the wheels of the years turn in vain.

These were the lyrics of “An Ordinary Life,” the popular song he most loved to sing. Just as the lyrics said, he spent his life struggling ceaselessly in pursuit of his dreams.

With his own hands, Chung built more than eighty companies, including Hyundai Engineering & Construction (1950), Hyundai Motor (1967), Hyundai Cement (1969), Hyundai Heavy Industries (1973), Hyundai Mipo Dockyard (1975), and Hyundai Electronics (1985). He served as chairman of Seoul's Olympic bid committee, chairman of the Federation of Korean Industries for five consecutive terms, president of the Korean Sport & Olympic Committee, and chairman of the Korea-Soviet Economic Association, working tirelessly day and night.

He was an industrious Korean. Rising at three in the morning, he reviewed faxes from abroad until five, then took telephone calls, met visitors, and read the newspapers. At 7:30, he left for the office and began the day's work.

While traveling by car, he liked to sing popular songs such as “Sunrise,” “Morning Dew,” “Honestly,” “Longing,” and “Encounter.”

His temper was fiery. Entering a construction site at five or six in the morning, he would not stop until he had confirmed completely that the work was progressing smoothly. Because of his impatience, not one of his eight children escaped being struck by him.

Although he had completed only elementary school, his English and Japanese were good enough for conversation. Even at his busiest, he carried a vocabulary notebook and memorized English words.

He wanted to work at Seosan when he was 120.

He was one of twentieth-century Korea's great men.

# The Revolt of the Princes and the Successors

Thought becomes action, action develops into habit, and habit develops into character. Character will determine a person's destiny.

—Chung Ju-yung

During his lifetime, Chung Ju-yung was a highly successful entrepreneur. The son of a poor farming family without even a patch of land on which to stand, he became an entrepreneur who owned more than eighty-three companies with sales exceeding 130 trillion won—an achievement that can only be called an extraordinary success. Yet like Lee Byung-chull, he failed to resolve the problems involving his children entirely satisfactorily.

His eldest son, Chung Mong-pil, who had served as president of Incheon Steel Group, died in a traffic accident. As Chung Ju-yung's health deteriorated, his second son, Chairman Chung Mong-koo, and his fifth, Chairman Chung Mong-hun, began a fierce struggle over succession to the group.

During this process, Chung Ju-yung's younger brother Chung In-yung and In-yung's eldest son, Chung Mong-gyu, who had effectively managed Hyundai Motor, agreed to relinquish management control. At the same time, a plan was developed to divide Hyundai into several groups. Hyundai Motor Group, including Kia Motors, would go to Chairman Chung Mong-koo. Hyundai Engineering & Construction, Hyundai Corporation, Hyundai Securities, and other companies centered on Hyundai Electronics would go to Chairman Chung Mong-hun. Hyundai Heavy Industries and two other companies would be entrusted to adviser Chung Mong-joon.

Chairman Chung Ju-yung also waged a fierce internal struggle at this time. For a period, funding shortages at Hyundai Investment Trust exposed liquidity problems at the parent company, Hyundai Engineering & Construction, plunging the entire group into crisis. Chung played the card of having the father and three sons—Mong-koo and Mong-joon, as the source lists them—withdraw simultaneously from front-line management. In the end, however, Hyundai was divided around the three brothers Mong-koo, Mong-hun, and Mong-joon.[^TN-CH26-001]

Hyundai Motor Group, led by Chung Mong-koo in the role of eldest son, ranked fifth in the Korean business world. In addition to its three pillar companies—Hyundai Motor, Kia Motors, and Hyundai Mobis—it owned eleven companies including Incheon Steel, Hyundai Hysco, Sammi Specialty Steel, Hyundai Capital, and Hyundai Aerospace. The twenty-three companies large and small under Chung Mong-hun, including Hyundai Electronics, or Hyundai Hynix, and Hyundai Securities, ranked second nationwide in the Financial Supervisory Service's 2000 performance table, with sales of 61.8974 trillion won.

Hyundai Heavy Industries Group, led by Chung Mong-joon, also recorded sales of 6.6 trillion won last year, including net profits exceeding 15.1 billion won.

In addition, Chung Ju-yung's sixth son, Chung Mong-yoon, served as chairman of Hyundai Credit Finance,

while his younger brother Chung Mong-il served as the company's president.

After Samsung Chairman Lee Byung-chull died in 1987, his eldest son, Lee Maeng-hee, made the largest conglomerate independent; his second son, Lee Chang-hee, founded Saehan Electrics; and his daughter Lee In-hee established Hansol Group. Hyundai underwent a similar process.

In the Financial Supervisory Service's 2000 financial tables, however, Chairman Lee Kun-hee's Samsung still ranked firmly in first place. This demonstrates the excellence of Lee Kun-hee's management ability.

Only a few months have passed since Hyundai Chairman Chung Ju-yung's death. As time goes by, the true shape of Hyundai Group will gradually emerge.

Although Chairman Chung Mong-hun, the fifth son, appeared on the surface to be Hyundai's legitimate successor, he no longer controlled Hyundai Engineering & Construction, the group's original enterprise. Hyundai Hynix also faced severe difficulties, and its ultimate fate remained a focus of public attention.

By contrast, Hyundai Motor Group under Chairman Chung Mong-koo remained relatively stable.

Chairman Lee Kun-hee took over Samsung's management from his father at the end of 1987, fourteen years ago. The transformation of Samsung's management approach can be said to have begun with the Frankfurt Declaration of 1993. The so-called New Management initiative was Lee Kun-hee's creation. He sounded the alarm with the striking words, “Change everything except your wife and children.”

“The sense of crisis created by Samsung's present condition and the changes at the end of the century often sent chills down my spine. . . . If we continued this way, Samsung would not merely lose one or two companies; it might face bankruptcy. In this state of mind, especially from the summer through the winter of 1992, my weight dropped by more than ten kilograms.” Such was Chairman Lee Kun-hee's state of mind when he proclaimed New Management.

“Change begins with me” captured the reality of Samsung's 1993 decision to implement New Management amid a host of crises. The transformation revealed an entirely new Samsung.

Through three rounds of restructuring, Samsung completed the reorganization of its smaller affiliates and introduced

new measures including an early-start, early-finish work schedule, from seven in the morning to four in the afternoon; consumer cultural centers; reform of personnel and education systems; the creation of social-service organizations; freedom from uniforms for female employees; support for small and medium-sized enterprises; and integrated overseas production bases in Britain, Mexico, and elsewhere. The seven-to-four schedule in particular brought a breath of fresh air to Korean corporate culture and gave other companies a new stimulus. Samsung's practice of “standing in the consumer's position” when conflicts arose also won broad consumer approval. Samsung today is pursuing “quality management.”

At the Los Angeles strategy conference in February 1995, Lee Byung-chull announced that he would “take responsibility only for semiconductors, automobiles, and social service, and leave everything else entirely to professionals.”[^TN-CH26-002] Thereafter, the space for the chairman's approval disappeared from Samsung documents.

Although Chairman Lee Kun-hee led Samsung Group, with annual sales as high as 103 trillion won, in practice he made relatively few approvals. Each company's president decided its management strategy independently. This was the management philosophy that allowed Samsung to operate successfully. It would be wrong, however, to conclude that Lee Kun-hee merely marked time. Decisions essential to the group's survival, such as semiconductor investments, naturally remained his responsibility.

Because few specific matters required his approval, Lee did not often go to his office on the twenty-eighth floor of Samsung headquarters. He worked primarily from his private office at Seungjiwon.

Chairman Lee Kun-hee had many areas of interest: golf, automobiles, video, flowers, food, films, television dramas, economic documentaries, Jindo dogs, reading, and more. For this reason, he had a private secretary for each field of management.

He first investigated and analyzed necessary matters extensively, then reflected and developed his ideas. At any time, he might summon the chairmen and presidents of affiliated companies to Seungjiwon, his private workplace in Itaewon-dong, for a discussion. Sometimes they talked all day; at other times, until dawn. In short, these meetings could occur at any moment. Their principal subjects were the direction of corporate management in the twenty-first century, the role of large enterprises, trends in cutting-edge technology, and related issues, but they were not limited to these. Lee also discussed a wide variety of detailed matters, showing himself to be meticulous yet approachable. The discussion naturally took place in a relaxed atmosphere without neckties, where everyone spoke freely and without inhibition. Because Lee himself never drank, however, no alcohol was served.

His work now centered on three tasks:

first, producing first-rate products; second, using them to build Samsung into a truly world-class enterprise; and third, conducting strategic research in order to survive in an era of constant change. Toward these goals, he studied intently and thought comprehensively. The material for this research and reflection might come from watching a film, viewing an economic documentary, or reading a book. It might come from dismantling automobile components or registering the Jindo dog as a protected animal. It might also involve copying valuable video materials and distributing them to employees throughout the company—for example, a videotape on raising a goldfish worth thirty million won, or a DVD about a bonsai worth more than one hundred million won. Lee also distributed Japanese writer Ryohei Kuri's children's story *One Bowl of Noodles* to every employee. These were not merely private hobbies. They were a means by which Samsung Group kept its employees alert, while providing fertile ground for a corporate culture devoted to “producing first-rate products.”

He watched and collected more than fifteen thousand videotapes. Sometimes he sent one to a company president, sometimes to employees at the director or section-chief level, and sometimes to staff members. He persisted in doing so as a kind of inoculation that kept employees vigilant.

He also took golf beyond the realm of personal interest and incorporated it into his investment plans. Pak Se-ri, who later became one of the world's leading female golfers, was sponsored by Samsung. Once she became famous, Samsung's brand received the greatest return from a minimal investment.

Chairman Lee Kun-hee's hobbies were horseback riding and golf. Almost every week, he rode for three or four hours in a single day at Anyang Golf Course. About once every two months, he went out on the course to play golf, but ordinarily he hit balls at the driving range.

His approach to health was inherited from his late father, Chairman Lee Byung-chull. Although he did not drink alcohol, he smoked three to four packs of cigarettes a day.

# The Men Who Built the Empires

Everyone experiences good fortune and bad. Fortune is a matter of time. When one works happily and joyfully throughout the twenty-four hours of the day and the four seasons of the year,

that can be called good fortune. For those who work hard because they do not wish to let good fortune escape, there is no place for misfortune to stand.

—Chung Ju-yung

Most of the men who rendered distinguished service under the two giants, Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung, have now retired. Samsung Corporation chairmen Lee Chang-eop and Kyung Joo-hyun and Samsung Electronics chairman Chung Jae-eun were leading figures during the economic leap of the 1970s; all have left front-line work because of age. More recently, Samsung Corporation vice chairman Lee Pil-gon handed the company to Chairman Hyun Myung-kwan. Chairmen Kang Jin-koo and Kim Kwang-ho, who created the electronics and semiconductor legend, turned their businesses over to representative director and vice chairman Yoon Jong-yong and president Lee Hak-soo. Lee Gil-hyun, a former president of Shilla Hotel who had been especially favored by Chairman Lee Byung-chull, left the front line to become an adviser, while Samsung Construction chairman Park Ki-seok was reassigned as an adviser to Ulsan Construction.

The situation at Hyundai was much the same. Hyundai Engineering & Construction chairman Lee Myung-bak entered politics, while Jeon Gap-won and most of the other contributors who had devoted themselves to Middle Eastern construction projects had also left front-line management.

Among the veterans who worked during Lee Byung-chull's era, management specialists still at Samsung included Chairmen Lee Soo-bin and Lee Dae-won. Lee Byung-chull trusted Lee Soo-bin greatly; Lee now chaired Samsung Life and led the financial group. Lee Dae-won served as vice chairman of Samsung Heavy Industries and Samsung Aerospace.

Hyun Myung-kwan, now chairman of Samsung Corporation, passed the civil-service examination and worked for a time in the public prosecutors' office. After joining Samsung, he began as president of Shilla Hotel and Samsung Watch. He then demonstrated exceptional ability as head of the Chairman's Secretariat and ultimately rose to become vice chairman and chairman of Samsung Corporation.

Samsung's Chairman's Secretariat has now been reorganized as the Corporate Restructuring Headquarters, which operates through a system of collective consultation. It holds weekly meetings devoted principally to the group's future direction and long-term vision.

Immediate difficulties facing the group are studied and resolved by each company's chairman, president, or more senior group head, so they fall outside these discussions. Chairman Lee Kun-hee naturally does not participate. The personnel principle inherited from his father—“Do not employ those you doubt; do not doubt those you employ”—remains in effect. Lee once convened seminars for president-level executives twice a year; now even these have been eliminated.

At Samsung Group's highest level stood Chairmen Lee Soo-bin and Lee Dae-won, both recruited in the sixth open intake. Graduates of Seoul National University's College of Business Administration, they were masters of financial management. When problems arose in corporate operations, they could draw on their influence and standing in the business community to obtain strong assistance from relevant figures and resolve the difficulties.

Although Samsung Life entered the insurance industry relatively late, its performance quickly rose to first place under Lee Soo-bin, a quintessential Samsung management specialist.

After graduating from Seoul National University's Department of Economics, Lee Soo-bin joined Samsung affiliate Cheil Jedang and volunteered for its Busan plant, beginning his career as a Samsung man. He chose a regional posting to learn factory operations. Beginning in his second year in Busan, he spent six years rotating through accounting, planning, and management, and in 1970 assumed the important position of accounting-section chief at Cheil Jedang. Gifted in both calculation and memory, he was promoted in 1972 to a management role in the Chairman's Secretariat, where he worked for four years. During that period, when external auditors questioned him, Lee answered fluently and omitted not a single management figure. His meticulous replies deeply impressed the auditors and became a celebrated story, illustrating his exceptional memory and numerical ability.

While working in the Secretariat in 1972, Lee proposed a group management plan for the twenty-first century: Samsung's second five-year plan. Under it, Samsung's sales soared from 103.6 billion won to 966.3 billion won in five years, an increase of fully 930 percent. This established Chairman Lee Byung-chull's trust in him.

Because of these achievements, twelve years after joining the company, at age thirty-eight and approaching forty, Lee became representative director of Cheil Industries, moving ahead of his contemporaries.

After serving as representative director of companies in textiles, sugar, precision instruments, life insurance, and securities, Lee was promoted in 1991 to head the group Secretariat, a post he held for three years. During that period, he managed group affairs with clarity and order. In 1994, he became chairman of Samsung's financial group and began an exceptionally aggressive style of management.

After taking over Kukje Securities, he reorganized it and relaunched it under the Samsung Securities name;

he thoroughly remade Samsung Credit Finance as the new Samsung Finance; transformed Kukje Securities Futures into Samsung Futures; and acquired Tongyang Investment Trust & Securities, integrating it as Samsung Trust Securities. These forceful measures returned the businesses to normal operation. Within the financial group he now led, Samsung Life, with assets approaching five billion won, served as Samsung Group's cash cow.[^TN-CH27-001]

He spent a total of twenty-four years as a company president. People joked that being president was his profession, and he earned the nickname “the Immortal.”

Lee Dae-won, chairman of the Future Strategy Group, joined the company in the same intake as Lee Soo-bin and was likewise highly proficient in accounting. Beginning as accounting-section chief at Cheil Industries, known as “Samsung's military academy,” he successively served as head of management at the textile, construction, electronics, and sugar companies. In 1987, he returned to Cheil Industries as its representative. Beginning in 1992, he served as representative director of Samsung Aerospace, Samsung Heavy Industries, and Samsung Watch, becoming head of the machinery group.

Lee was not only a meticulous accountant but also a broad-minded leader whose subordinates willingly followed his direction. He preferred inspecting actual conditions on site to sitting shut inside an office, frequently visiting factories and issuing decisions on the spot through a laptop computer. This greatly improved efficiency and won broad praise.

President Park Hong-gi, formerly head of Samsung Corporation's Living Culture Division and its distribution operations, entered Samsung's leadership through the same sixth open intake as the two men above. He took over Cheil Industries from Lee Dae-won. After construction-division president Choi Hun resigned, Cheil Industries representative Yoo Hyun-sik was transferred to become president of Samsung General Chemicals.

These outstanding recruits from the sixth intake formed Samsung's brain trust and helped formulate its principal policies.

Although now retired, the veterans who worked under Chairman Lee Byung-chull—especially Kang Jin-koo, the seventy-four-year-old former chairman of Samsung's electronics and electrical group—could be called the locomotives that drove the Korean national economy in their era.

A graduate of Seoul National University's College of Engineering, Kang Jin-koo witnessed the entire development of Korea's electronics industry and became a legendary figure in the field during the three decades from the 1970s through the 1990s. Beginning in 1957, he joined Korea's television broadcasting system as a technician. He contributed to the launch of KBS television in 1961 and also built broadcasting equipment. After Chairman Park Chung Hee saw camera equipment Kang had made at his official residence, he was delighted and asked, “Whose masterpiece is this? The picture is so clear!”

In 1964, Kang moved to Tongyang Broadcasting, where he first encountered television broadcasting equipment. At Chairman Lee Byung-chull's direction, he took charge of Samsung Electronics in 1973 and fully demonstrated his management ability in the new position, turning the company from loss to profit almost immediately.

Three months after becoming a managing director, Kang was promoted to executive vice president and representative director. Nine months later, he was promoted again, to president, setting a domestic record for the fastest advancement that remains unbroken.

Kang then successively served as representative director of SDI, Samsung Electric, Samsung Precision, and other machinery-related companies. After Samsung Electronics merged with Samsung Semiconductor and Telecommunications in 1988, he helped transform semiconductors, an area with no previous foundation, into a leading business. This achievement contributed to his promotion from representative director and vice chairman to chairman in 1990. When Samsung Group established its “Hall of Fame” in 1995, he was among the first management specialists inducted.

Former vice chairman Kim Kwang-ho, head of the electronics group, came from TBC and was a semiconductor specialist who devoted thirty-five years to Samsung Electronics and semiconductors. Chairman Lee Byung-chull is said to have decided to invest in the semiconductor industry only after reading Kim's report. Kim later became the company's “commander of the night battle” in semiconductors, dedicating himself continually to development with Chairman Lee's wholehearted support.

Kim was a thorough man of action whose management motto was “Let's go—to the workplace!” As a founding contributor and symbolic figure in semiconductors, he led not merely an ordinary group but the much larger electronics group that included Samsung Electronics.

During his tenure as president of Samsung Electronics from 1994 to 1995, the company sold 157.8 million 4-megabit memory chips, ranking first in the world. In 1995, it sold 23 billion 16-megabit memory chips and retained the world-leading position.[^TN-CH27-002]

Driven by strong memory-chip sales, Samsung Electronics' net profit approached three trillion won in 1995, and Kim made an outstanding contribution to its rise as a world-leading company. Originally an engineer, he became a world-class master of semiconductors.

At the time, his every movement could have an enormous effect on the securities market. One city bank even assigned a specialist to monitor everything he did, because a single remark from him could send Samsung Electronics' share price up or down.

Suh Byung-hae was among Chairman Lee Byung-chull's closest associates in the 1970s and 1980s and served longer than anyone else as head of the Chairman's Secretariat. After Chairman Lee Kun-hee took over Samsung Group, Suh was transferred to an inconsequential post, but later returned as vice chairman of Samsung Card.

His return broke Samsung's longstanding personnel tradition that “once expelled, one is never reemployed,” and he once again took a vice-chairman's seat. Later claims that Chairman Lee Kun-hee was broad-minded in his use of people began with Suh's return. Suh now served as an adviser to Samsung Fire & Marine Insurance.

Samsung Corporation chairman Hyun Myung-kwan passed the civil-service examination and worked for a time in the public prosecutors' office before joining Samsung. As head of the Chairman's Secretariat, he won admiration for his ability to reconcile internal conflicts within the group. He also served as president of Shilla Hotel and Samsung Watch. Samsung Corporation recorded sales of 40.64 trillion won last year and employed 4,740 people.

Lee Hak-soo, president-level head of the Corporate Restructuring Headquarters, served successively as accounting-section chief and management director at Cheil Industries, known as “Samsung's military academy.” Promoted to group head in the Secretariat in 1982, he moved to Cheil Jedang as a managing director in 1984 and returned to the Secretariat as finance-group head in 1985. In September 1992, he became deputy head of the Secretariat. He later served as representative director of Samsung Fire & Marine Insurance and Cheil Jedang, returning to the Secretariat again in 1996. Of his twenty-five years at Samsung, he spent ten in the Secretariat. Meticulous calculation, clarity with management figures, and an amiable character made him ideally suited to head Samsung's Corporate Restructuring Headquarters.

Most veterans who had struggled alongside Chairman Lee Byung-chull withdrew from the front line. Yet Shilla Hotel president Lee Young-il, Everland president Huh Tae-hak, and Cheil Communications president Bae Dong-man remained there, joining Hyun Myung-kwan, Lee Hak-soo, and others in leading Samsung into the twenty-first century.

Chairman Chung Ju-yung's veteran associates were headed by Lee Myung-bak, who later entered politics, followed by Lee Nae-heun, age sixty-six; Shim Hyun-young, sixty-two; Park Se-yong, sixty-one; Kim Yoon-kyu, fifty-six; Lee Ik-chi, fifty-six; and Lee Byung-gyu, forty-seven. They represented Hyundai's first and second generations of contributors, with Lee Myung-bak, Lee Nae-heun, Shim Hyun-young, and Park Se-yong belonging to the first.

Real-estate magnate Shim Hyun-young joined the company in 1963 and directed construction sites alongside Chairman Chung. He worked at Hyundai Heavy Industries, Halla Construction, Incheon Steel, and other companies, and briefly became representative of Hyundai Development Company in 1986.

From 1993 to 1995, Shim headed the group's Comprehensive Planning Office and took charge when Chairman Chung ran for president, producing some friction with the government for political reasons. In 1996, as successors Chung Mong-koo and Chung Mong-hun began front-line work, Shim temporarily left Hyundai for Chonggu Group. In 2000, he became representative director of Hyundai Plastic Engineering, a Hyundai Development subsidiary, and now took charge of Hyundai Engineering & Construction amid its liquidity crisis.

Chairman Lee Nae-heun worked in the Presidential General Affairs Secretariat in 1969. A year later, he joined Hyundai Group and became an outstanding management specialist.

While chairman of Hyundai Engineering & Construction, Lee temporarily left Hyundai after failing to secure the 1998 World Cup stadium project in Sangam-dong. Chairman Chung later recalled him to serve as chairman of Hyundai Information Technology.

Whenever the group faced difficulties, Lee drew effectively on his extensive network of personal relationships, rushing tirelessly from place to place and serving as an excellent adviser. During the fourteenth presidential election, he followed Chairman Chung's wishes and entered the race midway, but ultimately lost.

For a time, Lee was demoted to president of Hyundai Development, but three months later, at Chairman Chung's direction, he returned to the presidency of Hyundai Engineering & Construction in September 1996. He was bold in character and exceptionally approachable.

Incheon Steel chairman Park Se-yong graduated from Yonsei University's Department of Business Administration and joined

Hyundai Engineering & Construction in 1967, working mainly on overseas projects and planning. By the time a project was won in 1976, he had risen to sales-department manager. He was a quintessential strategist under Chung. While serving for many years as head of Hyundai Group's Comprehensive Planning Office, he simultaneously headed the merchant-marine and trading companies. In 1999, however, after opposing assistance projects involving North Korea, he was transferred from chairman of Hyundai Merchant Marine to president of Incheon Steel.

Park possessed an extraordinary memory and a forceful personality. Once a decision was made, he drove it toward early completion like a bulldozer, earning the nickname “the Little Bulldozer.”

Chairman Chung arrived for work at seven each morning, but Lee Nae-heun came even earlier and worked late every day before going home. He was a round-the-clock management specialist, happiest when immersed in work.

Kim Yoon-kyu was among the best-known Hyundai management specialists. He served first as chairman of Hyundai Asan and took overall responsibility for assistance projects involving North Korea.

Whenever Chairman Chung visited North Korea, Kim followed him like a shadow. He even called Chung “Father,” demonstrating the depth of Chung's trust in him. Kim frequently attended Chung's family meetings as well.

In 1989, the Korean Air plane carrying Kim crashed in Tripoli, the capital of Libya. Chairman Chung personally visited him in the hospital and then flew with him to Japan on business.

After Hyundai Engineering & Construction's liquidity crisis emerged, Kim endured sustained public pressure to step down. He now concentrated on assistance projects involving North Korea.

Former Hyundai Securities chairman Lee Ik-chi, who began as a secretary, graduated from Seoul National University's College of Business Administration. Under the International Monetary Fund management regime in 1998, he unleashed a “Korean Wave fever” that attracted worldwide attention. But during the controversy over Hyundai's suspected share-price inflation, judicial authorities detained him, adding a disgraceful entry to his management career.

Hyundai Department Store president Lee Byung-gyu joined Hyundai Engineering & Construction in 1977 at age twenty-four, beginning a twenty-year career as Chung's secretary and head of his secretariat.

Always at Chairman Chung's side, he earned the nickname “the Shadow.”

Brothers Chung Mong-koo and Chung Mong-hun acknowledged that Lee Byung-gyu understood their father's mind better than they did. Shortly before his death, Chairman Chung still shared lunch with Lee. Discreet, quick-witted, and fluent in English, he kept confidences closely.

In addition to these men, Kim Jae-soo, age fifty-three, a former secretary who chaired Hyundai's Corporate Restructuring Committee and served as vice chairman of Hyundai Engineering & Construction, was another veteran who had earned Chairman Chung's trust.

# After the Age of the Giants

Chung Ju-yung's “I can do it!” spirit of challenge and Lee Byung-chull's meticulous, detailed analysis and judgment are precious assets bequeathed to our economic history.

Although the age of the two giants has ended, further study and development of the spiritual legacy they left behind will provide a cornerstone for future progress.

The two giants, Chung Ju-yung and Lee Byung-chull, were leading forces in the development of Korea's national economy in their time. Like many figures of our parents' generation, they possessed immense personal charisma and the ability to rally others. No one can deny that they played foundational roles in Korea's economic takeoff after the 1960s.

Compared with Japan and the United States, Korea was once among the countries that placed relatively heavy pressure on businesspeople. As Korea gradually joined the ranks of developed countries, those engaged in economic activity received increasing respect. During the difficult 1950s and 1960s, business experts such as Lee and Chung rushed tirelessly among construction sites, while the Korean people displayed craftsmanship, humility, and honesty equal to those of any other nation. Through their efforts, Korea leaped from a backward, impoverished country to become the world's eleventh-largest economic power. Korea's per capita national income now approaches ten thousand dollars, although it remains below its level before the Southeast Asian financial crisis.

For Korea to maintain strong development momentum in the twenty-first century, today's businesspeople must demonstrate their abilities on the world stage, while the country's policies and institutions align as closely as possible with international standards. Yet Korea is still judged an unsuitable place for enterprise development. The government should therefore act decisively to improve the investment environment and abolish restrictive provisions.

The overall caliber of the Korean people is outstanding by global standards. They have erected high-rise buildings in deserts hotter than sixty degrees Celsius and laid natural-gas pipelines in Siberia at temperatures below minus forty. From sixty above zero to forty below, our people endured a full one-hundred-degree temperature range. It may be difficult to find another people anywhere in the world who have done the same. There are, of course, the Japanese, whose appearance and physique resemble ours. Yet even among the peoples of Asia, it would be hard to find people as adaptable to their environment as Koreans, or as capable of working through sheer force of will.

Chung Ju-yung's “I can do it!” spirit of challenge and Lee Byung-chull's meticulous, detailed analysis and judgment are precious assets bequeathed to our economic history.

Although the age of the two giants has ended, further study and development of the spiritual legacy they left behind will provide a cornerstone for future progress.

# Afterword

Qiji
Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung; Chung Ju-yung and Lee Byung-chull.

Both men have now left this world. Although they were born into different family circumstances, the two giants both became world-renowned entrepreneurs. This was what they shared.

First, both possessed a positive attitude and a passion for doing everything well. Even when everyone around them said something could not be done, they refused to give up.

Chung Ju-yung often asked, “Have you tried it?” Giving up before making an attempt, without ever taking on the challenge, was something he never did.

Chairman Lee Byung-chull was the same. Even when something appeared impossible to others, he approached it through meticulous investigation and research. If he found even the slightest possibility, he was willing to take the chance.

Korea has a saying: “From Gwacheon[^FB-F049] to Seoul, you have to crawl.” It conveys how forbidding Seoul can be. Chairman Lee came to Seoul alone and carved out a world of his own.

Second, they were skilled at employing people, bringing out their initiative, and managing personnel with exceptional ability.

Both Lee and Chung valued the recruitment of talented people and joined them in opening paths to world markets.

Third, even in situations that appeared impossible, they could devise good ideas and thereby achieve their goals.

Chung encountered difficulties while constructing the Jubail port project, just as Lee did while developing the semiconductor industry. Even in unfavorable circumstances, they found solutions and ultimately overcame the difficulties. They never believed in so-called luck. When the situation was adverse, they made every possible effort to solve the problem. Samsung's success in semiconductors is a fine example.

Fourth, both men experienced the setbacks of failure, but neither allowed himself to sink into despondency.

Chairman Chung accumulated enormous debts while building the Goryeong Bridge project and spent nearly twenty years merely repaying them. Yet the experience did not crush him. The same was true when the Narathiwat Highway in Thailand incurred a major deficit. After carefully accumulating experience, he ultimately reaped great rewards from it during the Vietnam War. Perhaps few entrepreneurs experienced as many failures as Chung did. No sooner had his automobile repair shop been built than a fire reduced it to ashes, together with the cars awaiting repair. Although his life was filled with hardship, he never bowed before any difficulty. At his most desperate financially, he even borrowed at usurious interest, yet he always believed tomorrow would be better.

Chairman Lee was ordered to surrender Korea Fertilizer to the state because of a smuggling incident. Later came the May 16 illicit-profiteering affair, during which the government for a time declared that it would confiscate all his property. In 1980, the government nationalized his Tongyang Broadcasting Company. During the Korean War, he lost nearly all his assets. Under Japanese colonial rule, failure in real estate returned him to his starting point. He experienced many failures, but he was also a genius at overcoming difficulty. If he was stronger than an ordinary person, it was because he never dwelled excessively on failure. Instead, he drew lessons from it and did better.

In the same circumstances, an ordinary person might drink himself into a stupor and become ruined for life. These men did not. They stood up again. They failed not merely once or twice, yet every time, they pulled themselves together and rose again like a weighted tumbler.

Fifth, both Chung and Lee were skilled at responding to changes in their era and keeping pace with the times.

Chung successively entered construction, shipbuilding, automobiles, and semiconductors, while Lee took on challenges in sugar, textiles, home appliances, semiconductors, and numerous other fields. Their paths demonstrate their exceptional ability to adapt to changing times. In this respect, both were highly flexible entrepreneurs, not stubborn men trapped in old ways.

Korea's economy is now in difficulty, but if we learn well from the enterprising spirit and courage of these two giants, anyone can rise again. This is what we should truly learn from them.

The persistent conviction that “I can do it” and the power to rally an immediate response from others—these are what we should learn from the lives of Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung.

Korea's national income now stands at only ten thousand dollars. In the future, we must raise that figure to about thirty thousand. Korea and its people cannot remain despondent amid today's temporary difficulties. We must tighten our belts and advance into international markets once more. Here, it is worth recalling Chung's words again.

“There will be trials, but I will never fail!”

Yes! We are being tested now; we have not failed. Before the test becomes failure, we must rise again and launch a new advance into world markets!
