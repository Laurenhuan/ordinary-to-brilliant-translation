# Han River Bridge and the Tokyo Initiative

Although Lee Byung-chull drew inspiration for most of the industries he established from Japan and received extensive technical support from Japanese companies,

in semiconductors, for example, he surpassed many Japanese companies and demonstrated his creativity.

One of the strengths of Japanese companies was likewise that their automobile, shipbuilding, electronics, and semiconductor industries learned technology from advanced Western countries and ultimately surpassed them.

The same was true of Lee. Although he obtained technology from Japan, he ultimately surpassed Japan in fields such as semiconductors. His creativity must therefore be acknowledged.

After suffering enormous losses on the Goryeong Bridge project, Chung Ju-yung continually searched for an opportunity to rise again.

Opportunities always come to those who are prepared. When the Korean government announced bidding for the reconstruction of Han River Bridge, Chung's opportunity finally arrived.

Han River Bridge was Korea's largest postwar project.

The original bridge had been destroyed during the Korean War. Reconstruction was delayed for seven years because the project was so vast and the budget so large. After struggling for years to raise the funds, the government finally invited public bids.

The project had originally been intended for either Chohung Construction or Heunghwa Engineering Office. But a problem arose.

The minister of home affairs wanted to award it to Chohung Construction, while the Ministry of Finance, which held the contracting authority, favored Heunghwa Engineering Office. Neither company would yield. Their confrontation continued for a year beyond the budget schedule without a result, and when compromise proved impossible, the government resorted to competitive bidding.

The major companies submitted their estimates, and an unexpected development occurred.

Heunghwa Engineering Office, then one of the largest construction companies, bid only 1,000 won. That amount was one quarter of the taxi fare from downtown Seoul to the Han River, effectively offering to perform the work for nothing. Because the lowest bid would ordinarily win, the other companies could only smile bitterly and withdraw.

The incident caused a major public reaction. The minister of home affairs, who received the bids, then announced that Heunghwa's 1,000-won estimate showed no sincere intent to compete and could not be accepted even as a donation. It was not a normal commercial act.

The government consequently decided to award the project to the company with the second-lowest bid: Chung Ju-yung's Hyundai Construction, which proposed 230 million won.

This occurred in September 1957.

The struggle between Heunghwa Engineering Office and Chohung Construction ultimately allowed Chung to benefit from their dispute. It was another opportunity for him.

Chung did not intend to repeat the nightmare of Goryeong Bridge. Equipment would be crucial to completing Han River Bridge successfully, so he established a heavy-machinery shop inside the Hyundai automobile repair facility he operated.

Whenever new models became available, the U.S. Eighth Army disposed of its old equipment at low prices.

Hyundai Construction had earned the trust of U.S. forces through the UN cemetery lawn project using barley, so Chung was able to buy their surplus equipment cheaply.

He purchased heavy machinery they no longer used, bought the necessary parts on the market, and refurbished it at Hyundai's heavy-machinery shop.

Sometimes parts were simply collected and assembled into new machines. All this was the work of Chung's brother-in-law Kim Yeong-ju, the “doctor of machinery.”

Hyundai completed the project in only one year. After Goryeong Bridge had taken too long and left the company heavily indebted, this project was finished in the shortest possible time.

Han River Bridge was a project of immense scale, and the final profit reached 40 percent of the contract price.

The project carried Hyundai Construction into the ranks of Korea's five largest construction companies and put it on a powerful upward trajectory. Many new contracts flowed to the company.

In 1958, it undertook work at Osan Air Base. In 1959, it won the project for the U.S. military's No. 1 dock at Incheon Port, described as the largest project since the founding of the Republic of Korea.

By 1960, Hyundai Construction had become the leader among more than one thousand domestic construction companies. Five years after the nightmare of Goryeong Bridge, Hyundai had secured its position in the industry.

In December 1959, Korea produced its first domestically made radio. The first company to manufacture one was GoldStar.

Lee Byung-chull was also busily traveling at the time.

He had just landed at Haneda Airport in Japan when he heard that Korea had produced a radio.

Returning from the United States, Lee had stopped in Tokyo to change planes. He intended to catch a flight back to Seoul, but heavy snow there made landing impossible. With no alternative, he remained in Tokyo for several days, staying at the Imperial Hotel opposite the Imperial Palace.

Located in central Tokyo, the Imperial Hotel ranked then, as it does now, among the city's finest hotels alongside Hotel Okura Tokyo and Hotel New Otani Tokyo.

Lee had studied at Waseda University when he was young and conducted extensive trade with Japan, so he knew the country well.

In 1960, Japan was striving with tremendous energy to join the ranks of the developed nations.

After the Korean War broke out in 1950, Japan earned 6.2 billion dollars by selling supplies to the United Nations.[^TN-CH10-001] This ended the economic distress that had followed its 1945 defeat and produced its best economic conditions in history, the so-called “Jimmu Boom.” It was followed by a succession of prosperous periods, including the “Takamagahara Boom” and the “Iwato Boom.”

Electric washing machines, refrigerators, and black-and-white televisions were subsequently produced, and these advanced household appliances began entering people's homes.

The Japanese called this “the season of flowers after a harsh winter,” and people throughout the country were filled with hope and anticipation for the future.

Against this social background, a Japanese television station prepared a New Year's program for 1960, inviting leading experts to discuss and forecast political, economic, military, and social developments around the world.

The military discussion centered on whether nuclear weapons would be used again. Economically, the focus was on U.S.-Soviet rivalry, especially whether the Soviet economy would overtake that of the United States.

At the time, the U.S. economy was growing at 3 percent a year while the Soviet economy expanded at 7 percent, and many people wondered when Soviet competitiveness would surpass America's.

What most interested Lee in these discussions was the stated intention of ten developed countries—including the United States, Britain, West Germany, France, Italy, Canada, and Australia—to provide long-term, low-interest loans to less-developed countries with growth potential.

Lee was eager to establish a fertilizer plant but had always lacked the funds, so this news was extremely important to him. It later became a driving force behind his successful creation of such a plant.

At the beginning of every year, television stations and newspapers publish economic forecasts and information.

Lee later began visiting Tokyo at the start of each year. From this practice emerged the famous “Tokyo Initiative.”

Samsung Electronics Chairman Kang Jin-ku described the Tokyo Initiative as follows:

1. First, Lee paid particular attention to special programs planned and produced by Japan's major broadcasters, especially those in which prominent Japanese scholars and journalists reviewed the previous year's economic trends and offered forecasts for the new year. He watched them carefully.

2. He then invited respected or particularly insightful economic journalists from leading Japanese publications to lunch or dinner and spoke with them.

Rather than gathering many people at once, he met them individually. He asked for their views on the previous year's economy, their forecasts for the new year, and which industries had strong prospects. He also asked about the essentials of developing an enterprise.

Journalists did not look only at surface figures. They also understood underlying realities and could explain their causes clearly.

3. After learning about the direction of Japan's economic development from journalists, Lee selected industries that interested him and arranged meetings chiefly with university professors and prominent scholars. He did not seek pure theorists, but scholars with insight into economic trends. He likewise asked for their views on industries that had emerged during the previous year and their outlook for the next.

He questioned them in detail about how development could occur and what would drive it.

Because the scholars knew these subjects thoroughly, they could provide clear answers.

4. He then invited prominent entrepreneurs from the business community.

Lee had a wide circle of friends in business and knew many entrepreneurs. When he met them, he asked each one which industries had performed well over the past year and why they had done so.

The entrepreneurs each had their own detailed insights, providing extremely valuable points of reference.

5. After gathering information from different sectors of Japanese society, Lee informed the chairman's secretariat of his ideas for new industries or management systems that Samsung might introduce, so that they could be developed into concrete plans.

When launching a new project in particular, he personally directed and supervised every stage, from the initial preparations through implementation.

Checking and checking again was characteristic of Lee's way of working.

As his return approached, he always visited bookstores, selected useful reference works, and bought them in stacks to take back to Korea.

6. As soon as he returned, Lee organized the projects he had personally identified as promising into a table and gave it to the chairman's secretariat, instructing its staff to investigate whether they could be developed under Korean conditions.

Through this careful and complex process, Lee conceived the fields Samsung might develop. He ultimately selected insurance, papermaking, synthetic fibers, mass media, electronics, heavy industry, and petrochemicals.

In other words, the initial concepts for Samsung's main industries all germinated in Tokyo.

Japanese companies themselves had localized business models from the United States, Britain, Germany, France, and other countries.

Japan's major companies today, such as Mitsui and Mitsubishi, developed around the time of the Meiji Restoration in 1868. They combined the strengths of companies in advanced Western countries with Japan's team-oriented approach to management. Lee understood this well.

He had his own secret when putting this knowledge into practice. An industry that achieved great success in the United States would not necessarily succeed in Korea. But if a successful American industry also succeeded in Japan, Lee believed it had a strong chance of taking root and growing in Korea. Why? Korea and Japan were similar in several respects, including food habits and patterns of daily life. An industry successful in Japan was therefore more likely to succeed in Korea. In this way, these emerging industries achieved enormous success together.

This might lead people to say that Lee succeeded in Korea by imitating Japanese compa-

nies, but that is not what actually happened.

Lee Byung-chull's Tokyo Initiative remains a Samsung Group tradition today.
