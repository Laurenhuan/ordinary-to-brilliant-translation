# The Revolt of the Princes and the Successors

Thought becomes action, action develops into habit, and habit develops into character. Character will determine a person's destiny.

—Chung Ju-yung

During his lifetime, Chung Ju-yung was a highly successful entrepreneur. The son of a poor farming family without even a patch of land on which to stand, he became an entrepreneur who owned more than eighty-three companies with sales exceeding 130 trillion won—an achievement that can only be called an extraordinary success. Yet like Lee Byung-chull, he failed to resolve the problems involving his children entirely satisfactorily.

His eldest son, Chung Mong-pil, who had served as president of Incheon Steel Group, died in a traffic accident. As Chung Ju-yung's health deteriorated, his second son, Chairman Chung Mong-koo, and his fifth, Chairman Chung Mong-hun, began a fierce struggle over succession to the group.

During this process, Chung Ju-yung's younger brother Chung In-yung and In-yung's eldest son, Chung Mong-gyu, who had effectively managed Hyundai Motor, agreed to relinquish management control. At the same time, a plan was developed to divide Hyundai into several groups. Hyundai Motor Group, including Kia Motors, would go to Chairman Chung Mong-koo. Hyundai Engineering & Construction, Hyundai Corporation, Hyundai Securities, and other companies centered on Hyundai Electronics would go to Chairman Chung Mong-hun. Hyundai Heavy Industries and two other companies would be entrusted to adviser Chung Mong-joon.

Chairman Chung Ju-yung also waged a fierce internal struggle at this time. For a period, funding shortages at Hyundai Investment Trust exposed liquidity problems at the parent company, Hyundai Engineering & Construction, plunging the entire group into crisis. Chung played the card of having the father and three sons—Mong-koo and Mong-joon, as the source lists them—withdraw simultaneously from front-line management. In the end, however, Hyundai was divided around the three brothers Mong-koo, Mong-hun, and Mong-joon.[^TN-CH26-001]

Hyundai Motor Group, led by Chung Mong-koo in the role of eldest son, ranked fifth in the Korean business world. In addition to its three pillar companies—Hyundai Motor, Kia Motors, and Hyundai Mobis—it owned eleven companies including Incheon Steel, Hyundai Hysco, Sammi Specialty Steel, Hyundai Capital, and Hyundai Aerospace. The twenty-three companies large and small under Chung Mong-hun, including Hyundai Electronics, or Hyundai Hynix, and Hyundai Securities, ranked second nationwide in the Financial Supervisory Service's 2000 performance table, with sales of 61.8974 trillion won.

Hyundai Heavy Industries Group, led by Chung Mong-joon, also recorded sales of 6.6 trillion won last year, including net profits exceeding 15.1 billion won.

In addition, Chung Ju-yung's sixth son, Chung Mong-yoon, served as chairman of Hyundai Credit Finance,

while his younger brother Chung Mong-il served as the company's president.

After Samsung Chairman Lee Byung-chull died in 1987, his eldest son, Lee Maeng-hee, made the largest conglomerate independent; his second son, Lee Chang-hee, founded Saehan Electrics; and his daughter Lee In-hee established Hansol Group. Hyundai underwent a similar process.

In the Financial Supervisory Service's 2000 financial tables, however, Chairman Lee Kun-hee's Samsung still ranked firmly in first place. This demonstrates the excellence of Lee Kun-hee's management ability.

Only a few months have passed since Hyundai Chairman Chung Ju-yung's death. As time goes by, the true shape of Hyundai Group will gradually emerge.

Although Chairman Chung Mong-hun, the fifth son, appeared on the surface to be Hyundai's legitimate successor, he no longer controlled Hyundai Engineering & Construction, the group's original enterprise. Hyundai Hynix also faced severe difficulties, and its ultimate fate remained a focus of public attention.

By contrast, Hyundai Motor Group under Chairman Chung Mong-koo remained relatively stable.

Chairman Lee Kun-hee took over Samsung's management from his father at the end of 1987, fourteen years ago. The transformation of Samsung's management approach can be said to have begun with the Frankfurt Declaration of 1993. The so-called New Management initiative was Lee Kun-hee's creation. He sounded the alarm with the striking words, “Change everything except your wife and children.”

“The sense of crisis created by Samsung's present condition and the changes at the end of the century often sent chills down my spine. . . . If we continued this way, Samsung would not merely lose one or two companies; it might face bankruptcy. In this state of mind, especially from the summer through the winter of 1992, my weight dropped by more than ten kilograms.” Such was Chairman Lee Kun-hee's state of mind when he proclaimed New Management.

“Change begins with me” captured the reality of Samsung's 1993 decision to implement New Management amid a host of crises. The transformation revealed an entirely new Samsung.

Through three rounds of restructuring, Samsung completed the reorganization of its smaller affiliates and introduced

new measures including an early-start, early-finish work schedule, from seven in the morning to four in the afternoon; consumer cultural centers; reform of personnel and education systems; the creation of social-service organizations; freedom from uniforms for female employees; support for small and medium-sized enterprises; and integrated overseas production bases in Britain, Mexico, and elsewhere. The seven-to-four schedule in particular brought a breath of fresh air to Korean corporate culture and gave other companies a new stimulus. Samsung's practice of “standing in the consumer's position” when conflicts arose also won broad consumer approval. Samsung today is pursuing “quality management.”

At the Los Angeles strategy conference in February 1995, Lee Byung-chull announced that he would “take responsibility only for semiconductors, automobiles, and social service, and leave everything else entirely to professionals.”[^TN-CH26-002] Thereafter, the space for the chairman's approval disappeared from Samsung documents.

Although Chairman Lee Kun-hee led Samsung Group, with annual sales as high as 103 trillion won, in practice he made relatively few approvals. Each company's president decided its management strategy independently. This was the management philosophy that allowed Samsung to operate successfully. It would be wrong, however, to conclude that Lee Kun-hee merely marked time. Decisions essential to the group's survival, such as semiconductor investments, naturally remained his responsibility.

Because few specific matters required his approval, Lee did not often go to his office on the twenty-eighth floor of Samsung headquarters. He worked primarily from his private office at Seungjiwon.

Chairman Lee Kun-hee had many areas of interest: golf, automobiles, video, flowers, food, films, television dramas, economic documentaries, Jindo dogs, reading, and more. For this reason, he had a private secretary for each field of management.

He first investigated and analyzed necessary matters extensively, then reflected and developed his ideas. At any time, he might summon the chairmen and presidents of affiliated companies to Seungjiwon, his private workplace in Itaewon-dong, for a discussion. Sometimes they talked all day; at other times, until dawn. In short, these meetings could occur at any moment. Their principal subjects were the direction of corporate management in the twenty-first century, the role of large enterprises, trends in cutting-edge technology, and related issues, but they were not limited to these. Lee also discussed a wide variety of detailed matters, showing himself to be meticulous yet approachable. The discussion naturally took place in a relaxed atmosphere without neckties, where everyone spoke freely and without inhibition. Because Lee himself never drank, however, no alcohol was served.

His work now centered on three tasks:

first, producing first-rate products; second, using them to build Samsung into a truly world-class enterprise; and third, conducting strategic research in order to survive in an era of constant change. Toward these goals, he studied intently and thought comprehensively. The material for this research and reflection might come from watching a film, viewing an economic documentary, or reading a book. It might come from dismantling automobile components or registering the Jindo dog as a protected animal. It might also involve copying valuable video materials and distributing them to employees throughout the company—for example, a videotape on raising a goldfish worth thirty million won, or a DVD about a bonsai worth more than one hundred million won. Lee also distributed Japanese writer Ryohei Kuri's children's story *One Bowl of Noodles* to every employee. These were not merely private hobbies. They were a means by which Samsung Group kept its employees alert, while providing fertile ground for a corporate culture devoted to “producing first-rate products.”

He watched and collected more than fifteen thousand videotapes. Sometimes he sent one to a company president, sometimes to employees at the director or section-chief level, and sometimes to staff members. He persisted in doing so as a kind of inoculation that kept employees vigilant.

He also took golf beyond the realm of personal interest and incorporated it into his investment plans. Pak Se-ri, who later became one of the world's leading female golfers, was sponsored by Samsung. Once she became famous, Samsung's brand received the greatest return from a minimal investment.

Chairman Lee Kun-hee's hobbies were horseback riding and golf. Almost every week, he rode for three or four hours in a single day at Anyang Golf Course. About once every two months, he went out on the course to play golf, but ordinarily he hit balls at the driving range.

His approach to health was inherited from his late father, Chairman Lee Byung-chull. Although he did not drink alcohol, he smoked three to four packs of cigarettes a day.
