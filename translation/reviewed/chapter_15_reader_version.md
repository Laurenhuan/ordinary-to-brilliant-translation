# Chung Ju-yung's Ships and Lee Byung-chull's Color Television[^TN-CH15-001]

Skilled workers, however, posed a serious problem. Having previously built only ships of twelve thousand tons, they now had to construct a

shipyard more than ten times that scale without the slightest experience.

Many people scoffed: how could they build ships when they did not even have a shipyard? They were mocking Chung Ju-yung for overestimating himself.

Chung's first task was to steady his people's nerves. He addressed his employees: “I will take responsibility for everything. Anyone who is afraid can go home and lie down.”

On August 24, 1999, Chung met Stavros Niarchos, chairman of the Greek shipping company Niarchos Group, at Hyundai Group's guesthouse in Seongbuk-dong after twenty-seven years apart. They enjoyed a cordial conversation. Niarchos, brother-in-law of the renowned Greek shipping magnate Aristotle Onassis,[^FB-F029] was himself a giant of Greek shipping and the first customer to order ships from Hyundai Shipbuilding. In 1972, he had contracted for two 260,000-ton oil tankers on the strength of nothing more than a photograph of a beach in Ulsan that Chung carried and plans for an oil tanker borrowed from someone else.

Niarchos had come to Korea to attend the naming ceremony the next day for Hyundai Heavy Industries' new oil tanker, the *Christina*. To thank the benefactor who had helped him thirty years earlier, Chairman Chung hosted a banquet for Niarchos, and they reminisced about the original ship order.

In September 1971, Chung decided to establish Korea's first shipyard. Hyundai Construction employed many specialists in machinery and electrical engineering, and he believed it would be safer to have them build ships worth hundreds of millions of dollars at home than to send them abroad as contract labor to earn foreign currency.

As a construction expert, Chung believed shipbuilding could not differ greatly from building construction. To him, it meant shaping steel plates into a hull and then installing equipment inside to propel the vessel.

Turning decisions into action was one of Chung's strengths. He began the preparations.

Building the shipyard would require approximately 80 million dollars. That may not seem an enormous sum today, but at the time it was astronomical. Korea's dollar market was in poor condition, and there was nowhere to borrow the money. Nor did Chung's proposed shipbuilding business arouse anyone's interest.

Chung planned to borrow from Mitsubishi Corporation in Japan. With Japan and China then approaching the establishment of diplomatic relations, Mitsubishi declined his request.

Never retreating once he had made up his mind, Chung flew to London in search of financing. London, New York, and Frankfurt were the places where the greatest concentrations of money in the world

were gathered.

All Chung brought with him was a photograph of Mipo Harbor, where he planned to build the shipyard: a desolate beach with only a few pine trees. In London, a financial center, attracting capital with a single photograph was impossible. He hurried from place to place seeking a loan.

Careful observation convinced him that Britain's major banks, which commanded global finance, followed Barclays Bank's lead. Barclays influenced London's bankers, and persuading it was therefore crucial.

Banks do not lend money lightly. Securing a loan required a project proposal and recommendation that met British standards. Chung commissioned the London firm A&P Appledore to prepare them, for a feasibility study and recommendation were the keys that could open the door to financing. A&P Appledore was itself doubtful about this obscure company from the Far East. Ultimately, its chairman, Lombardon, met Chung, and his distrust of the company from a developing country gradually faded.

“It will be difficult to apply for a loan if you do not yet have a buyer prepared to place an order. Barclays has serious doubts that Korea can actually build ships,” Chairman Lombardon said.

Chung pulled a 500-won banknote from his pocket.

“A turtle ship is printed here. It proves that we Koreans built an ironclad vessel in the sixteenth century. Britain's shipbuilding industry is generally said to have begun in the nineteenth century, so Korea was more than three hundred years ahead. This shows that Korea possesses great ability in shipbuilding. Our policy of national isolation merely slowed industrialization; in fact, the Korean people are diligent, resourceful, and full of potential for development.”

Moved by Chung's words, Lombardon arranged a meeting with a vice chairman of Barclays Bank.

They agreed to meet over a meal, leaving the question of the loan until last.

As soon as he sat down at the table, the Barclays vice chairman asked Chung, “What is your field of specialization?”

The question came without warning. Chung had attended only elementary school, yet he was being asked about his academic specialty.

“Vice Chairman, have you read the proposal we submitted?”

“Of course. I read it very carefully. It is extremely comprehensive and complete.”

“That is my specialty. In fact, I went to Oxford University yesterday and submitted this report as my application for a doctorate. After reading it, the professors awarded me a doctorate in economics on the spot. That proposal is my doctoral dissertation.”

Such was Chung Ju-yung's disarming humor.

Rather than explain that he had only completed elementary school, he demonstrated the breadth of his management experience and asked the other man to recognize it. Such was his confidence.

The vice chairman nodded. “It seems to me that your specialty is humor. Our bank will submit your proposal to the Export Credits Guarantee Department. Good luck.”

He had won this battle.

The story is now widely told, but according to the recollections of former Hyundai Group president Lee Myung-bak, it was only a small episode. A great deal of work had already gone into raising the funds and producing a detailed proposal, which ultimately won the bank's approval.

While the bank carefully read and examined the proposal, it also conducted a thorough investigation of Hyundai Group. The inquiry found Hyundai to be a group with enormous potential for development. With a leader as resourceful and courageous as Chung Ju-yung, it concluded that the company deserved the loan.

One final hurdle remained: approval from the loan-guarantee authority.

In Britain, a domestic bank could lend to a foreign enterprise only with a guarantee from the Export Credits Guarantee Department, or ECGD. To obtain that guarantee, the applicant had to present a ship-sale contract.

“Your shipyard proposal is excellent. But one question remains. If I wanted to order a ship, I would naturally go to a world-renowned shipyard, not to an inexperienced Korean yard. Without orders, to whom will you sell the ships you build? If they cannot be sold, how will you repay the money Britain lends you? Bring us proof that you can sell the ships. Until then, we cannot grant the loan.”

The head of Britain's Export Credits Guarantee Department had made a compelling point.

“Please do not worry. I need only bring you a purchase contract, do I not?” Chung declared boldly before leaving. In truth, the man's argument was so reasonable that Chung had been persuaded.

Where could he obtain an order?

The shipyard existed only on paper and had never produced a vessel. Who would sign a purchase contract with him? At one point, Chung even considered abandoning the shipyard project.

Then he gathered his thoughts. He had already arranged to borrow from Barclays and could not break his word. He had also promised President Park Chung Hee that he would establish the shipyard. This was a national undertaking and could not be abandoned. Hardship had been expected from the outset; whether success seemed possible or not, he had to persist to the end.

Carrying a photograph of several pine trees on a beach, Chung approached a succession of world-class shipping companies. Their owners thought him mad, which was an understandable reaction.

Then one man became interested in Chung's passion: Greek shipping magnate Stavros Niarchos. He offered to order two 260,000-ton vessels.

British-educated Niarchos was then thirty-six and in the prime of life. Having taken over the shipping business after his father's death, he was at the height of his momentum.

“It may be nothing but a beach now, but I will turn it into a successful shipyard and provide vessels at lower prices than other yards.”

Niarchos smiled at Chung's words. “Perhaps I am gambling, too. But your passion has moved me, and I have decided to sign the contract.”

Niarchos ordered two 260,000-ton vessels at a price of 30.35 million dollars each, to be delivered five years later. Chung received a deposit of 1.4 billion won.

The terms Chung promised Niarchos were also substantial. He

pledged to build high-quality ships and, if he failed to fulfill the contract, to return the deposit with interest.

Until then, the largest ship built in Korea had been only 17,000 tons. Now two 260,000-ton giants were to be produced at a Hyundai shipyard that had yet to be built.

Chung sent the order to the Export Credits Guarantee Department in London and thereby secured the funds needed to build the shipyard.

With financing in place, construction could begin. Skilled workers, however, posed a serious problem. Having previously built only ships of twelve thousand tons, they now had to construct a shipyard more than ten times that scale without the slightest experience.

Many people scoffed: how could they build ships when they did not even have a shipyard? They were mocking Chung for overestimating himself.

Chung's first task was to steady his people's nerves. He addressed his employees: “I will take responsibility for everything. Anyone who is afraid can go home and lie down.”

That was the essential spirit; nothing more needed to be said.

When the commander had placed himself at the front, what more was there to discuss? Construction of the Ulsan shipyard began. They learned while working, studied what they did not know, and stayed up through the night when time ran short. In this way, the shipyard formally began to take shape.

Everything began from zero. To deliver two 260,000-ton vessels by the date promised to Niarchos, they had to finish the yard quickly while leaving enough time to build the ships. Funds for the shipyard were borrowed through Barclays Bank from four countries, including France, West Germany, and Spain. The financing arrived; the docks and quay walls were completed; factory buildings covering 140,000 pyeong were erected; and housing for five thousand people had to be constructed. Everything proceeded at a frantic pace.

After one year and three months, the shipyard was finally completed on March 22, 1972.[^TN-CH15-002] Occupying six hundred thousand pyeong, it had annual capacity of seven hundred thousand tons and two docks in the seven-hundred-thousand-ton class. In the shortest possible time, Chung had built the world's largest shipyard.

While building the yard, Hyundai also studied how to construct the two 260,000-ton vessels. Now that the shipyard was complete, all that remained was to build the ships Niarchos had ordered. Yet Chung did not stop there. In its first year after completion, Hyundai Shipbuilding received orders totaling 518,000 tons and immediately captured 1.75 percent of the world shipbuilding market. Chung's business acumen was remarkable.

Conditions improved further in 1973, the second year. Orders in the global shipbuilding industry increased two and a half times over the previous year. It was a year of abundance for shipbuilders.

Maritime transport naturally expanded sharply as well. The world's major shipping companies mainly ordered oil tankers, the most profitable segment. Hyundai Shipbuilding accordingly moved toward tanker construction and received orders for twelve very large crude carriers, or VLCCs.

In 1973, it completed 1.947 million tons and increased its share of the world market to 2.64 percent. This was attributed to the oil crisis, which had relatively lowered prices in the principal shipbuilding countries.

In 1974, Hyundai next received orders for two 260,000-ton VLCCs as well as smaller multipurpose cargo ships, raising its market share to 3 percent. Six of the orders came from Japan, accounting for more than half the total.

Having won so many orders from Japan, then the world's largest shipbuilding nation, Chung saw even greater prospects and began expanding the yard. His premise was that only mass production could maximize competitiveness. Hyundai Shipbuilding formally began its expansion in 1973.

The new third dock was ninety-two meters wide, designed in preparation for even larger vessels. The shipyard's factory buildings expanded from an initial thirty or forty thousand pyeong to eighty-eight thousand pyeong, roughly doubling in size. Steel-processing capacity rose from two hundred thousand to five hundred thousand tons. This was more than an expansion; it amounted to building another shipyard. The government had just declared its ambition to make Korea a heavy-industry power, providing substantial support for shipbuilding.

Just as Hyundai Shipbuilding was expanding at full speed, the first oil crisis struck and the world economy suddenly stalled. Trade between countries plummeted, plunging the shipping industry into crisis. In 1974, global shipbuilding faced overcapacity. By the end of 1973, tankers, which accounted for 40 percent of world shipping tonnage,

were already in surplus. After the oil crisis, the world's major energy-importing countries introduced measures to curb petroleum consumption, sharply reducing the volume of oil transported. The global shipbuilding industry, centered on tankers, consequently faced severe difficulties. Hyundai Shipbuilding, which had devoted itself to tanker production, suffered enormous losses. It possessed experience only in building tankers and had no other path available. The company was beset by crisis.

As the world economy faltered, many customers refused delivery of ships already under contract. The principal cases were vessel 7302, a 260,000-ton tanker ordered by Niarchos of Greece, and vessels 7308 and 7310, ordered by Ireland Shipping Company of Hong Kong.

Vessel 7302, ordered by Niarchos, was launched and named in June 1974 and fully completed that November. Because of the oil crisis, however, Niarchos decided not to accept delivery: if he bought it, no customer would charter it.

Niarchos began pointing out problems here and there to delay completion. If the vessel could not be delivered by the contract date, he could cancel the agreement and recover his deposit with interest.

Every time he identified a defect, Hyundai's workers labored through the night to satisfy his demand. Just as delivery seemed imminent with no problems remaining, he pointed out another deficiency that could not possibly be corrected before the deadline.

After confirming that this was Niarchos's final demand, Chung began the correction. The ship was completed one day past the contract date, and Niarchos refused delivery on the grounds that the deadline had been exceeded. Hyundai appealed to an international arbitration commission. Refusing a 260,000-ton vessel over “a delay of only one day” seemed absurd to anyone who heard it.

The completed ship Niarchos had ordered remained moored at the quay. Oh Won-chul, the Blue House's second economic secretary, believed that leaving a finished vessel idle represented a national loss, so he put it to use carrying oil from the Gulf region. Hyundai then won its case against Niarchos. Thirty years later, Chung invited him to Korea. The two men met again at Hyundai Group's guesthouse. Niarchos, once a thirty-six-year-old young man, was now approaching seventy, while Chung was over eighty. They talked for a long time.

Most people would never resume contact after relations had been broken off, yet Chung was able to invite Niarchos back to Korea. He was a giant not only in business but also in human relationships.
