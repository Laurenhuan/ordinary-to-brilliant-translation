# Building Factories and Exporting Abroad

While American and European businesspeople were visiting Korea to explore investment opportunities, Chung Ju-yung turned his gaze overseas and planned an even greater undertaking.

Beginning with the Han River Bridge project in the mid-1950s, Hyundai Construction grew steadily and, by the 1960s, had become the leader of Korea's construction industry.

Taking on new fields was one of Chung Ju-yung's particular strengths.

“For resource-poor Korea to survive, its only path is exports,” Chung said.

“Looking at the situation now, it seems that, no matter how I consider it, I was wrong to launch a military revolution. Our country is like a thatched hut that has been looted. Fifteen years have passed since liberation, yet why are things still this way? It is because our politicians are corrupt to the core, because they are consumed by self-interest. I do not possess much learning, so I have asked those said to be our country's famous economists how we can make the nation prosperous and strong. They say that ours is fundamentally an agricultural country, with no resources, no technology—nothing. We lack the foundations for economic development. Does our country truly have no hope now?”

After the May 16 military coup, Park Chung Hee spoke these words to Chief Secretary Lee Dong-won, who was then exercising presidential authority.

Although the military government formulated a five-year economic development plan, reduced farmers' high-interest debts to alleviate poverty, and carried out currency reform to rebuild the economic system, the poor economic situation unsettled public sentiment.

The currency reform intended to raise funds ultimately failed to do so and instead aggravated inflation. When President Park Chung Hee announced an “extension of military rule,” the United States government threatened to “suspend aid.” If this continued, the Korean economy was certain to face a grave crisis.

As President Park put it, Korea was struggling even to feed and clothe its people. At the time, Korea's annual per capita income was only 78 dollars.

A revolution had been necessary to enable the people to live well, but even after its success, no path forward was yet in sight.

As Park himself said, he consulted numerous renowned economists and officials, but none of their answers satisfied him. The economy continued to deteriorate.

Before the revolution, GNP grew by 6.1 percent in 1959; the growth rate fell by 2.3 percent in 1960 and by another 2.8 percent in 1961.

Lee Byung-chull and other business leaders, whom the May 16 military government had arrested on suspicion of “illicit profiteering” and later released, founded the Korean Businessmen's Association, the predecessor of the Federation of Korean Industries.

At a meeting held at the Bando Hotel on August 16 that year, business leaders including Choi Tae-seop, president of Korea Glass; Kim Yong-wan, president of Kyungbang[^FB-F020]; Jeon Taek-bu, president of Cheonil-sa; Lee Han-won, president of Daehan Flour Mills; Shim Sang-jun, president of Jedong Shipping; and Kim Sang-mok, president of Pyeongan Textiles unanimously selected Lee Byung-chull, chairman of Samsung Trading, as the association's new chairman.

At a time when the military government was at a loss over Korea's economy, Lee Byung-chull, the newly elected chairman of the Korean Businessmen's Association, resolved to find a direction and a breakthrough for the country's economic development.

At the time, two people proposed paths to national prosperity. One was President Jeon Taek-bu, and the other was Chairman Lee Byung-chull.

Jeon argued that exports were Korea's only way forward, while Lee believed that industrialization was the country's only path. Their views converged on exporting abroad through industrialization.

Under the title “A Path to National Prosperity,” Lee Byung-chull published a five-part series in the *Hankook Ilbo* setting out his vision for economic development.

I believe per capita income must reach 178 dollars. That would be comparable to levels in countries such as the Philippines and Turkey and higher than the Korean government's target.

If Korea could successfully attract 2.1 to 2.3 billion dollars in foreign capital over the next ten to twenty years, the factories built with it would begin generating returns in only three years. If the annual return were 200 million dollars and that money were invested in private enterprises, the country could secure 1.5 to 2 billion dollars in factory-construction funds within ten years.

An investment of 4 billion dollars could build one thousand factories, each capitalized at 4 million dollars. If investment grew at 70 percent of the annual total—that is, by 2.8 billion dollars—per capita income could reach 100 dollars.

If each such factory employed five hundred workers, they would create five hundred thousand jobs and support 2.5 million people. Including related industries and distribution, they could be expected to provide employment for five million people. This would draw rural people into factory work—in other words, it would absorb one-third of the fifteen million farmers into industry. The cultivated area per person could then

rise from the existing 420 pyeong to 630 pyeong, while industrialization would also make fertilizer and farm implements available at lower prices.

As factories paid more in taxes, civil servants' incomes could multiply, thereby eliminating corruption and making society harmonious and healthy.

The core of Lee Byung-chull's thinking was to attract foreign capital and build factories. He envisioned establishing one thousand factories capitalized at 4 million dollars each, creating five million jobs, and raising the country's per capita national income from just 78 dollars to 178 dollars within ten years.

After the articles appeared, Lee Byung-chull and Namgung Ryeon, president of Far East Shipping, went to see President Park Chung Hee almost every day to persuade him.

They stressed, “Industrialization is the only way to escape poverty. If we miss this opportunity and delay economic development, we will never emerge from the shadow of poverty and disorder. Korea has no time to follow the classical pattern of development that Britain followed before the Industrial Revolution. We must boldly reverse the sequence: industrialize first, then develop from large enterprises toward small and medium-sized businesses.”

Lee identified the key industries that should be developed first: cement, fertilizer, electrical goods, steelmaking, synthetic fibers, and oil refining. Park agreed.

Yet building a steel mill would require 130 million dollars, more than the Korean government possessed even if it exhausted all its foreign-exchange reserves. Park looked troubled.

Lee said the problem could be solved by attracting foreign capital; there was no other way. The military government accepted his plan. The question was how—and by whom—the enormous sum of 4 billion dollars needed to develop the key industries could be obtained.

A plan for attracting foreign capital was therefore drawn up:

1. Because it was extremely difficult for individual enterprises to attract capital, delegations should be sent to the United States, Japan, and Europe to seek foreign investment.

2. The government should be able to guarantee private-sector loans.

3. Commercial officers should be posted to overseas consulates to support private-sector economic diplomacy.

4. Private enterprises should be allowed to invite foreign technicians and businesspeople freely to discuss factory construction.

5. When factories were built with foreign capital, domestic financing should also be raised to the greatest extent possible, and

a comprehensive guarantee system should be established.

6. Enterprises relying on foreign capital to import machinery and equipment should be allowed to import the raw materials they required, and their profits should serve as operating funds.

7. Investment should be sought from foreign long-term financial institutions.

8. Laws and agreements such as the Foreign Capital Inducement Promotion Act, the Foreign Exchange Control Act, and a treaty for the avoidance of double taxation should be enacted.

Vice Chairman Park Chung Hee accepted these proposals and enacted them one by one. All that remained was to attract 4 billion dollars in foreign capital. The government assigned the task to an Americas investment delegation headed by Lee Byung-chull and a European delegation led by Lee Jeong-rim, an executive at Korea Cement. In November 1961, Lee Byung-chull departed for the United States, leading the Americas delegation to Washington.

General James Van Fleet, who had commanded the US Eighth Army during the Korean War, gave Lee considerable assistance. Although Van Fleet had already retired, his extensive connections in American business enabled him to introduce Lee to many leading figures in the economy.

Lee explained to them, “Korea will need 2.6 billion dollars over the next ten years. Of that, 1.3 billion must come from foreign capital, 1 billion from the World Bank or export-import banks, and the remaining 300 million from private investors overseas.”

The American businesspeople said that Korea must first create an environment conducive to investment. Their conditions were as follows:

1. To attract foreign capital and industrialize, Korea must first secure transportation and financing and establish “special industrial zones” along the coast or inland. In selecting these areas, it must carefully examine transportation, electricity, access, water resources, labor, factory sites, and other conditions so as to improve its international competitiveness across the board.

2. It must formulate a sound Foreign Capital Inducement Promotion Act and create favorable political, economic, and social conditions for investors.

3. It must clearly distinguish investment from technical cooperation and specify each project's details, terms, revenues, and expenditures.

At the time, American businesspeople approached developing countries with a chivalrous desire to help and a sense of mission.

Lee Byung-chull responded to their conditions with great humility. Korean

businesspeople had no experience attracting foreign capital, and this was their first venture abroad, so they needed to approach it as an opportunity to learn.

The central demand made by the American businesspeople was that Korea establish special industrial zones in coastal areas so that raw materials, machinery, and other goods could be transported by ship. They knew how weak Korea's transportation infrastructure was. Such zones would also need electricity, water, labor, and factory sites.

Lee promised that as soon as he returned home, he would select a suitable location based on the conditions the American business community had laid out. Korea had already been considering the construction of industrial zones for several years, and Lee was among those who had conceived the idea. He hoped to establish an industrial zone containing a world-scale fertilizer plant. Because he had long held this vision, he had also been searching for a suitable place. Ulsan interested him most.

Ulsan was not only a coastal city; the Taehwa River ran through its center, giving it abundant water resources. With the necessary power and labor, Ulsan would become an ideal industrial zone. The government could provide electricity, and unemployment meant that labor would be readily available.

The Korean businesspeople visiting the United States recommended Ulsan to their American counterparts. The Americans responded positively: “We will send a delegation to inspect the site.”

After gathering materials in the United States on potential investment and cooperation projects, Lee and the other business leaders returned home in early December. Meanwhile, the delegation to Europe visited companies in West Germany, including Krupp Steel and Siemens, to seek loans. The West German and American business communities did not impose especially harsh conditions, and matters proceeded smoothly.

Six months after the European delegation returned, 25 million dollars in capital attracted from West Germany was used to establish companies including GoldStar, Hanil Cement, and SsangYong Cement. Lee Byung-chull's theory of attracting foreign capital had proved successful.

On February 3, 1962, the government and business leaders held a groundbreaking ceremony for the “Ulsan Industrial Center.”

Five days after the groundbreaking ceremony, Lee Byung-chull, chairman of the Korean Businessmen's Association, and Vice Chairman Namgung Ryeon led a group of experts to the United States. Traveling around the country, they promoted Korea's Ulsan Industrial Center to American businesspeople and urgently pressed them to send an investment delegation to inspect Korea as soon as possible.

At last, three months later, on May 11, a twenty-eight-member “Delegation of Prominent American Businessmen,” headed by General James Van Fleet, former commander of United Nations forces in Korea, arrived at Gimpo Airport.

The delegation included representatives of world-class corporations such as General Motors, Ford, Westinghouse Electric, and Dow Chemical, as well as Texaco, Hanwha Industrial Bank, and American Standard.

That day, the American businesspeople paid courtesy calls on Chairman Park Chung Hee and Prime Minister Song Yo-chan, then attended a briefing at the Economic Planning Board on Korea's economic conditions, including the Five-Year Economic Development Plan. The next day, they traveled to Ulsan by military aircraft and special train.

They were accompanied by Kim Ip-sam, vice chairman of the Federation of Korean Industries. According to his memoirs, the Ulsan industrial zone at the time amounted to little more than flags planted in a desolate barley field. The members of the American delegation, who had arrived full of hope, were bitterly disappointed.

The members asked General Van Fleet, who had served as a commander during the Korean War, to explain Korea's circumstances. The portly general stepped forward. Pointing to the barley field, he told the American businesspeople that Korea was different from the United States: its territory was small, and not a single patch of land could be wasted.

Encouraged when the American guests nodded in understanding of Korea's circumstances, the official in charge began explaining Ulsan's advantages as an industrial base.

First, Ulsan was a strategic hub for both sea and land transportation. Pohang lay to the north and Busan to the south, while the surrounding stretch of the southern coast had the potential to develop into an industrial belt.

He went on to explain that numerous small and medium-sized cities surrounded Ulsan, so labor would not be a problem. As for water, the Taehwa and Imjin rivers were nearby,[^TN-CH12-001] and if necessary, water from the Nakdong River could be brought in by pipeline. The American businesspeople were more concerned with the port city's location and terrain. After personally inspecting the surrounding land, they were highly satisfied with Ulsan.

The American delegation left Korea on May 26, having spent the period before its departure consulting with Korean businesspeople about investment cooperation.

Ultimately, several American companies exchanged letters of intent with Korean enterprises. The day before the delegation departed, the Korean government, the Korean Businessmen's

Association, and the American business delegation signed a preliminary investment agreement at Prime Minister Song Yo-chan's residence.

Chairman Lee Byung-chull and delegation leader Van Fleet issued a joint statement emphasizing the need for economic exchange between the two countries.

As the Korean people watched the American investment delegation's activities, they began to believe that economic development could be achieved by attracting foreign capital.

Korean businesspeople also benefited greatly from the effort to attract investment: they learned a great deal and gained confidence.

It was after the May 16 military coup that Korea began to find the direction of its economic development.

Lee Byung-chull's advocacy of foreign investment provided an opening for Korea's economic development. His target of “178 dollars in per capita income” was achieved in 1969, three years earlier than the 1972 date he had envisioned.

While American and European businesspeople were visiting Korea to explore investment opportunities, Chung Ju-yung turned his gaze overseas and planned an even greater undertaking.

Beginning with the Han River Bridge project in the mid-1950s, Hyundai Construction grew steadily and, by the 1960s, had become the leader of Korea's construction industry.

Taking on new fields was one of Chung Ju-yung's particular strengths.

“For resource-poor Korea to survive, its only path is exports,” Chung said.

In 1965, Chung secured the Narathiwat Highway project in Thailand, defeating twenty-nine companies from sixteen developed countries. It was the first time a Korean construction company had ventured abroad. For a Korean construction industry with no experience building highways, this was a momentous undertaking. Working overseas also placed immense pressure on Hyundai Construction, and industry insiders at home criticized the project. Chung, however, found it enormously attractive. The contract was worth 5.22 million dollars—more than Hyundai Construction's total annual project value and more than 65 percent of the combined value of all domestic and overseas construction contracts awarded to Korean firms in 1965.

Faced with a project of such enormous scale, Chung devoted all his energy and effort to it. He dispatched his finest technical team, frequently sent his younger brother Chung In-yung to supervise the site, and flew to Thailand himself whenever he had time.

“We must honor the contract. No matter what difficulties arise, we must finish the project by the stipulated deadline. That is our obligation to the Thai government!”

Chung said this at the construction site.

Yet the project was extraordinarily difficult. To begin with, Thailand's weather was entirely different from Korea's. In Korea, most rain fell during the monsoon season; in Thailand, it could rain at any time. Sometimes a sudden storm swept through during the day, but more often, torrential rain fell without stopping. Work naturally had to cease during a downpour, not merely for a few hours but sometimes for four or five days. The project was inevitably delayed, while workers still had to be paid when it rained, driving costs beyond the budget.

The soil was also very different from Korea's. In Korea, digging into the ground would turn up either stones or bedrock.[^TN-CH12-002] In Thailand, no matter how deep they dug, they found only soil. Construction therefore had to begin by spreading crushed stone over the soft ground. Because the soil was loose and yielding, even the gravel would sink. In short, the materials required differed enormously from what had been budgeted based on Korean conditions.

Equipment posed another serious problem. Chung had brought conventional machinery, but conditions in Thailand were utterly different from those in Korea. The forests were tall and dense, and rain caused the rivers to overflow. Access roads laboriously laid to transport materials could vanish without a trace after a single night of heavy rain. The equipment brought from Korea was wholly inadequate for the project.

The local hired workers also presented difficulties; they were extremely indolent. Once the sun set, they would do no more work. In Korea, by contrast, workers would continue by torchlight to keep a project on schedule.

Compared with Thailand, Korea's climate, land, and human resources seemed exceptionally well suited to construction.

At the site, Chung had people build the vibratory rollers, cranes, mixers, and other equipment they needed, and he purchased additional machinery.

Yet even the latest equipment they purchased sometimes proved useless, because the locally hired workers did not know how to operate it and repeatedly broke the machinery.

A lack of technology and experience, a weak project-management system, and unforeseen

weather and soil conditions continually obstructed the project.

Some employees began arguing that the project should be abandoned. But Chung Ju-yung was Chung Ju-yung: he was determined to see it through. In the end, he completed the project despite suffering a massive loss.

Yet the Thai highway project did not leave him empty-handed. He had been the first in Korea to venture abroad to build a highway, and just one year later he successfully completed the Kyungbu Expressway project. He gained something else as well: an employee named Lee Myung-bak.

Lee Myung-bak had not trained in construction. At the Thai project site, he worked as an accountant. In 1966, Thai laborers rioted over pay. They smashed machinery and surged toward the site office. A Thai accountant shouted urgently to Lee, “Mr. Lee, run for your life!” But Lee could not abandon the safe and flee. Everyone else ran; he alone remained.

The rioters were startled to find him there alone. One of them brandished a knife and said,

“If you know what's good for you, hand over the key to the safe.”

Lee refused and stood his ground.

“Do you want to die?”

The rioters held their knives before Lee's face.

Lee backed up several steps until he was against the wall. He seemed certain to die. For an instant, he considered handing over the key. In fact, the safe held only a small amount of petty cash.

But as the accountant, it was his duty to protect the safe. He clasped it to his chest.

“Let go!”

The rioters seized him by the collar, but he never let go. Countless kicks struck his back. Fortunately, the police arrived promptly after receiving a report and rescued him.

News of the safe incident traveled to Hyundai Construction's Bangkok branch and its Seoul headquarters. It also reached Chung Ju-yung.

From that point on, Chung took notice of the twenty-six-year-old Lee, setting him on the path of a meteoric rise. Lee became a managing director at Hyundai Construction at twenty-nine, a senior managing director in 1974, and company president in 1975, when he was thirty-five.

He joined the company in 1965 and, in just ten years, rose from an entry-level employee to president.

News that Chung Ju-yung had suffered heavy losses on the Thai highway project reached the Blue House.

President Park Chung Hee was then contemplating the Kyungbu Expressway. He summoned Chung to the Blue House and said,

“President Chung, I hear you lost a great deal in Thailand this time?”

Chung replied,

“Rather than a loss, I would call it rather expensive tuition. The current project will be completed by next March. Another project will follow, and I will earn back the tuition I paid on that one.”

“You must recover in Thailand what you lost there!”

President Park said this from the standpoint of the national interest.

“Please do not worry. How could I go to Thailand for a project and return at a loss? I will be sure to come back having made money.”

Chung did not regard himself as having failed. Although the contract revenue he had expected had evaporated, the knowledge he gained was considerable: the areas urgently requiring improvement were experience and technology. Applying what he had learned, he successfully completed the Kyungbu Expressway project in Korea.
