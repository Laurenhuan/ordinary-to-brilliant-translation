# Two Men in the Face of Power[^TN-CH11-001]

Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung shared one trait: neither yielded to pressure of any kind, and both stood by their

positions. Lee would set out reason after reason to persuade the other party, while Chung was as stubborn as an ox and refused to give an inch.

At seven in the morning on May 16, 1961, Lee got into a car outside Tokyo's Imperial Hotel to go golfing.

Lee went to bed at ten every night and rose at six. At 6:40 he bathed and ate, and at eight he began work. He maintained this habit throughout his life.

That day, too, the incident occurred after he rose and set out.

His Japanese driver, Kuwabara, asked, “Have you heard the news about the military coup in Korea?”

Until that moment, Lee had known nothing about it.

Although the sudden news threw his thoughts into turmoil, he continued on to the golf course.

The May 16 military coup had broken out in Korea. After the coup, the military government arrested eleven business leaders on suspicion of illicit accumulation of wealth.

Based on corporate sales, eleven people ranked first through eleventh were designated illicit profiteers. Lee headed the list. Because he was in Tokyo, however, he temporarily avoided arrest.

Public opinion criticized the military government for ignoring Lee, “Japan's leading illicit profiteer,” while imprisoning people whose offenses were less serious.

The military government sent two people to Japan to urge Lee to return. After learning about the situation in Seoul, he decided to go home.

At ten in the morning on June 24, 1961, Lee issued a statement at Tokyo's Imperial Hotel to correspondents from the Associated Press, United Press International, and other foreign news agencies: “To help eliminate poverty, I intend to donate all my property to the nation. After returning home, I will carry this out gradually as needed and await the government's arrangements.”

By the time he arrived at Seoul's Gimpo Airport, it was pitch-dark and pouring with rain.

Expecting to be taken straight to prison, Lee climbed into a waiting jeep.

The vehicle sped away, but not toward a prison. It headed in the direction of Shinsegae Department Store, taking him to the Metro Hotel in Myeongdong, where he spent the night.

Deeply concerned for his father, Lee's eldest son, Lee Maeng-hee, feared he might be subjected to brutal interrogation. Throughout the night, from a building opposite the Metro Hotel, he watched his father's every movement through binoculars.

The next day, Lee went to the office of the vice chairman of the Supreme Council for National Reconstruction and met Park Chung Hee. The atmosphere in the room was severe. Deputy Chairman Park[^FB-F017] gave an immediate impression of uncompromising integrity.

Although his own position was precarious, Lee carefully observed whether Park was a leader of virtue and standing.

The meeting brought together the two leading figures of the Republic of Korea's business and political worlds.

“When did you return? Is everything all right?”

Unexpectedly, Park addressed Lee in a very gentle tone.

After a brief exchange of greetings, Park switched to a soldier's direct manner and asked, “How do you think the eleven illicit profiteers should be dealt with?”

Lee himself was one of the eleven—and ranked first among them.

“I do not believe these people are guilty of any crime.”

Lee answered Park honestly according to his own convictions.

Park appeared surprised by the response, and his expression darkened.

Lee continued:

“In my own case, if I evaded taxes, then you may call that illicit enrichment. But the current tax law requires companies to pay taxes equal to 120 percent of sales. If taxes are paid that way, no enterprise can avoid bankruptcy. Any company that does survive would be a miracle.”

Even in the military government's lion's den, standing before its supreme leader, Park Chung Hee, Lee showed no fear and set out his argument.

“Every company strives to increase profits and expand its scale. In other words, these men entered the group of eleven because they ran their businesses well. Yet that success has now become illicit enrichment, while those who lacked ability or opportunity and failed to rank among the eleven are treated as innocent. I do not believe this can serve as a standard of judgment.”

In Lee's view, it was absurd to convict people not for tax evasion or embezzlement, but simply by ranking companies and labeling those in first through eleventh place illicit profiteers. He had immediately identified the weakness in the military government's policy.

Park nodded and asked:

“Then what do you think should be done?”

“An entrepreneur's task is to run an enterprise, provide employment, pay taxes to the nation, and expand investment. It takes at least twenty or thirty years to cultivate a successful entrepreneur. I hope you will make full use of businesspeople and treat them well.”

As an entrepreneur, Lee did not retreat before power. A long silence followed in the tense room.

Park said, “If we do that, the people will not accept it.”

Lee replied, “If it benefits national development, then the people must be persuaded. Is that not what politics means?”

The discussion of how entrepreneurs should be handled ended there. Park asked whether he could meet Lee again and where Lee was staying.

Lee replied that he was under house arrest at the Metro Hotel. Park looked astonished and immediately ordered that all the detained entrepreneurs be released the next day.

Lee, too, left the Metro Hotel and regained his freedom several days later, on the twenty-ninth.

In the spring of 1980, Chung Ju-yung was likewise summoned by Chun Doo-hwan's newly established authoritarian military leadership. The atmosphere surrounding the new military authorities was equally severe.

In the name of restructuring the economy and industry, the new military leadership was imposing a unified plan on private enterprises.

The National Security Emergency Measures Committee summoned Hyundai Chairman Chung Ju-yung, his younger brother Chung Se-yung, an executive at Hyundai Motor, and Hyundai Construction president Lee Myung-bak.

When Chung arrived, he found that Daewoo Group Chairman Kim Woo-choong had also been summoned. Calling the two men together meant that the committee intended to consolidate the automobile and heavy-industrial sectors.

The plan, in other words, was to exchange Kim Woo-choong's Daewoo Motor for Hyundai Group's Changwon[^FB-F018] Heavy Industries. In a capitalist country, companies may buy and sell businesses between themselves, but the state does not order corporate restructuring.

Under the committee's plan, Daewoo Motor would be absorbed into Hyundai Motor, while Hyundai's Changwon Heavy Industries would be incorporated into Daewoo Heavy Industries, supposedly increasing the competitiveness of the entire industry.

After explaining the plan, the committee official asked in a tone that assumed Chung's agreement, “Chairman Chung agrees to this proposal, doesn't he?”

Chung answered in a single sentence: “I do not agree.”

What kind of place was the committee? It could arrest anyone who displeased it. Those who lived through the 1980s all remember the atmosphere of terror it created.

How could anyone say “I do not agree” in such a place?

Only Chung Ju-yung dared to say it.

The committee official's expression changed at once. His face seemed to ask Chung: the nation is in a state of emergency and pursuing reform—how dare you speak so casually?

But Chung Ju-yung remained Chung Ju-yung:

“What I have to say may take some time, but please hear me out. From the day I began my business, I built it piece by piece, starting with the selection of each factory site. I have never sold a company because of an economic downturn, and not one of my companies has gone bankrupt.”

Enraged by Chung's composed attitude, the committee official shouted, “What kind of place do you think this is? What are you saying?”

As a supposed concession, the committee allowed Chung to choose between heavy industry and automobiles.

Its official told Chung to choose first and leave the remaining business to Kim Woo-choong, using the offer of priority to pressure him.

Chung firmly refused and continued to resist.

The committee then adopted a divide-and-conquer strategy, taking Chung Ju-yung, Chung Se-yung, and Lee Myung-bak into separate rooms and threatening them.

After an arduous exchange, Chung asked for two weeks to consider the matter, but the committee gave him only one.[^TN-CH11-002]

Several days later, Chung met committee chairman Chun Doo-hwan somewhere in Ahyeon-dong. He was meeting the supreme ruler.

Faced with Chun's lengthy explanation, Chung finally decided to relinquish Changwon Heavy Industries and choose Daewoo Motor.

But the final result was that Changwon Heavy Industries went to Daewoo, while Daewoo Motor also remained with Daewoo Group.

Chung suffered a crushing defeat.

Before a military government, this was the only possible outcome. In the new military leadership's lion's den, Chung managed to say what he wished, but in the end he was one man against many. When revolution comes, entrepreneurs always lose.

Deeply dissatisfied with Chung's attitude, the new military authorities demanded that he resign as chairman of the Federation of Korean Industries (FKI).[^FB-F019]

Chung replied, “The members elected me chairman, so I cannot resign arbitrarily,” and rejected the demand. In the end, the new military authorities could do nothing with him, and he continued as FKI chairman.

Lee Byung-chull and Chung Ju-yung shared one trait: neither yielded to pressure of any kind, and both stood by their positions.

Lee would set out reason after reason to persuade the other party, while Chung was as stubborn as an ox and refused to give an inch.

[^TN-CH11-001]: The printed Chinese title uses 权利 (“rights”); the translation follows the evident intended sense 权力 (“power”).

[^FB-F017]: In 1961, after the May 16 military coup, the chairman of the Supreme Council for National Reconstruction was Chang Do-yong. Park Chung Hee served as vice chairman but held real power. On July 3, 1961, after deciding that the political situation had stabilized, Park forced Chang to resign and succeeded him as chairman. Park became acting president on March 20, 1962, and president on October 15, 1963. [Translator's note: The Chinese source gives the name as 张都焕. Historical records identify the first chairman as Chang Do-yong (张都暎); the translation uses the verified identity.]

[^FB-F018]: Changwon is located in South Gyeongsang Province, Korea.

[^TN-CH11-002]: The printed source gives the name as 郑州永 here; the translation uses the established Chung Ju-yung.

[^FB-F019]: FKI is the abbreviation for Korea's Federation of Korean Industries.
